# Founder Insights: Deconstructing Modern Business Sense

## 1. "Bet the Company" Risk Evaluation: Morris Chang (TSMC)
**Source:** *Acquired* Podcast (January 2025)
True business sense often involves distinguishing between reckless gambling and calculated, existential risk. Morris Chang faced a defining moment in 2010-2011 when Apple’s Jeff Williams asked TSMC to manufacture iPhone chips.
*   **The Scenario:** Apple required a 20-nanometer process, which was not on TSMC’s product roadmap. TSMC was already heavily invested in its 28nm node.
*   **The Numbers:** The pivot required an unprecedented capital expenditure of roughly **$9 billion**—nearly half of TSMC's cash reserves at the time. 
*   **The Mental Model:** *"I bet the company, but I didn't think I would lose."* Chang recognized the macro-pattern: failing to secure Apple would be a far greater long-term danger to TSMC's pure-play foundry model than the short-term financial hit. 
*   **The Discipline:** Despite taking on massive debt for this pivot, Chang exhibited strict business sense by refusing to eliminate shareholder dividends to fund the project, and he only accepted 50% of the capacity Apple initially requested to ensure TSMC wouldn't catastrophically overextend itself in case of production delays.

## 2. Mastering Unit Economics & Spatial Strategy: Tony Xu (DoorDash)
**Source:** *Acquired* Podcast (December 2020)
While competitors like Uber Eats and Grubhub focused on dense urban centers, Tony Xu demonstrated superior unit-economic intuition by applying a counterintuitive spatial strategy.
*   **The Strategic Pivot:** DoorDash targeted the suburbs. Xu recognized that suburban unit economics were significantly stronger: larger family-sized average order values (increasing revenue per trip), easier parking (lowering driver delivery times/friction), and a higher reliance on delivery due to the lack of walkable restaurant options.
*   **Operational Execution:** Xu treated DoorDash not as a food app, but as a local logistics network. The team maintained a "maniacal focus" on shaving literal seconds off delivery times and eliminating defects. 
*   **The Mindset:** They understood that food delivery was a "winner-take-all" market. They willingly sacrificed short-term margins and accepted high capital dilution to win market share, knowing that operational efficiency at scale was the only path to sustainable unit economics. 

## 3. The Opportunity-Cost Pivot: Tobi Lütke (Shopify)
**Source:** *How I Built This*
Pivots are often romanticized as desperate reactions to running out of money, but the best founders pivot based on opportunity cost and pattern recognition.
*   **The Origin:** In 2004, Tobi Lütke launched Snowdevil, an online snowboard shop. Frustrated by "clunky, expensive, and user-hostile" e-commerce options, he coded his own backend in Ruby on Rails.
*   **The Pivot:** Snowdevil wasn't failing—it was a functional "lifestyle business." However, Lütke recognized a clear pattern: other merchants were constantly asking about the backend software, not the snowboards. 
*   **The Business Sense:** Lütke understood that selling infrastructure is exponentially more scalable than selling physical inventory. He made the strategic decision to pivot away from a functioning business to launch Shopify, turning an internal tool into a platform that solved a universal retail bottleneck.

## 4. "Cockroach" Survival & Doing Things That Don't Scale: Brian Chesky (Airbnb)
**Source:** *How I Built This*
Business sense isn't just about spreadsheets; it's about sheer survival instinct to artificially extend your runway.
*   **The Scenario:** In 2008, Airbnb was deep in debt with maxed-out credit cards and zero revenue.
*   **The Numbers:** Chesky and his co-founders bought 1,000 blank cardboard boxes, manually hot-glued them in their apartment (resulting in "a one-to-one ratio of burn to box"), and created political-themed cereal: "Obama O's" and "Cap'n McCain's." They sold them for **$40 each**, netting roughly **$30,000**.
*   **The Strategic Impact:** This $30,000 kept the company alive during a financial "nuclear winter." More importantly, when they handed a box to Y Combinator's Paul Graham, it proved their "cockroach mentality." Graham funded them not because he loved the Airbnb idea, but because founders willing to do unscalable, gritty work to survive are historically the best bets.

## 5. Mental Models on Risk and Strategy (Charlie Munger & Jensen Huang)
**Source:** *Acquired* Podcast (October 2023 interviews)
*   **The Superpower of Ignorance (Jensen Huang):** Huang noted that building a business is "a million times harder than any of us expected." He believes a founder's greatest asset is a degree of necessary ignorance; if you truly understood the risk and pain upfront, you would never start. True business strategy comes from "subtraction"—ruthlessly prioritizing a few things and sacrificing the rest.
*   **Betting on Your Edge (Charlie Munger):** Munger criticized standard business school teachings on diversification, stating: *"When you know you have an edge, you should bet heavily... Of course you've got to bet heavily on your best bets."* He emphasized that true business sense is recognizing that you will only have five or six "sure things" in a lifetime; the key is having the temperament and conviction to act decisively when they appear.
