# 01 — Alcohol → Alkyl Halide: The Basic Transformation

## 1. Start with the two structures

An alcohol contains a hydroxyl group:

\[
\boxed{R-OH}
\]

An alkyl halide contains a halogen attached to the alkyl group:

\[
\boxed{R-X}
\]

where X = Cl, Br or I.

So the basic conversion is:

\[
\boxed{R-OH \longrightarrow R-X}
\]

In words:

> **The –OH group of an alcohol is replaced by a halogen atom.**

---

## 2. What does “replace –OH by X” actually mean?

Take ethanol:

\[
CH_3CH_2OH
\]

It is an alcohol because it contains –OH.

Replace –OH with Cl:

\[
CH_3CH_2Cl
\]

Now it is chloroethane, an alkyl chloride.

Similarly:

\[
CH_3CH_2OH \rightarrow CH_3CH_2Br
\]

produces bromoethane, and

\[
CH_3CH_2OH \rightarrow CH_3CH_2I
\]

produces iodoethane.

---

## 3. Reagents that can perform this conversion

The conversion can be carried out using several types of reagents:

### Hydrogen halides

\[
HCl,\ HBr,\ HI
\]

General reaction:

\[
ROH + HX \rightarrow RX + H_2O
\]

where X = Cl, Br or I.

### Phosphorus halides

Examples include:

\[
PCl_3,\ PCl_5,\ PBr_3,\ PI_3
\]

These are halogenating reagents: they help replace –OH by a halogen.

### Thionyl chloride

\[
\boxed{SOCl_2}
\]

It is especially useful for preparing alkyl chlorides.

---

## 4. The main idea to remember

Do **not** think of these reactions as random reagent tricks.

The real problem is:

\[
\boxed{\text{How do we make the –OH group leave?}}
\]

Once that problem is solved, the halide ion can become attached to carbon.

That is why the next note is important: **–OH is not a good leaving group.**

---

## Quick check

**Q:** What is the product of converting CH₃CH₂OH by replacing –OH with Br?

**A:**

\[
\boxed{CH_3CH_2Br}
\]

**Q:** What does X represent in R–X?

**A:** A halogen, usually Cl, Br or I in this topic.
