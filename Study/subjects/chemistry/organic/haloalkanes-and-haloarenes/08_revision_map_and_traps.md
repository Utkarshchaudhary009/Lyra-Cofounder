# 08 — One-Page Revision Map + Common Traps

## A. The master reaction

\[
\boxed{ROH \rightarrow RX}
\]

Alcohol → alkyl halide.

\[
X = Cl, Br, I
\]

---

## B. The core problem

The –OH group is a **poor leaving group** because direct loss would give OH⁻.

So we need to activate the –OH group or use a reagent that provides a better pathway.

The key protonation idea is:

\[
ROH + H^+ \rightarrow ROH_2^+
\]

Then water can leave:

\[
ROH_2^+ \rightarrow R^+ + H_2O
\]

Under an SN1-type pathway, the halide attacks:

\[
R^+ + X^- \rightarrow RX
\]

Remember: primary alcohols often use an SN2-type pathway instead of forming a free carbocation.

---

## C. Reagent map

| Conversion | Important reagent / condition | Main idea |
|---|---|---|
| ROH → RCl | HCl | H⁺ activates OH; Cl⁻ supplies Cl |
| ROH → RCl | HCl + ZnCl₂ for 1°/2° alcohols | ZnCl₂ helps activate the alcohol |
| ROH → RCl | SOCl₂ | Preferred because SO₂ and HCl escape as gases |
| ROH → RBr | HBr (48% constant-boiling mixture) | Gives alkyl bromide |
| ROH → RBr | PBr₃ | Phosphorus halide route |
| ROH → RI | NaI/KI + 95% H₃PO₄, heat | Gives good yields of alkyl iodide |
| ROH → RI | PI₃ | Phosphorus halide route |

---

## D. Why SOCl₂ is preferred

\[
ROH + SOCl_2 \rightarrow RCl + SO_2\uparrow + HCl\uparrow
\]

The unwanted products are gases and escape from the reaction mixture.

Therefore the alkyl chloride can be obtained in a relatively pure form.

### Must remember

\[
\boxed{SOCl_2 \text{ → RCl + gaseous SO}_2\text{ + HCl}}
\]

---

## E. Alcohol reactivity order

For a given haloacid in the context discussed:

\[
\boxed{3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ}
\]

The important reason is connected to carbocation stability when an SN1-type pathway is involved:

\[
\boxed{3^\circ\ carbocation > 2^\circ\ carbocation > 1^\circ\ carbocation}
\]

More stable carbocation → easier formation → faster reaction in that pathway.

---

## F. Primary, secondary, tertiary: how to identify them

Look only at the **carbon directly bonded to OH**.

### 1° alcohol

OH-bearing carbon is attached to one other carbon.

\[
R-CH_2OH
\]

### 2° alcohol

OH-bearing carbon is attached to two other carbons.

\[
R_2CHOH
\]

### 3° alcohol

OH-bearing carbon is attached to three other carbons.

\[
R_3COH
\]

---

## G. ZnCl₂ / Lucas reagent

\[
\boxed{Lucas\ reagent = HCl + ZnCl_2}
\]

ZnCl₂ helps activate the alcohol so replacement of –OH by Cl becomes easier, particularly for primary and secondary alcohols in the context of the NCERT statement.

---

## H. HBr and HI facts

### HBr

NCERT notes that **48% HBr**, a constant-boiling mixture, is used for preparing alkyl bromides.

### HI / iodide method

\[
\boxed{ROH + NaI/KI \xrightarrow[\text{heat}]{95\%\ H_3PO_4} RI}
\]

The useful memory pattern is:

**iodide salt + phosphoric acid + heat → alkyl iodide**.

---

## I. “In situ”

**In situ = formed where it is used.**

PBr₃ is generated from red phosphorus + Br₂.

PI₃ is generated from red phosphorus + I₂.

\[
\boxed{red\ P + Br_2 \rightarrow PBr_3}
\]

\[
\boxed{red\ P + I_2 \rightarrow PI_3}
\]

---

## J. Common traps

### Trap 1 — “OH simply leaves.”

Wrong mental model:

\[
ROH \rightarrow R^+ + OH^-
\]

Better idea: activate the –OH group so it can leave as H₂O, or use another pathway.

### Trap 2 — “HCl only provides Cl.”

No. In the conceptual mechanism, H⁺ helps activate the alcohol and Cl⁻ becomes the incoming halide.

### Trap 3 — “All alcohols form carbocations.”

No. Primary alcohols can react through SN2-type substitution without a free carbocation.

### Trap 4 — “ZnCl₂ is the final reagent that becomes part of RCl.”

No. ZnCl₂ is a catalyst/activator here; the chloride in RCl comes from the halide source.

### Trap 5 — “SOCl₂ gives SO.”

For the reaction discussed, the gaseous sulfur product is **SO₂**, together with HCl.

### Trap 6 — “In situ means stored beforehand.”

Exactly the opposite. It means generated **inside the reaction mixture**.

## K. 30-second recall test

Without looking above, answer these:

1. What is the general conversion? **ROH → RX**.
2. Why is OH a poor leaving group? **Direct loss gives OH⁻, a poor leaving group.**
3. What can H⁺ do? **Convert –OH into a form that can leave as H₂O.**
4. Reactivity order? **3° > 2° > 1°.**
5. Why is SOCl₂ preferred? **SO₂ and HCl escape as gases.**
6. What is Lucas reagent? **HCl + ZnCl₂.**
7. How to make R–I with the NCERT method? **NaI/KI + 95% H₃PO₄ + heat.**
8. What does in situ mean? **Generated in the reaction mixture.**
9. PBr₃ from? **Red P + Br₂.**
10. PI₃ from? **Red P + I₂.**

## Final mental model

```text
                 ALCOHOL
                   R–OH
                     │
          ┌──────────┴───────────┐
          │                      │
   Main obstacle            Different reagents
   –OH is poor LG                 │
          │                       ├─ HCl → RCl
          │                       ├─ HBr → RBr
   Activate / change              ├─ NaI/KI + H₃PO₄ → RI
   the leaving group              ├─ SOCl₂ → RCl
          │                       └─ PBr₃ / PI₃ routes
          ↓
     easier substitution
          ↓
       R–X
```

### The one sentence to carry forward

> **Alcohol → haloalkane is fundamentally a substitution problem: the difficult part is replacing the poor leaving group –OH, and different reagents solve that problem in different ways.**
