## 🎯 Stage 1: The Core Idea

### The Two MVPs

If concentration terms were cricket players, Molarity and Molality would be openers. They appear in *every* exam, every year.

**Molarity (M)** — "How many moles of solute per litre of *solution*?"
Think of it as: you have a 1-litre bottle. How many moles fit inside?

**Molality (m)** — "How many moles of solute per kilogram of *solvent*?"
Think of it as: you have 1 kg of pure water. How many moles did you dissolve into it?

### The One Difference That Changes Everything

| | Molarity | Molality |
|--|---------|---------|
| Denominator | Litres of **solution** | kg of **solvent** |
| Temperature? | **Changes** (volume expands/contracts) | **Constant** (mass is fixed) |
| Used when | Lab reactions, titrations | Colligative properties |

> **The single most tested concept in this chapter:** Molarity is T-dependent. Molality is T-independent.

## 🔬 Stage 2: The Formula Lab

### Molarity Formula — Three Versions

```
         n_solute          W_solute (g) × 1000
M = ──────────────── = ──────────────────────────
      V_solution (L)      MM_solute × V_soln (mL)
```

### Molality Formula — Three Versions

```
         n_solute           W_solute (g) × 1000
m = ───────────────── = ──────────────────────────────
     W_solvent (kg)        MM_solute × W_solvent (g)
```

### Units Summary

| Term | Units | Abbreviation |
|------|-------|-------------|
| Molarity | mol/L | M (capital M) |
| Molality | mol/kg | m (lowercase m) |

> **⚠️ Notation Trap:** M = Molarity (mol/L). m = Molality (mol/kg). In handwriting, confusing these costs marks.

### Interrelation Formula (M ↔ m using density)

```
         M × 1000
m = ──────────────────────────
     (d × 1000) - (M × MM_s)

where d = density in g/mL, MM_s = molar mass of solute
```

### Ebullioscopic Constant K_b (Preview — used in Ch. 9)

```
        M_solvent × R × T_b²
K_b = ───────────────────────────
         ΔH_vap × 1000
```

### Type 1: Calculating Molarity — Basic

**Pattern:** Given mass of solute + volume of solution → find M.

#### Solved Example 3.1
**Q:** 9.8 g of H₂SO₄ (MM = 98) is dissolved to make 250 mL of solution. Find M. 🟢

```
n = 9.8/98 = 0.1 mol
V = 250 mL = 0.250 L
M = 0.1/0.250 = 0.4 M
```

### Type 3: Finding Moles or Molar Mass from M or m

**Pattern:** M or m is given → find unknown mass or MM.

#### Solved Example 3.3
**Q:** 500 mL of 2 M H₂SO₄ solution. How many grams of H₂SO₄ are present? 🟡

```
n = M × V = 2 × 0.5 = 1 mol
W = n × MM = 1 × 98 = 98 g
```

#### Solved Example 3.4
**Q:** 1.8 g of a solute dissolved in 90 g of water gives 0.2 m solution. Find MM of solute. 🟡

```
m = n / kg_solvent
0.2 = n / 0.090
n = 0.018 mol

MM = W/n = 1.8/0.018 = 100 g/mol
```

### Type 4: Molarity from w/w% and Density

**Pattern:** Given w/w% and density → find Molarity. This is the most-asked bridge problem.

#### Solved Example 3.5
**Q:** Concentrated H₂SO₄ is 98% w/w, density = 1.84 g/mL, MM = 98 g/mol. Find M. 🟡 ⭐

```
Per 100 g solution:
    W_solute = 98 g
    n_solute = 98/98 = 1 mol
    V_soln = 100/1.84 = 54.35 mL = 0.05435 L

M = 1/0.05435 = 18.4 M

Direct formula:
    M = (10 × w/w% × d) / MM
    M = (10 × 98 × 1.84) / 98 = 18.4 M
```

> **Formula to memorize:** `M = (10 × w% × d) / MM`

**3.4b:** M = (10 × 68 × 1.41) / 63 = 958.8/63 = **15.22 M**

### Type 5: Temperature Dependence — Conceptual

**Pattern:** What happens to M and m when temperature changes?

#### Solved Example 3.6
**Q:** A 1 M glucose solution is prepared at 25°C. Will molarity increase or decrease at 50°C? What about molality? 🟡

```
At 50°C, water expands → V_solution increases.
Same moles of glucose in greater volume.
→ M = n/V decreases.

Mass of water doesn't change.
→ m = n/kg_solvent stays constant.

Answer: M decreases; m is unchanged.
```

#### Practice Questions — Type 5

| # | Question | Difficulty |
|---|----------|------------|
| 3.5a | A 2 m NaCl solution is cooled. Does molality change? | 🟢 |
| 3.5b | Explain why molality is preferred for freezing point depression calculations. | 🟡 |
| 3.5c | At what conditions would M ≈ m? | 🔴 |

<details>
<summary>💡 Solutions for Type 5</summary>

**3.5a:** No. Molality = mol/kg (mass). Cooling doesn't change mass. **Molality is unchanged.**

**3.5b:** Freezing point depression (ΔT_f = K_f × m) occurs at very low temperatures. If we used molarity, the value would shift as the solution's volume changes near the freezing point. Molality (mass-based) stays constant → gives correct results.

**3.5c:** M ≈ m when: (1) density of solution ≈ 1 g/mL (dilute aqueous solution), AND (2) molar mass of solute is much larger than the amount used (so solute mass contributes negligibly). Mathematically: when W_solute << W_solvent, V_soln ≈ V_water ≈ W_water in mL, so M (mol/L) ≈ m (mol/kg).
</details>

| DPP 5.8 | A volume of 500ml of a 0.1M solution of AgNO₃ added to 500ml of 0.1M solution of KCl. The concentration of nitrate ion in the resulting solution is: (A) 0.05M (B) 0.1M (C) 0.2M (D) Reduced to zero | Mixing | 🟡 |

<details>
<summary>💡 Solutions for Type Mixer</summary>

**3.M1:**
- M of conc. HCl = (10 × 37 × 1.19)/36.5 = 12.06 M
- Use M₁V₁ = M₂V₂: 12.06 × V₁ = 0.5 × 500
- V₁ = 250/12.06 = **20.73 mL**

**3.M2:**
- n_NaCl = 2.5/58.5 = 0.04274 mol
- W_soln = 2.5 + 100 = 102.5 g; V_soln = 102.5/1.02 = 100.49 mL = 0.10049 L
- **M = 0.04274/0.10049 = 0.425 M**
- W_solvent = 100 g = 0.1 kg
- **m = 0.04274/0.1 = 0.427 m**

**3.M3:**
- At 25°C: 2 M in 1 L → n = 2 mol; W_soln = 1000 × 1.12 = 1120 g
- At 60°C: same 1120 g solution; V_new = 1120/1.08 = 1037 mL = 1.037 L
- **M_new = 2/1.037 = 1.929 M ≈ 1.93 M**

**DPP 2.8:**
- Initial NaOH = 50 mL × 0.10 M = 5 mmol. Reaction: H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O.
- 5 mmol NaOH consumes 2.5 mmol H₂SO₄. Let V mL of 0.10 M H₂SO₄ be added.
- Added H₂SO₄ = 0.10V mmol. Remaining free H₂SO₄ = 0.10V − 2.5 mmol. Total volume = V + 50 mL.
- M_final = (0.10V − 2.5) / (V + 50) = 0.050 M → 0.10V − 2.5 = 0.050V + 2.5 → 0.050V = 5.0 → V = **100 mL → Answer: (C)**

**DPP 5.8:**
- Reaction: AgNO₃ + KCl → AgCl(s) + KNO₃. Nitrate ion (NO₃⁻) is a spectator ion and does not precipitate.
- Moles of NO₃⁻ = 500 mL × 0.1 M = 50 mmol. Total volume = 500 + 500 = 1000 mL.
- Concentration of NO₃⁻ = 50 mmol / 1000 mL = **0.05 M → Answer: (A)**
</details>

## Key Takeaways from Chapter 3

| Rule | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Molarity denominator | Litres of **solution** |
| Molality denominator | kg of **solvent** |
| M shortcut from w% | `M = (10 × w% × d) / MM` |
| Temperature effect | M changes; m is constant |
| When M ≈ m | Only for very dilute aqueous solutions (d ≈ 1) |

<summary>💡 Detailed Explanations for Stage 7</summary>

**3.S1 → Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation.**
- Molarity of pure liquid = Density (in g/L) / Molar Mass.
- Density of water at $4^\circ\text{C}$ = $1000\text{ g/L}$.
- $M = 1000 / 18 = 55.55\text{ M}$.

**3.S2 → Statement I is True, Statement II is False.**
- Statement I is true: $M = n/V$. If V increases, M decreases.
- Statement II is false: Adding water does NOT change the amount (moles) of solute. Moles remain constant during dilution.

**3.S3 → Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation.**
- You know moles and volume of solution. To get mass of solvent (for molality), you need mass of solution. Mass of solution = Volume $\times$ Density. Then, mass of solvent = mass of solution - mass of solute. Density is indispensable here.

**3.S4 → Statement I is False, Statement II is True.**
- Statement I is false: $1\text{ M}$ $CaCl_2$ gives $2\text{ M}$ of $Cl^-$ ions because each molecule provides two $Cl^-$. So it contains $2\text{ moles}$ of $Cl^-$ per liter.
- Statement II is true: This is the exact reason Statement I is false.

**3.S5 → Answer: (C) A is false and R is false.** (Both False)
- A is false: Even if density = $1.0\text{ g/mL}$, $1\text{ L}$ solution weighs $1000\text{ g}$. But this $1000\text{ g}$ includes BOTH solvent AND solute.
- R is false: The mass of the solvent is $1000\text{ g} - \text{mass of solute}$. It is NOT exactly $1000\text{ g}$ (unless the mass of solute is zero, which means it's not a solution). Therefore, denominator for molality is $< 1\text{ kg}$, making $m > M$.

**3.S6 → Statement I is True, Statement II is False.**
- Statement I is true: This is the standard shortcut formula.
- Statement II is false: In this specific shortcut formula with the "10", density $d$ is in **g/mL** (or g/cm$^3$), not kg/L.

**3.S7 → Answer: (C) A is true but R is false.**
- A is true: Molality depends on mass, which is T-independent.
- R is false: The volume of the solvent *does* change with temperature (it expands). But molality uses the *mass* of the solvent, which is why it doesn't matter.

**3.S8 → Statement I is False, Statement II is True.**
- Statement I is false: $1\text{ m}$ solution means $1\text{ mole}$ in $1\text{ kg}$ of **solvent**. So $1\text{ kg}$ of solvent + mass of $1\text{ mole}$ of $NaCl$ ($58.5\text{ g}$) = $1.0585\text{ kg}$ of **solution**. Therefore, $1\text{ kg}$ of solution contains *less* than 1 mole.
- Statement II is true: Molality is per kg of solvent.

**3.S9 → Answer: (C) A is true but R is false.**
- A is true: If volumes are EQUAL, the arithmetic mean works. $(0.1 \times V + 0.2 \times V) / 2V = 0.15\text{ M}$.
- R is false: It ONLY works if volumes are equal. If you mix $10\text{ mL}$ of $0.1\text{ M}$ with $90\text{ mL}$ of $0.2\text{ M}$, it is not $0.15\text{ M}$. You must use the weighted average $M_{mix} = (M_1V_1 + M_2V_2) / (V_1 + V_2)$.

**3.S10 → Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation.**
- Beakers are wildly inaccurate for volume. Volumetric flasks have a precise calibration mark for exact molarities.

**3.S11 → Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation.**
- If you have $20\% (\text{w/w})$, assume $100\text{ g}$ solution. $20\text{ g}$ solute, $80\text{ g}$ solvent. Moles of solute = $20/MM$. Molality = moles / $0.080\text{ kg}$. You don't need volume or density at all.

**3.S12 → Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation.**
- $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2 \rightarrow 1 \times 100 = M_2 \times 200 \rightarrow M_2 = 0.5\text{ M}$.
- When volume is doubled, concentration is halved. (Assuming additive volumes).

**3.S13 → Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation.**
- $1\text{ m}$ solution: exactly $1000\text{ g}$ water ($55.5\text{ moles}$). Mole fraction = $1 / (1 + 55.5) = 0.0177$.
- $1\text{ M}$ solution: $1000\text{ mL}$ solution. Mass of solution = $1000 \times d$. Water = $1000d - 180$. Since $d \approx 1$ (often slightly >1 for glucose, but let's assume), water is much less than $1000\text{ g}$. Fewer moles of water $\rightarrow$ smaller denominator $\rightarrow$ larger mole fraction.
- (Psychological trap: It takes deep understanding to link M/m directly to mole fraction logic!).

**3.S14 → Statement I is False, Statement II is True.**
- Statement I is false: Molarity of water = $1000 / 18 = 55.5\text{ M}$. Molarity of pure ethanol = $789 / 46 = 17.1\text{ M}$. Water has a higher molarity.
- Statement II is true: Ethanol ($46\text{ g/mol}$) > Water ($18\text{ g/mol}$). Because ethanol is heavier and less dense, far fewer moles fit in a liter.

**3.S15 → Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation.**
- $Ag^+$ and $Cl^-$ precipitate. $Na^+$ and $NO_3^-$ are spectator ions.
- Moles of $NO_3^- = 0.1 \times 0.1 = 0.01\text{ moles}$.
- Total volume = $200\text{ mL} = 0.2\text{ L}$.
- $[NO_3^-] = 0.01 / 0.2 = 0.05\text{ M}$.
