## 🪤 Top 10 Trap Questions — Read Before You Start

These are the ten mistakes that cost students the most marks in this chapter. Read them now. Revisit them after completing the book.

> ⚠️ **Trap 1 — Strict vs Non-Strict Monotonicity**
> "f is increasing" usually means **f'(x) ≥ 0** (non-decreasing); "strictly increasing" means **f'(x) > 0**. NCERT's theorem uses ≥ for increasing. If f'(x) = 0 at isolated points (e.g. x³ at x = 0), the function is STILL strictly increasing. Don't conclude "not monotonic" just because the derivative vanishes somewhere.

> ⚠️ **Trap 2 — Rate of Change: Wrong Variable (t vs x)**
> In related-rates problems, x and y both depend on **time t**. You must differentiate with respect to **t**, not x. Write dV/dt = (dV/dr)(dr/dt) — never drop the dr/dt chain factor.

> ⚠️ **Trap 3 — Endpoint Forgetting in Absolute Max/Min**
> For a global extremum on [a, b], you MUST evaluate f at critical points AND at the endpoints a and b. A local min inside the interval is often NOT the absolute minimum — an endpoint can dominate. This trap alone costs 2–3 marks every year.

> ⚠️ **Trap 4 — Reporting the x-value as the Extremum**
> A critical point c where f'(c) = 0 is NOT the maximum/minimum **value** — it's the **location**. The extremum value is f(c). Examiners deduct marks when you write "maximum = 2" when 2 is the x-coordinate.

> ⚠️ **Trap 5 — Second Derivative Test "Fails"**
> If f'(c) = 0 AND f''(c) = 0, the second derivative test is **inconclusive** — NOT "no extremum." You must fall back to the First Derivative Test (sign change of f'). E.g. x⁴ at x = 0: second test fails, but first test shows a minimum.

> ⚠️ **Trap 6 — Normal Is Vertical When Tangent Is Horizontal (and vice versa)**
> If the tangent is horizontal (m_T = 0), the normal is the **vertical line x = x₁**, not "y = 0." If the tangent is vertical (m_T undefined), the normal is horizontal: y = y₁. The "−1/m" formula blows up here — handle separately.

> ⚠️ **Trap 7 — Approximations: Sign and Base Choice**
> For (3.968)^(3/2), take x = 4 and Δx = −0.032 (NOT +0.032). Then f(x+Δx) ≈ f(x) + f'(x)Δx, and you must **add** f'(x)Δx to f(x). Picking x far from x+Δx makes the linear approximation invalid.

> ⚠️ **Trap 8 — "Angle with the Plane of the Curve" Mix-up (Tangents)**
> The slope m_T = dy/dx is the tangent's inclination to the **x-axis**. "Equally inclined to the axes" means m_T = ±1; "equal intercepts on the axes" means m_T = −1. These are NOT the same — students conflate them.

> ⚠️ **Trap 9 — Domain Restrictions Disappear**
> Monotonicity and extrema must respect the **domain**. For f(x) = sin 3x − cos 3x you must restrict to the given interval (e.g. 0 < x < π) before claiming where it rises/falls. For x^x you need x > 0. Ignoring the domain is a silent mark-killer.

> ⚠️ **Trap 10 — Local vs Absolute Confusion**
> A local maximum is the tallest point in its *neighbourhood*; the absolute maximum is the tallest point on the *whole interval*. A function can have several local maxima but only one absolute maximum. Mixing the two up wrecks optimization answers.

## 🎓 A Note Before You Begin

The derivative was born from a simple human question: *how fast is something changing right now?* Newton asked it about planets; Leibniz asked it about curves. Chapter 6 is where the derivative stops being a mechanical rule and becomes a **tool for understanding the world** — how a balloon's area grows as you blow, where a rocket must be aimed to just graze a planet's surface, how a factory finds the cheapest can that holds exactly 500 cm³ of cola.

Every idea here chains back to one rule you already know: **dy/dx is the slope of the tangent.** From that single fact we get rates of change, monotonicity, tangents and normals, approximations, and optimization. One rule. Endless consequences.

As you work through these seven chapters, you will find them connected. Rate-of-change problems feed optimization. Tangents feed normals and angles-between-curves. Approximations are just the tangent line used for estimation. Maxima and minima sit on top of everything.

Understand it deeply, and this becomes one of your strongest weapons in the exam.

**Let's begin. 🚀**
