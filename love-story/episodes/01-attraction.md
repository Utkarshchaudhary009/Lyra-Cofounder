# Episode 1 — Attraction

The first lie about love is usually a pleasant one.

It begins with something so small that no one thinks to question it.

A person makes an ordinary afternoon less ordinary.

That is enough.

---

At 4:17 p.m., the auditorium lights failed for the third time.

Arjun Mehra was standing on a plastic chair with one hand inside the open metal casing of a switchboard and the other holding a phone between his shoulder and ear.

"No, Kabir," he said, "you cannot solve an electrical problem by switching the main on and off and hoping the universe gets embarrassed."

Kabir's voice crackled through the phone.

"It worked yesterday."

"Yesterday the microphone worked because it was plugged in."

"That feels like an accusation."

"It is an accusation."

Arjun pulled his hand back. A tiny spark snapped near his thumb. He climbed down.

On the stage, a group of first-year volunteers stood around a half-painted banner for the university's annual festival, Aakaar. Someone had written TECH + LITERATURE in enormous blue letters and forgotten the second E in LITERATURE.

Arjun stared at it.

"We're finished," he said.

A girl sitting cross-legged on the edge of the stage looked up from a stack of printed schedules.

"No," she said. "We're missing a vowel."

Arjun looked at her for the first time.

She held the schedule pages against one knee. Her hair was tied badly enough that several strands had escaped. She had a pen tucked behind one ear and another pen in her hand. There was a blue ink mark on the side of her wrist.

"You're the literary team?" he asked.

"I'm the person who has been fixing the literary team's mistakes for three hours."

"So yes."

"So no."

He smiled despite himself.

She looked at the banner again.

"Can you fix the lights?"

"Probably."

"Can you fix that?"

She pointed at LITERATRE.

Arjun looked at the paint.

"Definitely not."

"Then you're less useful than you looked."

She went back to her papers.

He should have walked away.

Instead he said, "What's your name?"

She looked up again.

"Why?"

It was not flirtation exactly. It was simply a question asked with enough suspicion to make him aware that he had just asked one.

"For administrative reasons."

"You're not administration."

"I could become administration."

"That's frightening."

He laughed.

"Mira."

"Mira what?"

She stared at him.

"You want my surname for administrative reasons too?"

"Mira is enough."

"Good."

She returned to the schedule.

Arjun walked backstage to find the circuit breaker.

He kept smiling for no good reason.

---

For the next two weeks they met in fragments.

Never deliberately at first.

At a cafeteria table where she had taken the last chair.

In a corridor outside Seminar Hall 3.

At 11:43 p.m. in the festival coordination group chat.

Outside the library when both had forgotten umbrellas despite the rain.

The university was the kind of place that encouraged accidental intimacy. There were too many people and not enough spaces, so proximity disguised itself as fate.

Arjun began noticing details.

Mira hated overly sweet tea.

She read messages and forgot to reply, then answered three hours later as though the delay were invisible.

She laughed before finishing her own jokes.

When concentrating, she tapped a pen against whatever surface was nearest.

She argued with people as though disagreement were a form of affection.

One evening, while they were sorting festival passes, she asked him what he wanted to do after college.

"Make something," he said.

"That's vague."

"That's the point."

"No. That's what people say when they haven't decided."

"I want to build products. Maybe a company."

"Why?"

"Because I like making things."

"That's not a reason to make a company."

"It is for me."

"Then maybe you want to make products, not a company."

He looked at her.

"You do this with everything?"

"Do what?"

"Pull apart the sentence until it stops sounding impressive."

She smiled.

"Usually."

He liked that.

That was the first moment he used the word attraction, though only privately.

Not because of her appearance, although he liked her face.

Not because of her voice, though he liked how she stretched the final word of a sentence when she was amused.

He liked the feeling of being around her.

It was easier to think.

And because humans are very efficient storytellers, his mind immediately converted that sensation into meaning.

Maybe this was different.

---

On a Saturday afternoon, Mira found the blue notebook.

It belonged to Dr. Devika Rao, who had left it in the seminar room after the student committee meeting.

The cover was plain.

Inside were notes, dates, fragments of sentences, questions.

On one page, written alone in the center, was:

**A relationship is not a feeling. A relationship is what two people do with their feelings.**

Mira read it twice.

Arjun came in carrying a box of extension cables.

"That's yours?" she asked.

"What?"

She held up the notebook.

"No. That's Professor Rao's."

"Read this."

He took the book.

He read the sentence.

"That's depressing."

"Why?"

"Because it makes everything sound like project management."

Mira laughed.

"Maybe love is project management."

"No chance."

"Why?"

He put the notebook down.

"Because if you're thinking about what you're doing with your feelings all the time, you kill the feeling."

She tilted her head.

"So what is love?"

The question arrived casually.

But Arjun's mind treated it like an exam.

"Love is..."

He paused.

Mira waited.

He looked toward the auditorium doors, where sunlight fell through the glass in a long pale rectangle.

"Love is when someone improves an ordinary day just by being there."

Mira smiled.

"That's annoyingly good."

"Thank you."

"I didn't say it was true."

"You don't think it's true?"

"I think it's beautiful. Those aren't the same thing."

That sentence stayed with him longer than he expected.

---

Kabir noticed before Arjun admitted anything.

They were walking back from class when Kabir said, "You've become weird."

"I was always weird."

"Different weird."

"What does that mean?"

"You check your phone and don't complain when there's no notification."

Arjun laughed.

"That's your evidence?"

"You smile at your phone."

"People smile at phones."

"You used to call that pathetic."

"People evolve."

Kabir stopped walking.

"Who is she?"

Arjun kept walking.

"There's no she."

Kabir caught up.

"There is absolutely a she."

"Mira."

Kabir whistled.

"Literature girl?"

"She has a name."

"You know her name. That's already severe."

"Shut up."

"Do you like her?"

Arjun considered the question.

He could say yes.

Instead he said, "I like being around her."

Kabir shrugged.

"That's how it starts."

Arjun looked at him.

"How what starts?"

Kabir grinned.

"The thing people call love because 'I enjoy your company and find you attractive and my brain gives me a pleasant chemical reward when you appear' is less poetic."

Arjun laughed.

"You're an idiot."

"Probably."

They continued walking.

Neither noticed that Arjun had stopped listening to the conversation.

He was thinking about the sentence from the notebook.

A relationship is what two people do with their feelings.

It seemed unnecessary.

If the feeling was right, the rest would probably follow.

That was the assumption.

---

The festival arrived on a Friday.

The campus transformed itself.

Fairy lights hung between trees. Food stalls filled the quadrangle. The auditorium smelled of dust, paint, and overheated wiring. Students wandered from debate rooms to gaming stations to poetry readings, all pretending they had nowhere else to be.

Arjun had been working since seven in the morning.

At noon, Mira found him behind the stage.

"You're bleeding."

He looked at his hand.

A thin scratch crossed one knuckle.

"That's nothing."

"Everything is 'nothing' with you."

She took a small first-aid packet from her bag.

"Give me your hand."

He hesitated for half a second.

Then he did.

She cleaned the scratch.

There was no dramatic music, no slow motion, no revelation.

Just two people standing behind a stage while a crowd outside applauded someone whose name neither of them knew.

"You should be more careful," she said.

"You're worried about me?"

She looked up.

"Don't make it weird."

"I'm not."

"You are."

He smiled.

She finished wrapping the small bandage.

"There."

"Thanks."

She started to walk away.

"Mira."

She turned.

"Yeah?"

He almost said something.

Anything would have changed the shape of the afternoon.

Instead he said, "The projector is working."

She rolled her eyes.

"Romantic."

Then she left.

Arjun stood still for a moment.

The word had been a joke.

His mind kept it anyway.

---

That evening, Dr. Rao gathered the student coordinators for a final check.

She placed the blue notebook on the table.

"This is going around more than the official event schedule," she said.

"Because your handwriting is dramatic," Mira replied.

"My handwriting is honest."

Dr. Rao opened the notebook.

"I wrote a question in here years ago. I want each of you to answer it before you leave."

She turned the page.

**What do you call love when you remove the story?**

There were groans.

"This is why she teaches literature," someone whispered.

"Answer seriously," Dr. Rao said.

One student wrote: **care**.

Another: **trust**.

Naina wrote: **sacrifice**.

Sameer wrote: **freedom**.

Kabir wrote: **chemistry** and drew a laughing face.

Mira stared at the page for a long time.

Then she wrote:

**feeling**.

Arjun looked at the word.

"That's not enough," he said.

Mira looked at him.

"What would you write?"

He took the pen.

For a second he considered Professor Rao's earlier line.

Then he wrote:

**the person who makes an ordinary day feel less ordinary.**

Mira read it.

She did not laugh.

"You really like that definition," she said.

"Maybe."

"You're going to regret definitions."

"Why?"

"Because reality doesn't sign contracts with them."

He stared at her.

"You sound like Professor Rao."

"That's insulting."

She smiled.

And the room moved on.

---

Three weeks later, they were sitting on the steps outside the library.

The festival was over.

The campus had returned to its normal rhythm.

No emergency meetings.

No late-night coordination messages.

No reason, technically, for Arjun to be sitting there with Mira.

Yet neither had suggested leaving.

"Do you ever think we only like people because they become part of our routine?" Mira asked.

Arjun looked at her.

"That's dark."

"It's a question."

"Maybe."

"Then how do you know if you actually like someone?"

He thought.

"When they stop being convenient and you still want them around."

She looked impressed.

"That's better."

"You don't have to sound surprised."

"I am surprised."

They laughed.

The sun had started going down.

Students moved past them carrying books, backpacks, helmets.

A bus honked somewhere outside the university gates.

Mira looked at the road.

"My friend Ishaan is back on campus."

Arjun's smile faded slightly.

"The same Ishaan?"

"There is only one Ishaan in my life currently."

"Right."

"We used to talk a lot."

"Used to?"

"It's complicated."

He knew that phrase.

Everyone used it when the truth had too many moving parts.

"Do you still like him?" Arjun asked.

Mira looked at him.

"You ask direct questions."

"You don't have to answer."

She looked back at the road.

"I don't know."

He nodded.

It was a small answer.

It should have been allowed to remain small.

But his mind immediately started building a story around it.

Maybe he had arrived at the right time.

Maybe she was choosing between them.

Maybe what they had was already something.

Maybe feeling this strongly meant it was real.

Maybe.

Maybe.

Maybe.

There is no greater factory of fiction than a person who wants certainty.

---

That night, Arjun opened a blank document on his laptop.

He wrote a title.

**Things I know about Mira.**

Then he deleted it.

He opened his notes app.

Deleted that too.

Finally he took a sheet of paper and wrote one sentence:

**I like her.**

He stared at it.

The sentence felt both embarrassingly simple and strangely enormous.

He did not know what would happen.

He only knew that an ordinary day now contained the possibility of becoming something else.

Outside his window, traffic moved through the city in long bands of light.

Inside, he turned the page.

At the top he wrote:

**LOVE**

And beneath it:

**When one person's presence improves the quality of an ordinary day.**

He underlined it once.

He did not know that months later he would look at that sentence again and barely recognize the person who had written it.

For now, it was enough.

For now, the story was beautiful.

And beauty is often where the first assumption hides.
