# Episode 1 — Attraction, continued

The week after Arjun wrote his definition, nothing happened that would have looked important from the outside.

That was the problem with beginnings.

They were built from events too small to recognize as evidence.

Mira missed a bus.

Arjun waited with her.

They shared a packet of chips.

She stole the last piece.

He complained.

She laughed.

A month later he would remember the exact sound of that laugh and mistake memory for proof that the moment had always carried destiny inside it.

---

One afternoon Mira asked him to come to a poetry reading.

"I'm not a poetry person."

"You don't have to be."

"Then why invite me?"

"Because I want to know what you look like when you don't understand something."

He laughed.

"That's cruel."

"Curious."

They went.

The room was small and overheated.

A student read a poem about distance.

Another read about memory.

A third read about someone leaving before being asked to stay.

Arjun watched the audience more than the stage.

Some people closed their eyes.

Some smiled.

Some looked bored.

Mira listened completely.

Afterward she asked, "What did you feel?"

"Mostly that the microphone was too loud."

She laughed.

"You're impossible."

"I don't know how to fake a reaction."

"Good."

He looked at her.

"You really mean that?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

She shrugged.

"Because I want to know what you actually think, not what you think you should think."

The sentence stayed with him.

He did not know that a similar principle would later become central to the relationship:

Do not manufacture the emotion you think a relationship requires.

---

At the end of the month, Arjun's product project failed its first demonstration.

He was furious.

He blamed the hardware.

Then the code.

Then the team.

Mira found him outside the engineering block.

"What happened?"

"Everything broke."

"And?"

"And it's a disaster."

She sat beside him.

"Do you want me to tell you it's fine?"

"No."

"Good."

They sat quietly.

After five minutes she said, "You know what's strange?"

"What?"

"You only tell people you care when they are useful to the outcome."

He looked at her.

"That's not true."

"Maybe."

He realized she was asking something deeper.

"What do you mean?"

"If someone makes your day better, it's easy to like them. What happens when they make your day harder?"

He had no answer.

It was another crack in his definition.

He wanted to believe love was a source of good feelings.

But the most valuable people in life were not always the people who made every day easier.

Sometimes they made you confront yourself.

He did not write that down.

Not yet.

---

A few days later, Mira was sick and missed class.

Arjun sent her notes.

She thanked him.

He asked whether she needed anything.

She said no.

He almost went to her hostel anyway.

Then remembered that care without permission could become intrusion.

He stayed.

The next day she told him,

"I'm glad you didn't show up."

He laughed.

"That's not the reaction I expected."

"Because if you had, I would have felt like I owed you something."

The sentence startled him.

He had always assumed helpfulness created closeness.

He had not considered that it could also create debt.

"So how should people care?" he asked.

Mira smiled.

"Ask."

Again, simple.

Again, difficult.

---

The semester grew busier.

Their friendship became routine.

That routine was the first thing that made Arjun believe there was something permanent.

They sat together.

They shared jokes.

They knew each other's schedules.

His phone often showed her name.

One evening he realized he had begun measuring his day around whether he would see her.

It felt wonderful.

It also felt dangerous.

He wondered whether a relationship added meaning to a life or whether a mind could begin outsourcing meaning to a person.

The distinction was still theoretical.

He had no idea how much theory life was about to demand.

---

At the end of the episode's first month, Mira asked him a final question before going home.

"If we stopped talking tomorrow, would you still be okay?"

He smiled.

"No."

She laughed.

"That's honest."

"Would you?"

She thought.

"I'd be sad. But yes."

He noticed the difference.

She could care about him without requiring continued access.

He was not there yet.

But he wanted to be.

That night, alone in his room, he opened the sheet where he had written his definition.

**Love is when one person's presence improves the quality of an ordinary day.**

He added:

**But maybe the real test begins when their absence does not destroy the day.**

He did not know whether the sentence was wise.

He only knew it frightened him.

And that was often the beginning of useful knowledge.
