# Episode 5 — Collision, continued

The morning after the dinner, the group chat was silent.

Not even Kabir posted a meme.

Silence in a group chat is rarely neutral. Everyone reads it differently.

Rhea thought it meant anger.

Naina thought it meant shame.

Mira thought it meant people were finally considering their actions.

Arjun knew it mostly meant nobody wanted to be the first to speak.

At eleven, he wrote one sentence.

**I think we should talk about what happened, not just about who caused it.**

The message remained on the screen.

Three minutes.

Five.

Then Sameer replied:

**Good. Start with what you know, not what you assume.**

That sentence changed the meeting.

---

They gathered in the seminar room that afternoon.

No professor.

No audience.

No performance required.

Sameer put six sheets of paper on the table.

"One page each," he said. "Facts first. Interpretation second."

Kabir groaned.

"You turned our emotional disaster into a worksheet."

"It already was one," Sameer said. "You just didn't label it."

Mira almost laughed.

They began.

**Fact:** Arjun sent the message.

**Fact:** Kabir saw it.

**Fact:** Kabir told Rhea.

**Fact:** Rhea told Naina.

**Fact:** Naina brought everyone together without asking.

Then came interpretation.

**Interpretation:** Kabir thought Arjun needed validation.

**Interpretation:** Rhea thought Naina could repair the group.

**Interpretation:** Naina thought forced proximity would create honesty.

**Interpretation:** Arjun thought Mira secretly wanted a relationship.

They looked at the list.

The facts were short.

The interpretations were enormous.

"That's disturbing," Mira said.

"Why?" Kabir asked.

"Because we made most of the story ourselves."

---

Arjun looked at Mira.

"I did."

She waited.

"I decided what you felt before you had decided it yourself."

She nodded.

"And I decided what you would eventually choose."

"Yes."

"That's unfair."

"Yes."

He expected anger.

Instead she looked tired.

"Do you know what was hardest?" she asked.

"What?"

"Sometimes your theory was right."

He frowned.

"What?"

"Sometimes I did want you. Sometimes I was jealous. Sometimes I thought about choosing you. So when you guessed correctly, it made you trust your guesses more."

Arjun went quiet.

That was more important than being told he was wrong.

A person can be right for the wrong reason.

If the conclusion happens to match reality, the method can become harder to abandon.

"How do I know when I'm interpreting instead of understanding?" he asked.

Mira answered, "You probably don't. That's why you ask the person."

He laughed once.

"Annoyingly simple."

"Reality is like that."

---

Naina spoke next.

"I wanted everyone to reconcile because I was scared the group would disappear."

Rhea looked at her.

"You could have said that."

Naina shrugged.

"I thought helping would be less selfish than admitting I was afraid."

Sameer nodded.

"That's important."

Naina looked irritated.

"Why?"

"Because sometimes people convert fear into morality. Instead of saying 'I don't want to lose this,' they say 'I'm doing what's right for everyone.'"

Naina looked at the floor.

She knew exactly what he meant.

---

The meeting lasted three hours.

No one repaired everything.

But they discovered something more useful.

Everyone had been using a different definition of responsibility.

Arjun thought responsibility meant fixing outcomes.

Naina thought it meant preventing pain.

Rhea thought it meant respecting a person's explicit boundary.

Mira thought it meant honesty about internal uncertainty.

Sameer thought it meant owning the effects of your choices without claiming control over someone else's.

They argued about which definition was best.

Sameer stopped them.

"Maybe you're asking the wrong question."

"What question should we ask?" Kabir said.

"What kind of responsibility belongs to what kind of power?"

Everyone became quiet.

"If you control the event, you're responsible for the event. If you control only your response, you're responsible only for that. Confusing the two creates either guilt or control."

Mira wrote the sentence in the notebook.

---

That evening, Arjun walked home alone.

The sky was violet above the rooftops.

He replayed the conversation.

He realized something uncomfortable.

For months he had wanted to become the person Mira would choose.

He had talked about self-improvement as though it were a strategy for becoming lovable.

Work harder.

Be calmer.

Be more interesting.

Listen better.

Never become predictable.

Every improvement contained a hidden negotiation:

**If I become this person, will she choose me?**

That was not growth.

It was optimization against another person's decision.

He stopped walking.

He laughed quietly.

"That's ridiculous."

A street vendor looked at him.

Arjun waved awkwardly.

Then he continued home.

---

Mira had her own realization.

She had spent months wanting Arjun close while resisting the responsibility implied by closeness.

She wanted the emotional benefits of being chosen without being responsible for what that choice required.

It was not malicious.

That was precisely why it was dangerous.

A person can create harm without intending harm.

She wrote in the blue notebook:

**Good intentions explain an action. They do not erase its consequences.**

Then, after a pause:

**But bad consequences do not automatically prove bad intentions.**

She underlined both.

---

Kabir and Rhea met the next afternoon.

They had spent a week apart.

Kabir looked different, though Rhea could not say why.

"I thought about what you said," he told her.

"Which part?"

"That I only become certain when I'm about to lose you."

She waited.

"I realized I keep using crisis to feel alive."

Rhea nodded.

"I like the moment when everything is threatened because then I don't have to wonder whether I care. The danger answers the question for me."

She sat beside him.

"And now?"

"Now I think I need to learn how to value something when nothing is breaking."

Rhea smiled.

"That's much harder."

"I know."

They did not immediately become a couple again.

They agreed to start by having ordinary days.

It sounded almost boring.

That was exactly the point.

---

One week later, the old group gathered for tea.

No one made a speech.

No one announced healing.

They simply sat together.

Mira handed Arjun a cup.

"No sugar."

He looked surprised.

"You remembered."

"I remember many things."

He smiled.

The cup was warm.

The moment was ordinary.

It did not need to be evidence of destiny.

It could simply be kindness.

That distinction felt almost revolutionary.

---

Before leaving, Arjun opened the blue notebook.

He turned to the page that had once asked what love meant.

Under his old definition, he wrote:

**A person can make an ordinary day less ordinary. That is beautiful. It is not yet a contract.**

Mira read it.

Then she added:

**And a contract is meaningful only when both people know it exists.**

Kabir leaned over.

"Can we please stop turning this notebook into the world's saddest constitution?"

Everyone laughed.

The laughter was different now.

Not lighter.

More aware.

The group had not become wiser in a single afternoon.

They had become more suspicious of their own certainty.

That was probably the more valuable beginning.

Because the next failure would not come from not caring.

It would come from caring very much and using that care as permission to choose for someone else.
