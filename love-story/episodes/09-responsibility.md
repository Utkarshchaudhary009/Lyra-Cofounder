# Episode 9 — Responsibility

The final illusion about love is that understanding a person automatically tells you what to do with them.

It does not.

You can understand someone's fear and still know you cannot build a life around it.

You can understand someone's ambition and still be hurt by its consequences.

You can love someone and still be incompatible with them.

By the end of summer, Arjun and Mira had enough knowledge to make their next decision much harder.

---

Mira received an offer for a graduate program in Delhi.

Arjun received a full-time role in Bengaluru.

Neither city was the same.

Neither career was temporary.

Neither person wanted to give up what they had worked for.

For the first time, the central problem was not emotional uncertainty.

It was structural incompatibility.

The old version of the story would have treated love as a force capable of overcoming everything.

The newer version required a less romantic question:

**What kind of life can these two people realistically build?**

---

They met in the old café.

Mira had the acceptance letter open on her laptop.

Arjun had his offer letter printed.

Neither had brought the blue notebook.

"So," Mira said.

"So."

"This is ridiculous."

"Very."

They laughed.

Then she said, "I want you in my life."

"I want you in mine."

"But I don't know if I want the same geography."

He smiled.

"Geography is not a small thing."

"Exactly."

Silence.

"Would you move?" she asked.

He thought.

"I could. But then I'd be giving up a role I've wanted for years."

"I could move too. But then I'd be giving up the program I worked for."

The old romantic instinct was obvious:

One sacrifices.

The sacrifice proves love.

The person who sacrifices more loves more.

But Naina's lesson returned.

Suffering was not automatically proof of depth.

A sacrifice made under emotional pressure could become resentment.

"Maybe neither of us should sacrifice immediately," Arjun said.

Mira nodded.

"Maybe we need to admit that love doesn't solve logistics."

"And logistics are not unromantic. They're reality."

---

They spent the next month researching options.

Not because they were negotiating a wedding.

Because they were testing whether their relationship could survive reality instead of fantasy.

Could one work remotely?

Could the other transfer?

How often could they visit?

What would distance do to communication?

What did each person actually want?

What was negotiable?

What was not?

They discovered an uncomfortable fact:

When the relationship was treated as a shared problem, they worked well together.

When one person treated the relationship as the other's sacrifice, resentment appeared quickly.

---

Kabir and Rhea faced the same question in a different form.

Rhea had been offered an excellent program in another city.

Kabir's small business was finally becoming viable in Lucknow.

Neither wanted to ask the other to abandon their path.

Kabir initially thought the answer had to be sacrifice.

Rhea challenged him.

"Why do we need one person to lose for the relationship to win?"

He thought.

"Maybe we don't."

"Then stop turning love into a scoreboard."

He laughed.

"I was raised on films."

"Then update your operating system."

They explored long distance.

Neither promised permanence.

They promised an honest trial.

The word trial sounded unromantic.

It was more honest than forever.

---

Naina was asked by a student she mentored whether she should give up an opportunity for her partner.

Naina paused.

The old answer would have been immediate.

The new one was a question.

"Do you want to give it up?"

"I don't know. I don't want to lose him."

Naina nodded.

"That's different."

The student looked confused.

"How?"

"Wanting to stay with someone is one reason. Fear of losing them is another. They can point to the same action but lead to very different futures."

She asked one more question:

"If he were guaranteed not to leave you, what would you choose?"

The student thought.

"The opportunity."

Naina smiled.

"Then your answer may already be there."

She walked away afterward and realized she was finally using what she had learned without using it to control someone.

---

Arjun and Mira's own decision became clearer.

They could attempt a relationship across cities.

They could also choose not to.

Neither option was proof of love or lack of love.

That was the strange conclusion.

The quality of love could not be measured solely by whether the relationship continued.

A relationship had to be judged by what the people inside it repeatedly did.

---

One night Mira asked Arjun, "What do you want? Not what should we do. What do you want?"

He answered honestly.

"I want to build my company. I want to stay close to my work. I want you in my life. I don't want either of us pretending distance is nothing."

She nodded.

"I want my program. I want Delhi. I want you in my life. And I don't want you to become responsible for my happiness."

They looked at each other.

"That's annoyingly compatible," Mira said.

He laughed.

"Which means the problem is still logistics."

"Exactly."

---

They decided to try long distance for six months.

Not forever.

Not because love demanded it.

Because both wanted to test whether their actual behavior could sustain what their feelings wanted.

They wrote down rules together.

No disappearing during conflict.

No using jealousy as a test.

No keeping score of who traveled more.

No demanding immediate replies as proof of care.

No threatening breakup to obtain reassurance.

And one strange rule:

**If either person genuinely concludes the relationship is no longer good for them, they must say it rather than extending the experiment out of guilt.**

Mira signed the page.

Arjun signed it too.

"This is very unromantic," she said.

"It is also the first thing we've done that feels like an actual relationship."

She smiled.

---

The first month was easy.

The second was difficult.

The third was confusing.

The fourth exposed the real problem.

Arjun was working seventy-hour weeks.

Mira's academic workload had doubled.

Visits were canceled.

Calls became shorter.

Both were exhausted.

The old patterns returned quietly.

Mira began feeling unimportant.

Arjun began feeling monitored.

One night she said, "I feel like I am always waiting for the leftover version of you."

He reacted defensively.

"And I feel like no amount of time is enough for you."

Silence.

They could have repeated the old story.

You don't care.

You don't understand.

Someone else would treat me better.

Instead Arjun asked, "What are you actually asking me for?"

Mira thought.

"Consistency. Not more hours."

He said, "That's doable."

Then he asked, "And what are you asking yourself to accept?"

She hesitated.

"That your work matters to you even when it doesn't include me."

They both laughed sadly.

The relationship survived that conversation because neither tried to make the other responsible for eliminating uncertainty completely.

---

Then the impossible thing happened.

Mira was offered a position in Bengaluru after graduation.

Arjun's company asked him to remain there.

Suddenly the structural problem disappeared.

The cities matched.

The future could fit.

The old story would call this destiny.

Mira did not.

She asked a harder question:

"Do we want each other now, or do we want the solution we just received?"

Arjun looked at her.

The question frightened him.

Because he realized that for months he had been fighting for compatibility to exist.

Now compatibility existed.

That did not automatically tell them whether they wanted the relationship.

The problem had changed again.

---

They met at the end of the semester.

No documents.

No notebook.

Just the two of them.

"I love you," Mira said.

Arjun looked at her.

"I love you too."

Then she asked:

"Do we choose each other?"

He took a long breath.

"I think we should choose whether we can actually build a good life together, not merely whether we can avoid losing each other."

She smiled.

"That's the answer I wanted."

"What is the answer?"

"I don't know yet."

He laughed.

"Of course."

They were back at uncertainty.

But this time uncertainty was no longer the absence of knowledge.

It was an honest recognition of what could not yet be known.
