# Psychological Models

The story treats each definition of love as a provisional model rather than a caricature.

| Model | What it gets right | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Pleasure | Relationships should contain joy and ease | Pleasure cannot decide what is right when desire conflicts with reality |
| Attraction | Desire reveals genuine preference | Attraction says little about long-term compatibility |
| Validation | Being seen and valued matters | External validation can become dependency |
| Possession | Commitment can feel safer when exclusivity is clear | Care can mutate into control |
| Freedom | A person remains an autonomous individual | Freedom without responsibility can become avoidance |
| Sacrifice | Love sometimes requires cost | Sacrifice can become manipulation, martyrdom, or identity |
| Compatibility | Shared values and life direction matter | A checklist cannot capture a living relationship |
| Commitment | Durable relationships require deliberate action | Commitment does not make incompatibility disappear |
| Responsibility | Choices create obligations and consequences | Responsibility must not become ownership of another adult's life |

## Progression principle

The story should never replace one simplistic model with another. Each season preserves what was true in the previous model while exposing what it could not explain.

The reader should gradually arrive at a composite understanding:

**feeling matters → desire matters → freedom matters → choice matters → consequences matter → responsibility matters → but no single component is sufficient by itself.**

The final position is not a formula for perfect love. It is a better method for reasoning about relationships under uncertainty.
