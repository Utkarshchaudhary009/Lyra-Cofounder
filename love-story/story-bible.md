# Story Bible — The Things We Call Love

## Logline
At a university in Lucknow, a young man who treats love as a pleasurable feeling becomes entangled with a girl who treats it as an unpredictable emotion, while their friends test competing ideas of attachment, freedom, loyalty, and responsibility. Across one academic year, each relationship appears to teach a new lesson until they discover that changing partners is not the same as changing the self.

## Central question
When feelings change, what remains that can honestly be called a relationship?

## Hidden engine
Every romantic conflict is also a test of a model:
- Attraction: "I feel strongly, therefore this must be meaningful."
- Pleasure: "If this makes us happy, it must be good."
- Possession: "If I am jealous of losing you, I must love you."
- Validation: "I need to be chosen to know that I matter."
- Sacrifice: "If I suffer for you, my love is proven."
- Freedom: "If I never ask anything of you, I cannot control you."
- Choice: "A relationship is repeatedly chosen, not merely felt."
- Responsibility: "Choice has consequences for another person's life, so care cannot stop at desire."

## Structural rule
The story never claims that one formula explains all love. Instead, it distinguishes categories that characters keep collapsing into one word. The final movement is from involuntary feeling to deliberate action without pretending feeling is irrelevant.

## Setting
Contemporary Lucknow and a nearby university campus. The world is ordinary: classrooms, student clubs, libraries, cafés, rented flats, festivals, buses, family dinners, internships, exams, and the awkward digital layer of messages and social media. The banality is intentional; extraordinary philosophical pressure should emerge from normal life.

## Tone
Intimate, observational, sometimes funny, sometimes severe. Indian social texture without turning the setting into a stereotype. Dialogue should feel lived-in. Philosophy should appear mostly as subtext, action, arguments, jokes, misunderstandings, silences, and repeated situations.

## Narrative architecture
Each episode has:
1. A concrete social or relationship problem.
2. A dominant theory of love being practiced.
3. A consequence that complicates that theory.
4. At least one reversal in which the audience's first interpretation is challenged.
5. A closing image or choice that quietly reframes the episode.

## Recurring motifs
- A campus clock that is always a few minutes slow.
- A blue notebook in which characters occasionally write definitions of love.
- Two tea glasses, one usually left untouched.
- A recurring question: "What did you expect me to do?"
- Train/bus departures as metaphors for timing without becoming heavy-handed.
- The line "I chose you" appearing with different meanings over the season.

## Ending principle
The ending should not reward possession with possession. The final emotional victory is the ability to care, choose, communicate, and accept reality without manufacturing a fantasy simply to avoid pain.
