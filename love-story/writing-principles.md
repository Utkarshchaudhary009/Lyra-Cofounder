# Writing Principles

## 1. Story first
Every scene must work as drama without requiring the reader to notice the philosophy.

## 2. No mouthpieces
No character exists to deliver the author's thesis. Everyone can be partly right and partly wrong.

## 3. Earned understanding
Characters only know what their experience has taught them. Their language should evolve gradually.

## 4. Behavioral proof
When a character learns something, prove it through a later choice under pressure.

## 5. Preserve ambiguity
Not every unanswered question needs a definitive answer. Some uncertainty is part of the subject.

## 6. No perfect villains
Most relational damage should emerge from ordinary human motives: fear, desire, pride, avoidance, insecurity, love, ambition, and the wish not to hurt someone.

## 7. No artificial misunderstandings
Conflict should arise from real differences in values, incentives, information, timing, or emotional capacity—not characters refusing to communicate an obvious fact for plot convenience.

## 8. Consequences persist
Apologies, explanations, and realizations cannot erase history. Later behavior must carry the weight of earlier choices.

## 9. Time changes people
A multi-year saga requires changed habits, language, ambitions, friendships, environments, and capacities—not merely older versions of the same personalities.

## 10. Repetition with variation
Recurring situations are valuable when their meaning changes. Never repeat a conflict only to recreate the same emotional scene.

## 11. Quiet is allowed
Some of the most important development happens through ordinary dinners, commutes, work sessions, missed calls, awkward silences, and small choices.

## 12. The reader should infer
Prefer evidence over explanation. A reader who notices the philosophy without being told is the desired outcome.
