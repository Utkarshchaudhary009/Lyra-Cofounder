# Chapter 5: Ghosts of the Cold

The Siberian taiga did not welcome visitors. It swallowed them.

The blizzard was a physical weight, a wall of white that tore at the lungs and blinded the eyes. Lyra pushed through snow that reached her thighs, her breathing ragged, feeling a kind of cold she hadn't known was possible. In the double-helix tower in Tokyo, temperature was perfectly regulated—twenty-one degrees Celsius, always. Here, nature was trying to kill her.

Ahead of her, Mei was struggling, her impossible speed useless against the friction of a frozen world. Anderson, however, moved like an icebreaker. He broke the trail, a dark, broad silhouette against the whiteout. He didn't shiver. He didn't complain.

"Two kilometers left," Anderson's voice came over the sub-vocal comms, devoid of inflection.

"If I freeze to death," Mei gritted out, her teeth chattering, "my ghost is going to haunt your casino chips."

"Keep moving," Anderson said.

Then, the snow exploded.

A high-caliber round tore through the trunk of a birch tree an inch from Lyra's head, spraying her with splinters and bark. Before she could scream, Anderson tackled her into the deep snow. 

Another shot rang out, impossibly loud. The sound echoed off the invisible mountains.

"Sniper!" Mei yelled, dropping low. "How? We're in a whiteout!"

"He's not aiming with his eyes," Anderson said. His body covered Lyra's completely, his weight heavy and uncompromising. A third round struck his shoulder blade. Lyra gasped, but Anderson didn't even flinch. The bullet flattened against his unbreakable skin, tearing his coat but leaving him unharmed.

"Dmitri!" Anderson roared, his voice cutting through the howl of the wind. "Stand down!"

Silence. The wind shrieked.

"Dmitri!" Anderson yelled again. "It's James!"

A long pause. The crosshairs of death hung heavy in the freezing air. Then, a voice cracked over a localized radio frequency they hadn't tuned into—it simply overpowered their comms. Rough, thick with a heavy Russian accent, and dripping with paranoia.

"James? You are supposed to be dead. Or in Montana."

"Plans changed," Anderson said, slowly getting to his knees, keeping himself between Lyra and the origin of the shot. "Let us in before the cold finishes what you started."

***

The cabin was a fortress built into the side of a mountain, surrounded by proximity sensors and crude, lethal traps. Inside, the heat from a massive iron woodstove was a violent assault after the freezing cold.

Dmitri "The Tracker" looked exactly like a man who hadn't spoken to another human being in a decade. He was built like a bear, wrapped in thick furs, his eyes darting with restless, predatory energy. He poured them vodka in silence, his head tilting slightly as if listening to something they couldn't hear.

"You brought a Genesis executive into my house," Dmitri growled, not looking at Lyra, but staring into the fire. He tapped the side of his head. "I can hear her heart. It beats like a metronome. Too steady for someone who just got shot at. Corporate ice in the veins."

"She's the only one who can access the mainframe, Dmitri," Anderson said, standing by the door, scanning the perimeter even though they were already inside. He hadn't taken off his coat.

"And the girl?" Dmitri gestured to Mei, who was huddled by the stove, practically hugging the metal.

"Muscle," Mei said, not looking up. "And I hate the cold. Have I mentioned that?"

Dmitri scoffed. "Cities are too loud. A million heartbeats, a million breaths, a million lies. Here, I only hear the wind. And wolves. Wolves are honest." He finally looked at Anderson. "You need me to find someone."

"I need you to map the Helix tower," Anderson replied. "Every floor, every guard, every heartbeat."

Dmitri's face darkened. "You are going back to the Machine."

As the two men began to talk tactics, Lyra retreated to a shadowed corner of the cabin. Her entire body ached. She pulled her knees to her chest, the heavy wool blanket Dmitri had thrown at her smelling faintly of pine and woodsmoke.

She reached into her inner coat pocket and pulled out a small, crumpled photograph.

She had taken it from Mei's family shrine back in Macau, during the chaos of the ambush. It was a faded black-and-white picture of Mei's grandmother. In the corner of the frame, the sleeve of her traditional dress was pushed up, revealing a faded string of numbers tattooed onto her forearm.

*Subject 3.*

Lyra stared at the numbers until they blurred. She closed her eyes, but the image remained. It matched the list in her grandfather's journal perfectly. Seven numbers. Seven subjects. Seven children.

*Disposability quotient.*

Her lungs felt tight. She wasn't just recruiting a team of useful assets. She was gathering the descendants of the people her grandfather had tortured, broken, and murdered in the name of progress. Genesis Biotech had stolen their futures to build its empire. And now, she was asking them to bleed for her to stop Julian. The guilt was a physical weight, heavier than the blizzard outside.

Lyra stood up and walked to the small, thick windowpane. The glass was heavily frosted, covered in crystalline patterns of ice. She leaned forward, resting her forehead against the freezing surface. Her reflection stared back at her—pale, sharp, exhausted. She looked through the frosted glass at the howling white darkness outside. The cold glass was a barrier, a shield. She had spent her entire life behind glass. Penthouse suites, the Atrium of First Light, corporate limousines. Observation without participation. Complete isolation.

She had always believed control meant safety. But looking at her own hollow reflection, she realized she hadn't been safe. She had just been alone.

A floorboard creaked.

Lyra didn't turn around. She knew the weight of that step. He moved with calibrated silence, but she had learned to feel his presence before she heard him.

Anderson stepped beside her. He didn't look out the window. He looked at her reflection in the glass.

"You're bleeding," he said. His voice was low, devoid of its usual tactical edge.

Lyra touched her cheek. Her fingers came away stained crimson. A splinter of bark from the tree Dmitri had shot must have grazed her. She hadn't even felt it.

"Sit," Anderson instructed, gesturing to a sturdy wooden chair near the table.

She obeyed, too tired to argue. Anderson retrieved a small first-aid kit from his tactical webbing. He pulled up a stool, sitting impossibly close. The scent of cedar and gunpowder clung to him, cutting through the smell of the woodsmoke.

He opened an antiseptic wipe. "This will sting."

"I know how alcohol works," she murmured.

He didn't smile, but the corner of his eye tightened—the closest he ever came. He pressed the wipe to her cheekbone. His touch was clinical, efficient, yet incredibly gentle for hands that could crush a human skull. She held her breath. The contrast between the freezing air in the cabin and the warmth of his skin was dizzying.

As he worked, his heavy coat fell open slightly. Underneath his tactical shirt, near his collarbone, the fabric had shifted, revealing the edge of a deep, jagged scar. It didn't look like a bullet wound. It looked like a burn, or perhaps a chemical brand.

Lyra's eyes caught on it. Anderson noticed her gaze.

He paused, the antiseptic wipe hovering millimeters from her skin.

"Oak Ridge," Anderson said quietly. The words felt torn from him, heavy with dust and eighty years of silence. "Nineteen forty-six. They wanted to see if the serum could regenerate irradiated tissue. Strapped me to a chair. Didn't give me anything for the pain."

Lyra's breath hitched. She looked from the scar to his eyes. They were dark, tired, infinite.

"James..." she whispered.

He swallowed. "They kept burning it. Over and over. Waiting for it to heal. By the fourth day—"

Without thinking, Lyra raised her hand. It was an involuntary movement, a surrender of her immaculate control. Her fingertips drifted through the space between them, reaching for the scar, reaching for the century of pain he carried alone.

Anderson's hand shot out.

He didn't grab her. He just caught her wrist in mid-air. His grip was firm, iron-clad, stopping her inches from his chest.

The air in the room vanished.

For three agonizing seconds, neither of them moved. Her pulse throbbed wildly against his fingers. He stared at her, his jaw clenching so hard she thought his teeth might crack. The distance between them was less than a foot, thick with an electric, desperate gravity. He was looking at her lips, then her eyes.

Eighty years of solitude. Twenty-eight years of control.

To close the distance meant risking everything. Caring was a fatal weakness. 

Anderson dropped her wrist like it was burning him.

He stood up abruptly, knocking the stool back. The sudden movement severed the tension like a knife. He didn't say another word. He didn't look back as he turned and walked out of the room, his heavy boots fading down the short hallway toward the back of the cabin.

Lyra sat frozen, her hand still raised in the empty air.

Her chest ached with a sudden, sharp hollow. She lowered her hand slowly, staring at the empty doorway. The silence in the cabin was deafening, the storm outside suddenly feeling miles away.

Then, she looked down at the wooden table beside her.

Sitting next to the open first-aid kit was a rusted, tin mug. Steam curled gently from its surface. He had made it before he came over to patch her up.

It was black coffee. Exactly the way she took it.

She picked it up. The metal was still warm against her palms.
