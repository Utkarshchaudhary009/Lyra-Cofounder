# Chapter 1: Site Zero

*The first thing they teach you when you inherit a corporation built on human suffering is how to smile at the shareholders.*

Lyra Sterling stood in the ninety-seventh-floor boardroom of the Genesis Biotech Helix, perfectly still. Beyond the floor-to-ceiling glass, the sprawling hyper-density of Tokyo at night was a circuit board of neon and rain. She looked at her own reflection ghosted against the cityscape. The sleek lines of her Tom Ford suit, the geometric precision of her posture, the icy composure of her face—it all overlapped with the shimmering towers of Minato. She stared at the intersection of her own eyes and the red navigation lights of a passing drone, and for a long, unsettling second, she couldn’t tell where she ended and the institution began.

"Quarterly projections for the neuro-regenerative division have exceeded conservative estimates by fourteen percent," Victor Vance was saying, his voice a smooth, modulated baritone that resonated comfortably in the cavernous room. Vance, Chairman of the Board, sat at the head of the long obsidian table. His irises possessed the faint, iridescent sheen characteristic of the Board’s proprietary serum—a subtle reminder that they were no longer operating on standard human biology. His resting heart rate was forty beats per minute. He never blinked more than was absolutely necessary.

Lyra turned from the glass, her heels clicking sharp against the marble. "Fourteen percent is an underperformance, Victor, given the capital we redirected from immune therapies. The market doesn't reward 'exceeding conservative estimates'. It rewards miracles. That’s what we sell."

The boardroom was silent. The eight other board members watched her with placid, serum-enhanced serenity.

Before Vance could respond, a discrete vibration buzzed against Lyra's ribcage. Her encrypted personal device. It was a frequency reserved for a single designation.

She didn’t reach for it immediately. She let a full beat pass, her expression entirely unreadable. "We will reconvene at eight tomorrow to discuss the restructuring of the R&D allocations. Thank you."

She walked out without waiting for their dismissal. As she crossed the Atrium of First Light—five stories of white marble, a living orchid wall, and OLED ceilings broadcasting cellular division—her thumb pressed the biometric sensor on her phone.

The text was a single line.

`SITE ZERO HAS GONE DARK.`

A cold, heavy stone dropped in Lyra’s stomach. Site Zero wasn't on any Genesis corporate map. It was a relic, a buried World War II bunker in the remote mountains of China, heavily fortified and watched over by elite private military contractors—Cerberus Teams.

Her phone buzzed again. Vance. He had stepped out of the boardroom.

"Lyra." The baritone was perfectly level. "An incident at the legacy facility in Qinghai. A catastrophic gas leak in the lower sublevels. Communications have been severed."

"A gas leak," she repeated, stepping into her private elevator. "In an airtight bunker designed to withstand carpet bombing?"

"These old systems degrade, Lyra."

He was lying. The Cerberus Teams ran diagnostics on the ventilation every forty-eight hours. They were equipped with redundant scrubbers. You didn't lose comms from a gas leak. You lost comms from a breach.

"I'll handle the logistics," Lyra said, her voice dropping twenty degrees. "I want the satellite feeds."

"The feeds are down. I'll dispatch a recovery team in the morning."

"I see." She terminated the call. As the elevator plummeted toward the executive garage, Lyra touched her reflection in the polished steel doors. *Gas leak.* Her grandfather’s legacy was buried in that mountain.

Whatever was down there hadn't suffocated. It had woken up.

***

Six thousand miles away, in the jagged, frost-choked mountains of Qinghai, the air inside Site Zero smelled of ozone, copper, and eighty years of stagnant dust.

The Genesis Biotech Cerberus Team had breached the main vault exactly five minutes and twelve seconds ago. They were highly trained, cybernetically enhanced operatives clad in matte-black tactical gear, their sub-vocal communicators pulsing in the darkness. There were nineteen of them.

Now, there were four left standing.

The heavy steel blast door of Sub-Level B12—a door forged in 1943 and designed to hold back armies—had not been blown open. It had been rotted away. A jagged, rust-powdered hole was carved directly through the foot-thick steel, the edges flaking like dead skin from accelerated molecular decay.

Beyond the door, in the flickering emergency amber light, a cryogenic stasis pod hissed as its seals broke.

A man stepped out.

He looked thirty. He was over a hundred. Julian Locke took his first breath of unfiltered air in decades. He didn’t gasp. He inhaled slowly, testing the composition of the atmosphere, letting the chill bite into his lungs. He was naked, his pale skin marked by a grid of ancient, silvered scars along his spine. 

"Target is mobile. Open fire!"

The remaining Cerberus squad had taken positions around the antechamber. Muzzle flashes strobed the darkness, an ear-shattering roar of automated fire. Nine hundred rounds per minute of armor-piercing ammunition converged on the man by the pod.

Julian didn’t flinch. He raised a hand, turning his palm outward.

The bullets struck him and stopped. They didn’t bounce off; they embedded themselves in his flesh, and then, before the operatives' eyes, the wounds simply closed, pushing the deformed lead out to clatter on the concrete floor. His cellular regeneration was moving so fast it looked like water freezing in reverse.

He looked up. His eyes, ordinarily a soft, intelligent brown, ignited with a terrifying, concentrated luminescence.

Twin beams of concentrated radioactive optical energy erupted from his irises. The beams swept across the room with blinding speed. They didn’t just cut through the operatives; they carved through the steel-reinforced concrete pillars behind them. Screams were instantly vaporized into silence as torsos were cleanly bisected, the edges of their armor glowing a furious, molten orange.

Julian lowered his gaze. The light in his eyes faded. Four operatives remained, backing away, their assault rifles shaking.

"Please," Julian whispered. His voice was soft, an aristocratic English accent untouched by time. He didn't sound angry. He sounded profoundly, infinitely sad.

He took a step forward. The concrete beneath his bare feet cracked.

Before he even raised a hand, the neurochemical aura hit them. It was invisible, a projected field of sheer, unadulterated biological terror. The operatives dropped their weapons, falling to their knees. One began to claw at his own helmet, desperate to tear his eyes out as hallucinations of his greatest fears flooded his visual cortex. Another simply began to weep, his nervous system short-circuiting under the weight of an induced despair so heavy it crushed his will to breathe. Four men, neurologically destroyed in seconds, curling into fetal positions on the blood-slicked floor.

Julian walked past them. He didn’t revel in the slaughter. He paused only to pick up a matte-black tactical jacket from one of the dead, slipping it over his shoulders. From a small, sealed compartment in the cryo-pod, he retrieved a single, faded photograph. He stared at it for a moment—a young woman with dark hair, her smile trapped in the 1940s—before folding it carefully into his breast pocket, directly over his heart.

He walked through the corridor of carnage, ascending the spiraling concrete stairs. The massacre had taken exactly five minutes and forty-eight seconds. Nineteen dead. Four neurologically destroyed. 

When he reached the surface, he pushed open the rusted exterior doors. The frigid mountain air rushed in. A full moon hung over the jagged peaks, painting the desolate landscape in pale silver.

Julian stepped out into the moonlight. As his bare feet touched the frozen earth, the permafrost yielded. Thin, green blades of grass sprouted instantly around his ankles, fighting through the snow, blooming wildly, fed by the staggering overflow of his biokinetic energy. 

He stood in the silence of the night, breathing in the scent of pine and blood. The world had moved on. The people who had chained him were likely dead. But the machine they built—the institution that had carved him into a weapon—was still running.

Julian looked down at his hands. They were perfectly still.

"I'm coming," he whispered to the empty air.

The grass around his ankles withered and died, turning to ash.
