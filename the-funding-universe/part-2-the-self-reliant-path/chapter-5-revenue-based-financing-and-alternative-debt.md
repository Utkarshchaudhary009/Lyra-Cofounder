# Chapter 5: Revenue-Based Financing & Alternative Debt — Capital That Bets on Your Revenue

---

## Opening: The Inventory That Almost Killed Them

February 2017. Gurugram.

Ghazal Alagh is staring at a spreadsheet at 11:47 pm. The cursor is blinking on cell D14, which contains the number that won't let her sleep. It says, in cold Calibri font: **₹83 lakh**.

That's how much Mamaearth needs — not eventually, not theoretically, but in the next 19 days — to fund the inventory order that's been sitting with their contract manufacturer in Manesar, waiting for a purchase order confirmation that Ghazal and her husband Varun haven't been able to send. Because they don't have ₹83 lakh. What they have is ₹31 lakh in the bank, ₹52 lakh in outstanding receivables from Nykaa and FirstCry, and a manufacturer who has been patient for two weeks but won't stay patient much longer.

The receivables are real. The money is coming. It is simply not here yet.

Here is the specific hell of being a D2C founder in 2017: you're growing at 30% month-on-month. Your products are selling. You have a proven formula, repeat customers, and a brand that is, against all odds, beginning to mean something. And you cannot get capital from any traditional source because every bank in India requires either three years of audited financials (you have eighteen months), property collateral (you don't own property — you're founders, not property investors), or a personal guarantee against assets you don't have.

The VC conversation is worse. You've had a few. They love the brand. They love the growth. They want 22% of the company for ₹1.5 crore. Twenty-two percent. For ₹83 lakh worth of inventory need — a working capital gap that would resolve itself in 45 days if only someone would bridge it.

Varun, sitting across from Ghazal at their dining-table-that-has-become-a-desk, does the math on the back of a printout: if Mamaearth is worth ₹100 crore someday, that 22% equity costs them ₹22 crore. They're being asked to pay ₹22 crore for a ₹83 lakh inventory problem. The math is insane, but the alternatives are: slow down growth, disappoint the manufacturer, or give away the company.

There is a fourth option they don't know about yet.

---

Three weeks later, a fintech lender they found through a reference from a founder in their network connects their bank account and GST portal to an algorithm. No pitch deck. No three-year financial projection. No in-person meeting. The algorithm looks at 14 months of revenue data, calculates revenue consistency, assesses churn in their repeat customer rate, flags the seasonality pattern in their sales, and 31 hours later tells them: *We will advance you ₹90 lakh at a 9% revenue share, repayable to a 1.3x multiple (₹1.17 crore total), no equity, no personal guarantee.*

This is revenue-based financing. It looks at Mamaearth's monthly revenue — not their story, not their charm, not their projections, just their actual, provable, GST-filed revenue — and says: this is enough collateral. Your sales are your credit score.

Ghazal clicks confirm.

The ₹90 lakh hits their account in 48 hours. They send the purchase order to the manufacturer. The inventory ships. It sells in 6 weeks on Nykaa and Amazon — partly because they've timed it beautifully ahead of Holi, and partly because the products are genuinely good. The revenue spike means they repay the advance in 4.2 months, paying ₹27 lakh above the principal for the privilege. 

That ₹27 lakh is not nothing. It's real money. But it is not 22% of Mamaearth. At Mamaearth's current valuation — the company would reach $1.2 billion by 2022 — 22% would be worth $264 million.

**The VC wanted 22% of her company. The revenue-based lender wanted 9% of her monthly sales for four months. Same capital problem. Completely different universe.**

---

This chapter is about that universe. The universe of capital that doesn't want a piece of your company. It wants to be repaid — with interest, with a revenue share, with a small equity kicker — but it doesn't want to own you. It doesn't want a board seat. It doesn't want to vote on your next hire. It doesn't want to be on the cap table when you eventually sell.

It wants to make a calculable, rational bet on your ability to generate revenue. And if you can do that — if your business has real, consistent, provable revenue — then you have more options than you think.

But here's what nobody tells founders before they discover these instruments: alternative debt is not free money. It has costs — sometimes opaque ones, sometimes brutally high ones. It has covenants that can trap you. It has counterparty risks that can blindside you. In March 2023, 44% of US venture-backed startups learned this the hard way when the bank they trusted, the bank that had been the beating heart of the startup ecosystem for four decades, collapsed in 48 hours.

We'll get to that.

First: let's understand the fundamental insight that makes all of this possible.

## Core Concepts

### 1. The Fundamental Insight: Revenue as Collateral

For most of recorded financial history, if you wanted to borrow money, you needed to own something that could be seized.

Land. Gold. Equipment. Receivables you could assign legally. Real property with a title. The bank's entire business model was built on this logic: we lend you money, and if you don't repay us, we take the thing you pledged. The collateral was the safety net. The lender didn't need to believe in your vision, your market, your team, or your product. They needed to believe in the thing you were pledging against the loan.

This model worked spectacularly well for farmers (land), manufacturers (equipment), and homeowners (property). It worked reasonably well for service businesses with owned real estate. It worked terribly — almost completely — for technology founders, D2C brands, SaaS companies, and digital businesses of any kind.

Because what does a SaaS founder own? Software. Servers, maybe, though increasingly not even that — they're on AWS. A brand. A customer list. Intellectual property that exists in computers and heads. None of these are things a bank can easily seize, hold, and auction to recover a loan. The collateral model broke, quietly and catastrophically, for an entire generation of founders.

Revenue-based financing is built on a different question. Not "what do you own that we can take?" but "what do you earn reliably that we can share until you've paid us back?"

The shift in question changes everything.

If you earn ₹40 lakh a month with 85% consistency across the last 12 months — if that number goes up and down a bit with seasonality but almost never drops below ₹34 lakh — then your revenue is, in every practical sense, more reliable than a piece of machinery that could break, or a property in a market that could crash. Your customers keep paying you. That pattern of payment is predictable. And predictability, for a lender, is everything.

**A bank sees a spreadsheet. Revenue-based financing sees a business. The difference, for a founder who has been rejected by every bank in the city, is everything.**

The mechanics of RBF are elegantly simple. A lender provides you with a fixed amount of capital — let's say ₹50 lakh. You agree to repay through a fixed percentage of your monthly revenue — let's say 8%. You also agree on a total repayment cap — let's say 1.35x the advance, which means ₹67.5 lakh total. Every month, the lender automatically collects 8% of whatever your revenue is, until you've paid back ₹67.5 lakh. No fixed monthly payment. No penalty for slow months. No acceleration clause triggered by lower revenue. If your revenue grows, you repay faster. If your revenue dips, repayment slows. The covenant is built into the structure itself.

That variability is what makes RBF profoundly different from a bank loan. A bank loan has a fixed EMI. If your revenue drops 40% in a bad month — delayed client payment, supply chain issue, seasonal dip — you still owe the EMI. Revenue-based financing breathes with the business. It is, in a phrase that sounds trite but is actually true, capital aligned with business reality.

---

Now for the math that changes how you think about equity.

Imagine you need ₹50 lakh. You have three options: an angel investor takes 15% of your company; an RBF lender charges you a 1.35x cap (₹67.5 lakh total repayment); or a bank loans you ₹50 lakh at 12% for 3 years (EMI of approximately ₹1.66 lakh/month, total repayment approximately ₹60 lakh).

If your company grows to ₹10 crore in value: the angel's 15% is worth ₹1.5 crore. The RBF cost you ₹17.5 lakh. The bank cost you ₹10 lakh. Both debt instruments are cheaper.

If your company grows to ₹50 crore: the angel's 15% is worth ₹7.5 crore. The RBF cost you ₹17.5 lakh. The bank cost you ₹10 lakh.

If your company grows to ₹500 crore — the scale that Mamaearth reached: the angel's 15% is worth ₹75 crore. The RBF cost you ₹17.5 lakh.

The comparison becomes almost comically one-sided at scale. Equity is the most expensive capital for companies that succeed. The tragedy is that you don't know, at the moment of the decision, whether you're going to succeed.

This is the fundamental trade-off at the heart of every financing decision. Not interest rates. Not repayment terms. The real question is: *how much do I believe in my own success, and can I service debt if that belief is wrong?*

### 2. Revenue-Based Financing — Deep Mechanics

The Indian RBF market is younger than you'd expect for how much it's grown.

Velocity, arguably India's most prominent dedicated RBF platform for D2C brands, was founded in 2019. GetVantage launched the same year. Klub followed in 2020. Recur Club built specifically for SaaS and subscription businesses. These are not banks — they're fintech lenders using API connections to your payment gateways, GST portal, bank statements, and marketplace dashboards to underwrite risk in a completely new way.

The underwriting process would have been unimaginable to a bank loan officer from 2005. Velocity, for instance, connects directly to your Shopify store or Amazon Seller Central account. Their algorithm reads your sales velocity, your return rate, your average order value, your month-over-month growth, your customer cohort retention. It then cross-references with your GST filings and bank statement data. In 24–48 hours — sometimes less — it tells you what you qualify for.

There is no pitch deck required. No story about the TAM. No slide about your founding team's credentials. The algorithm doesn't care that you went to IIM. It cares that your February revenue was ₹38 lakh, your March revenue was ₹41 lakh, your repeat customer rate is 34%, and you haven't had a month below ₹33 lakh in the last 8 months. That data profile is worth more to an RBF lender than any founding narrative.

Think about what that means for a founder who doesn't have the "right" background. For a founder who didn't go to the "right" college. Who doesn't have the network to get a warm introduction to a VC. Whose business is profitable and growing but doesn't fit the VC's 100x return thesis. For that founder, the RBF revolution is not an incremental improvement. It is a completely different game.

**For businesses with predictable revenue, the algorithm has become more compassionate than the banker.**

Let's talk about who qualifies. Most Indian RBF platforms require a minimum of ₹20–30 lakh per month in revenue, with at least 6 months of operating history showing some degree of consistency. SaaS businesses are especially attractive because their recurring revenue (ARR/MRR) is the most predictable cash flow in business — a subscription that auto-renews is as close to a guaranteed payment stream as a lender can find in the private markets. D2C brands with high repeat purchase rates also qualify strongly. Marketplace businesses with consistent GMV. Ed-tech platforms with subscription models. Any business where revenue recurs and can be demonstrated clearly through data.

Who doesn't qualify is equally important to understand: pre-revenue startups (obviously), businesses with highly lumpy, project-based income (an architecture firm gets ₹80 lakh in January when a project closes and ₹3 lakh in November — an RBF algorithm will reject this), and businesses with negative unit economics. That last point deserves emphasis. If you're losing money on every transaction — if your CAC is higher than your LTV — RBF lenders will see it in the data, and they will decline. Revenue-based financing is capital for businesses that work. It is not rescue capital for businesses that don't.

---

Here's a pattern Velocity's portfolio illustrates beautifully.

A D2C skincare brand — let's call it a representative composite of dozens of Velocity's actual customers — is doing ₹45 lakh a month on a combination of Myntra, Amazon, and their own Shopify site. They've been growing at 8% month-over-month for 11 months. Their repeat customer rate is 28%. Their EBITDA margin is 14%.

October arrives. Diwali is 6 weeks away. The brand knows from last year that October-November-December produces 40% of their annual revenue. They need to pre-order inventory now — in September — to have it ready. The inventory costs ₹1.8 crore. They have ₹60 lakh in the bank. They need ₹1.2 crore in 10 days.

Velocity looks at the data, sees the seasonal pattern, extends ₹1.2 crore at an 8% revenue share with a 1.3x cap (₹1.56 crore total repayment). The brand orders the inventory. The Diwali season produces ₹1.8 crore in sales across the three months. At 8% revenue share, they repay ₹1.44 lakh per crore — they've repaid the full ₹1.56 crore by mid-January. The cost of capital for those four months is ₹36 lakh.

Alternative: had they taken VC money for this at a pre-money valuation of ₹8 crore, they'd have given up roughly 13% of the company for ₹1.2 crore. If this brand grows to ₹50 crore someday, that 13% is worth ₹6.5 crore.

The comparison requires no further comment.

But here is the thing no one celebrates when they celebrate RBF: the ₹36 lakh is gone. Permanently. In the equity scenario, the founder loses the theoretical upside — but they also risked nothing concrete in the present. The ₹36 lakh cost of the RBF is real money leaving the business today. In a tight cash month, that 8% revenue haircut hurts.

The honest truth about RBF is that it's a spectacular tool for businesses that qualify and are growing. It is also an additional burden in months where growth slows or revenue disappoints. The flexibility of the structure helps — repayment slows automatically — but the total cost is higher than almost every bank loan alternative. The question is not whether RBF is cheap. It isn't. The question is whether it's cheaper than the alternative, and cheaper than equity almost always, yes.

There's one more thing about that Mamaearth story from the opening. In 2021, Mamaearth raised ₹50 crore in a Series F round at a valuation of ₹2,400 crore. By the time the company listed on the NSE in November 2023, its market cap was approximately ₹16,000 crore. Every rupee of equity given away in those early years — at those early valuations — cost the founders thousands of times the amount borrowed.

The money Ghazal clicked confirm on in 2017 was the cheapest capital she ever used.

### 3. Venture Debt — The Institutional Version

Revenue-based financing is available to any business with data-provable revenue. Venture debt is something different — it's a specific financial instrument designed for a specific animal: the VC-backed startup.

The distinction matters.

Venture debt is debt extended to startups that have already raised institutional equity. It is typically offered by specialized lenders — not banks, not RBF platforms — who understand the specific risk profile of a high-growth, probably-still-burning-cash startup. The lenders accept this unusual risk for two reasons: they charge higher interest rates than banks (14–18% in India, often higher globally), and they take a small equity warrant as additional compensation — usually 0.5% to 1.5% of the company, exercisable at the price of the last equity round.

In India, this market is dominated by three players: **Trifecta Capital**, **InnoVen Capital**, and **Alteria Capital**.

Trifecta Capital deserves its own moment in this chapter. Founded by Rahul Khanna and Nilesh Kothari, it is India's largest venture debt fund, having deployed over ₹2,000 crore across 100+ startups including Lenskart, Urban Company, Zetwerk, Furlenco, and Dailyhunt. The founding insight: every VC-backed company that is between equity rounds needs some amount of capital to bridge to the next milestone. Banks won't lend because the companies are burning cash. Equity investors don't want to write small checks between rounds. Venture debt fills precisely this gap.

How does a typical Trifecta deal work? A startup has raised a Series B of ₹80 crore. Their runway is 18 months. At month 10, they're ahead on some metrics, slightly behind on others, and the Series C conversation is going to happen in 4–6 months. But those 4–6 months are going to require more cash than expected — a key enterprise client wants a custom integration that requires 6 engineers for 3 months, a new city expansion is running 20% over budget, the marketing team has identified a paid acquisition channel that's working but needs more fuel.

The startup could try to raise a Series C now, but they're not quite at the metrics that would justify the valuation they need. A premature Series C at a lower valuation means more dilution and a potential morale-crushing "down round" dynamic. Instead, they take ₹20 crore from Trifecta: 3-year loan, 16% interest, 1% warrant. Total cost: ₹9.6 crore in interest over 3 years, plus a warrant on 1% of the company.

Six months later, they've hit the metrics. The Series C comes in at a strong valuation. The venture debt has extended the runway at the exact right moment. The equity dilution avoided at the premature Series C is worth far more than the interest cost.

**Venture debt is the financial equivalent of a bridge over a canyon. You don't live on it. You cross it. And you'd better make sure there's solid ground on the other side.**

The providers of venture debt understand this. Rahul Khanna has spoken publicly about Trifecta's underwriting discipline: they only lend to companies they believe will reach their next equity milestone. They look at cohort economics, burn rate relative to the next round needed, team quality, sector tailwinds. They are, in a meaningful sense, doing the same analysis a VC does — they're just placing a debt bet instead of an equity bet.

This is also why venture debt is different from bank debt in a critical way: venture debt lenders are sophisticated enough to understand the startup's actual situation. They don't expect profitability. They don't require hard collateral. They're betting on the founder's ability to hit milestones and raise the next round. In exchange for that unusual latitude, they get a higher return on the debt component and a small equity upside.

But — and this must be said clearly, without softening — venture debt is not patient capital.

If the Series C doesn't happen. If the growth stalls. If the market shifts and VCs tighten their checkbooks. If the founder makes a strategic mistake that pushes the next round out by 12 months instead of 6. The debt doesn't pause. The interest keeps accruing. And at some point, the lender will exercise the rights in the loan agreement that protect their capital — rights that can include demanding early repayment, triggering cross-default clauses, or in extreme cases, forcing a sale of the company at whatever price the market will bear.

This is the scenario that played out across dozens of Indian startups in 2022–23, when a global VC winter hit and the next rounds that everyone expected simply did not materialize on the expected timelines. Companies that had taken venture debt during the growth years suddenly found themselves in a situation nobody had modeled: the bridge was built, but the other side of the canyon had moved further away.

Venture debt, used correctly, buys time. Used incorrectly — or caught in bad luck — it brings the reckoning forward.

---

The InnoVen Capital story adds another dimension. Founded as a joint venture between Temasek Holdings (Singapore's sovereign wealth fund) and UOB Bank, InnoVen has been India's other major venture debt player, having backed companies like Cure.fit, Bounce, and Licious. InnoVen's Singapore parentage means they can also serve Indian startups with global operations in ways that pure domestic lenders can't. Their presence signals something important: the venture debt market in India is not a fringe phenomenon. It is backed by institutional capital from the world's most sophisticated investors.

The total venture debt market in India has grown from negligible in 2015 to an estimated ₹5,000–7,000 crore annually by 2024. That's a category that didn't exist in meaningful form a decade ago. The founders who know it's there — and know how to use it — have a tool that their predecessors simply didn't have access to.

The founders who don't know about it raise an equity round they didn't need to.

### 4. Traditional Bank Loans — Why They Don't Work (and When They Do)

Let's be honest about banks. Not cynical — honest.

Indian banks are not villains. They operate within a system designed to minimize defaults using centuries-old logic. The 2004–2012 period, when Indian banks extended enormous loans to infrastructure and real estate companies that subsequently defaulted, creating a ₹10-lakh-crore NPA crisis, is very much alive in every bank's institutional memory. Banks are cautious because they have been catastrophically wrong before. Their caution is rational given what happened to them.

But rational caution for a bank is a death sentence for a startup.

A bank will require, at minimum: two to three years of audited, profitable financial statements. Hard collateral — property, machinery, gold — that can be seized and sold. A CIBIL score above 700 (the individual founder's personal credit history). A personal guarantee, meaning the founder personally promises to repay if the business can't. And typically, a banking relationship of at least 12–18 months.

Most startups fail three of these five on day one.

The collateral requirement alone eliminates almost every technology company. What does a SaaS startup own? Code, yes — but try convincing a bank to accept code as collateral. The brand? Harder still. The customer list? Non-transferable. The domain name and intellectual property? Technically seizable, but a bank branch manager in Bengaluru has no framework for valuing or liquidating a domain name and a software product.

So banks say no. And then startups say banks are villains. Neither is quite right.

**There is a version of this story that works. It just requires waiting longer than most founders want to wait.**

After 3 years of operations, with consistent GST filings, audited books, a CIBIL score maintained carefully, and some fixed assets (even minor ones — vehicles, computers, leasehold improvements), a growing startup can access bank credit. Usually initially through a working capital demand loan or a cash credit facility — not a term loan. Working capital credit means the bank gives you a revolving credit line tied to your inventory and receivables. You draw down on it when you need cash, repay it when payments come in, and the cycle continues.

This form of bank credit, available to mature startups, can be astonishingly cheap — 10–12% per annum with good collateral. Bank credit at 12% makes RBF at 35% look expensive. This is why smart founders transition to bank credit as early as they can qualify, using RBF as a bridge to get there.

For those at the very beginning — micro-entrepreneurs, first-generation business owners, small manufacturers — the Indian government has created something extraordinary.

---

The **Pradhan Mantri MUDRA Yojana** was launched in 2015 with an idea that sounds almost impossible: give loans up to ₹10 lakh to micro-enterprises, with no collateral required, channeled through banks and MFIs and NBFCs across India. 

By 2024, MUDRA had disbursed over ₹22 lakh crore in loans — not in rupees, in crore — to more than 40 crore loan accounts. This is not a niche scheme. This is the largest small business lending program in the world by the sheer number of loans disbursed.

MUDRA operates in three tiers: Shishu (up to ₹50,000), Kishor (₹50,000 to ₹5 lakh), and Tarun (₹5 lakh to ₹10 lakh). The amounts are modest — ₹10 lakh is the ceiling, which won't fund a technology startup. But for the vegetable vendor who wants to buy a refrigerated cart, the dressmaker who wants a second sewing machine, the chai stall owner who wants to expand to a second location — MUDRA is transformative.

Consider Savitabai from Nashik, who borrowed ₹2.3 lakh under the Kishor category in 2018 to expand her papad manufacturing unit. By 2022, she employed seven women from her neighborhood and had a contract supplying two restaurant chains. The MUDRA loan was the first formal credit she had ever received. The entire collateral-free system meant that a woman with no property in her name — only in her husband's name, as is tragically common in India — could access credit based on her business plan and her lender's assessment.

That is not a small thing. That is the quiet revolution that nobody's writing thinkpieces about.

MUDRA has its critics — the default rates, particularly in Shishu loans, have been higher than comfortable. There are documented cases of political pressure to disburse loans that shouldn't have been made. The ₹22 lakh crore headline number includes a lot of restructured and rolled-over loans. None of this erases the fundamental point: India has created a credit infrastructure for micro-entrepreneurs that did not exist before 2015.

For tech founders, the more relevant institution is **SIDBI — the Small Industries Development Bank of India**. SIDBI's mandate has been evolving with India's startup ecosystem. It acts as a fund-of-funds — investing in VC funds that then invest in startups. It has direct lending schemes for SMEs and startups. It channels capital into the Startup India Seed Fund (alongside DPIIT). In 2022, SIDBI launched a dedicated fund for startups with a focus on early-stage lending at rates below market.

None of this is fast, or simple, or as exciting as a VC term sheet. But government-backed credit, accessed strategically, is often the cheapest capital available to Indian businesses. The founders who study these instruments — who actually read the SIDBI scheme guidelines, who actually speak to a MUDRA-authorized lender — often find capital that their peers, spending all their time chasing VCs, never knew existed.

### 5. Invoice Discounting & Factoring — The Working Capital Hack

It is 3:15 pm on a Tuesday in March 2022. An auto component manufacturer in Pune — a small supplier with 23 employees, supplying precision-machined brackets to a Tier-1 automotive company that supplies Maruti Suzuki — is staring at a bank account that has ₹8.9 lakh.

The payroll for 23 employees is ₹14.2 lakh, due in 12 days.

He is not broke. He has a signed purchase order from the Tier-1 supplier for ₹42 lakh worth of components, already manufactured, already quality-checked, already sitting in his warehouse, ready for pickup next week. He has an invoice for ₹42 lakh that will be paid — he has worked with this client for 6 years and they have never once defaulted — but will be paid in 90 days, because that is the payment term that large corporations impose on their small suppliers.

This is the working capital problem in its purest, most damaging form. He is not failing. He is not mismanaged. He is not wasteful. He has a growing order book and a proven product. He just cannot bend time. And payroll cannot wait 90 days.

The solution is called invoice discounting, and it is one of the most important financial instruments that most small business owners in India have never heard of.

The mechanics are simple. He takes his ₹42 lakh invoice to an invoice discounting platform — KredX, Cashflo, Vayana Network, or directly through the TReDS platform — and he sells it. Not assigns it, not pledges it — sells it. The platform (or the banks funding through it) give him 85–90% of the invoice value immediately: ₹35.7 to ₹37.8 lakh, same day or within 48 hours. When Maruti's Tier-1 supplier pays the invoice in 90 days, the full ₹42 lakh goes to the platform. The platform keeps the difference — ₹4.2 to ₹6.3 lakh — as their return. The business owner pays somewhere between 10–15% of the invoice value, annualized to roughly 15–20% APR.

He pays his payroll. His employees get paid. His machinery keeps running. His client never even knows the transaction happened.

This is not theoretical. KredX, one of India's leading invoice discounting platforms, has processed over ₹15,000 crore in invoices since its founding in 2015. Their client list includes businesses ranging from ₹50-lakh-annual-turnover micro-manufacturers to ₹500-crore mid-market companies. The use cases are remarkably consistent: the working capital gap created by the payment term asymmetry between large corporates and their small suppliers.

---

But the most significant development in this space — and the one that gets almost no attention in the startup press, despite being genuinely revolutionary — is **TReDS: the Trade Receivables Discounting System**.

RBI mandated the creation of TReDS in 2014 and licensed three platforms: M1xchange (run by Mynd Solutions), RXIL (run by SIDBI and NSE), and A.TREDS (run by Axis Bank and mjunction). The system works like an organized exchange for invoices. MSMEs upload their invoices from large corporate buyers. The large corporate confirms the invoice. Multiple financiers (banks, NBFCs) then bid to discount the invoice — yes, compete to give the MSME money. The MSME gets the best rate, and the market forces keep rates competitive.

In 2023, the Government of India extended TReDS registration requirements to all companies with turnover above ₹250 crore — meaning they must register and allow their MSME suppliers to access TReDS. This was not a suggestion. It was a regulatory mandate. The Ministry of MSME has been increasingly aggressive about enforcement.

The impact is real: MSME suppliers who previously had no leverage over their large corporate buyers' 90-day payment terms now have a mechanism to access the cash early, at competitive rates, backed by the strength of the corporate buyer's credit rating rather than their own. A small supplier to Tata Motors can discount invoices at rates closer to what Tata Motors would pay — 10–12% APR — because Tata Motors' creditworthiness backs the instrument.

**When a small supplier's borrowing rate depends on their own tiny balance sheet, they pay 24%. When it depends on Tata Motors' balance sheet, they pay 11%. TReDS is how you legally borrow someone else's creditworthiness.**

The adoption curve is still early. Awareness among MSMEs is low. Many business owners who would benefit from TReDS have never heard of it. The registration process intimidates people who don't have a CFO or a finance team. But for those who navigate the setup — which takes a day, once you know what you're doing — it transforms the working capital picture.

### 6. The Merchant Cash Advance — The Payday Loan of Business Finance

Let's be completely honest. No sugarcoating.

The merchant cash advance, or MCA, is the financial instrument that the most desperate businesses use when they've been turned down by everyone else. A lender gives you a lump sum — let's say ₹5 lakh — in exchange for a fixed percentage of your daily UPI or credit card sales until a pre-agreed total amount (let's say ₹7.5 lakh) has been repaid. The repayment comes out automatically, daily, as a percentage of every transaction. No fixed EMI. No monthly billing. Just a quiet, constant drain on your incoming sales.

The cost structure is designed to obscure the real rate. The "factor rate" model — you repay 1.3x to 1.5x of what you borrowed — sounds manageable until you calculate the annualized percentage rate. A ₹5 lakh advance repaid as ₹7.5 lakh over 8 months is a 78% APR. Some MCAs run higher. Some run into triple digits when the repayment period is short and the factor rate is high.

Why does anyone take these? Because they work fast (approval in hours, not days), they require no collateral, they don't check CIBIL scores, and the daily automatic deduction means the lender has no collection problem. For a restaurant that's been open 7 months, has no property to pledge, no formal credit history, and needs ₹3 lakh to fix a broken commercial refrigerator or lose the entire lunch menu — the MCA might be the only option.

There is exactly one scenario where an MCA is not obviously harmful: a genuinely seasonal business with a discrete, short peak. A fireworks retailer who needs ₹8 lakh in October inventory for a Diwali season that will generate ₹25 lakh in revenue across November. They know, with reasonable certainty, that they'll repay the advance in 6 weeks from the season's sales. The high annualized rate matters much less when the actual repayment period is 6 weeks and the revenue opportunity is real.

Every other scenario deserves scrutiny.

**A merchant cash advance on a struggling business is not capital. It is a very expensive way to postpone a conversation you need to have.**

The conversation is: why is this business struggling? Is it the product? The pricing? The location? The competition? The team? The market? Those questions have answers that capital cannot buy. Borrowing at 78% APR to fund operations that are losing money is not a financing strategy. It is an acceleration of the inevitable, with an additional fee attached.

The founders who end up in the MCA spiral — taking a new advance to repay the previous one, each cycle more expensive than the last — are not bad business people. Many of them are brilliant and hardworking. But they've confused capital access with business viability, and the MCA lender is sophisticated enough to know the difference. The lender is making money on the decline. The founder is paying for time they probably can't use productively.

This is not to vilify MCA lenders — they serve a genuine market need for businesses that have no other options. But every founder who picks up an MCA brochure should do the APR math first. If the annualized rate exceeds 50%, and the revenue opportunity isn't discretely large and near-term, close the brochure and have the other conversation.

### 7. The True Cost of Capital — The Framework Every Founder Must Master

Here is the exercise that will change how you think about every funding decision you ever make.

Take a blank piece of paper. Write down your capital need in the top left corner. Let's say ₹50 lakh. Now draw four columns, and label them: VC Equity, RBF, Venture Debt, Bank Loan.

Under each column, write what you'd give up today for that ₹50 lakh. VC equity: 15–20% of your company. RBF: a 1.35x cap, so ₹67.5 lakh total. Venture debt: 16% interest for 3 years plus a 1% warrant. Bank loan: 12% per annum for 3 years.

Now draw three more rows: "Company worth ₹10 crore," "Company worth ₹50 crore," "Company worth ₹500 crore."

Fill in the real cost of each option at each outcome:

| Option | Nominal Cost | At ₹10 Cr Exit | At ₹50 Cr Exit | At ₹500 Cr Exit |
|--------|-------------|----------------|----------------|-----------------|
| VC Equity (17% stake) | ₹0 upfront | ₹1.7 Cr | ₹8.5 Cr | ₹85 Cr |
| RBF (1.35x cap) | ₹17.5 L | ₹17.5 L | ₹17.5 L | ₹17.5 L |
| Venture Debt (16% + 1% warrant) | ~₹24 L interest + warrant | ~₹29 L total | ~₹29 L + ₹50 L warrant = ₹79 L | ~₹29 L + ₹5 Cr warrant = ₹5.3 Cr |
| Bank Loan (12%, 3 years) | ₹10.2 L interest | ₹10.2 L | ₹10.2 L | ₹10.2 L |

Read that table slowly. The bank loan's cost doesn't change no matter how successful you become. The RBF cost doesn't change. But the equity cost — the VC's stake — grows with every rupee of value you create. The more successful you are, the more expensive equity becomes, retroactively.

The math is devastating in its clarity. And yet most founders, when they face a ₹50 lakh capital need, default to raising equity — because they've been taught to think of equity as "free" (no interest payment today) and debt as "expensive" (interest is a real cash cost today).

The confusion comes from conflating *present cash cost* with *real economic cost*. Debt has a present cash cost. Equity has a deferred economic cost. For companies that succeed, the deferred cost is almost always larger — often many times larger — than the present cost would have been.

This doesn't mean you should always take debt. The table's right side has a fatal assumption: that the company reaches those exit values. If the company fails — if you're in the 90% of startups that don't make it — then the equity scenario costs you nothing (you gave away a percentage of zero). The debt scenarios cost you the loan balance, which you either have to repay from personal assets (if you gave a personal guarantee) or which triggers a company-level default (if you didn't).

The risk profile is asymmetric: equity costs more when you win, debt costs more when you lose.

Which means the question isn't "which is cheaper?" The question is: "How confident am I in success, and what can I afford to lose if I'm wrong?" The higher your confidence in success, the more expensive equity becomes relative to debt. The more uncertain your path, the more dangerous debt becomes.

This is why stage matters enormously in financing decisions. A pre-revenue startup taking debt is almost always wrong — the uncertainty is too high, the downside of default too severe. A Series B company with 3 years of data showing consistent 25% month-on-month growth taking equity instead of venture debt is almost always leaving money on the table.

**The right funding instrument is not the one with the lowest interest rate. It's the one whose cost structure matches the probability distribution of your outcomes.**

### 8. The SVB Collapse — When the Plumbing Breaks

March 8, 2023. A Wednesday afternoon. Greg Becker, CEO of Silicon Valley Bank, announces on a call with investors that SVB has taken a $1.8 billion loss on a bond portfolio sale and needs to raise $2.25 billion in new capital to shore up the balance sheet.

By Thursday, Peter Thiel's Founders Fund has wired all its money out of SVB and advised its portfolio companies to do the same.

By Friday morning, 10,000 founders and CFOs across the US are frantically trying to wire money out of SVB accounts while the banking system processes a run unlike anything it has seen since the Great Depression.

By Friday at 3:45 pm, the FDIC has taken SVB into receivership. The second-largest bank failure in US history is complete in 44 hours.

What happened — the actual mechanics — is worth understanding precisely, because it's not what most people think. SVB didn't fail because it made bad loans to risky startups. It failed because it invested customer deposits in something extremely safe: US Treasury bonds and government-backed mortgage securities. In 2020 and 2021, as the startup ecosystem flooded SVB with deposits (VC rounds of unprecedented size, all parked temporarily at SVB), the bank invested those deposits in long-duration bonds.

Then the Fed raised interest rates at the fastest pace in 40 years. When interest rates rise, existing bond values fall — this is basic bond mathematics. SVB's bond portfolio, perfectly safe in terms of credit (US government won't default), was now worth less in market value than what they'd paid. Not a problem as long as depositors didn't ask for their money back. They only had to hold to maturity.

But depositors did ask for their money back. The VC winter of 2022 meant startups were burning reserves. SVB had to sell bonds — at a loss — to meet withdrawal demands. The announcement of the loss triggered the very bank run that made the announced loss real. It was a self-fulfilling prophecy, accelerated by WhatsApp group chats where founders compared notes in real time and Twitter threads by prominent VCs (some of whom had conflicts of interest in advising withdrawals) lighting the fuse.

The lesson is not that SVB was reckless. The lesson is that the startup ecosystem had built a dangerous concentration of financial dependency on a single institution.

SVB held accounts for 44% of US venture-backed startups. Not 4%. Not 14%. Forty-four percent. Half the venture-backed startup ecosystem in the United States banked at the same place. When that place failed, 44% of US startups simultaneously faced payroll risk, vendor payment uncertainty, and existential panic — in a single weekend.

**SVB had $200 billion in assets and collapsed in 48 hours. The lesson isn't that banks are dangerous. The lesson is that concentration is.**

The India angle cuts in two ways. First, several Indian startups with US operations had accounts at SVB or at SVB's partner institutions, and experienced the same 48-hour panic. CRED, which had US operations, was among those who had to rapidly assess exposure. Second, and more importantly, the SVB collapse prompted a moment of honest reckoning in the Indian startup ecosystem: did Indian VC-backed startups have the same concentration problem? Many of them banked primarily at one of two or three banks — HDFC Bank and Kotak Mahindra Bank being disproportionately favored by the startup community. Not quite 44% concentration, but the question was worth asking.

The operational reality of the SVB weekend deserves to be imagined concretely. It's Sunday afternoon. Your company has ₹3 crore in its primary bank account. You learn the bank is in regulatory trouble. Your payroll is Thursday — ₹45 lakh. Your key AWS bill auto-charges Tuesday — ₹18 lakh. Your office rent draws Wednesday — ₹4.5 lakh. You don't know if any of these payments will clear.

What do you do?

If you have a second bank account with a buffer — two weeks of runway, separate institution — you redirect everything there. You call your key vendors, explain the situation, buy goodwill. You call your employees before they find out from Twitter. You survive the weekend.

If you don't have a second account — if you, like many founders, maintained one primary account because it was simpler — you have nothing to redirect to. You spend the weekend waiting for regulatory decisions you cannot influence. You may or may not survive depending on how quickly the regulator acts.

For US companies, the FDIC stepped in and guaranteed all deposits within 48 hours of the failure — a decision made over the weekend that prevented the scenario from becoming catastrophic at scale. Indian founders and companies who had SVB exposure were still waiting anxiously Monday morning for clarity.

The aftermath produced one behavioral change that every CFO and founder should internalize: **never have 100% of your operating capital at a single institution.** The cost of maintaining two or three bank accounts, of keeping 3–4 weeks of runway at a secondary bank, of having vendor payment accounts separate from your payroll account, is trivial. The insurance value of that setup is enormous.

This is not paranoia. This is what treasury management looks like for every large corporation, and what it should look like for every serious startup.

## Pros & Cons

### Revenue-Based Financing

| Dimension | The Honest Picture |
|-----------|--------------------|
| **Speed** | 24–72 hours from application to funding. Velocity and GetVantage have processed applications and disbursed funds within 48 hours. No comparison to a bank loan's 4–8 week timeline. |
| **Dilution** | Zero equity given. No cap table entry. No board seat. For founders who are equity-protective, this is existential. |
| **True Cost** | 25–40% effective annual rate in most cases. Higher than any bank loan. Lower than almost any equity deal for a company that succeeds significantly. |
| **Repayment Flexibility** | Revenue-contingent repayment means slow months automatically mean slower repayment. No EMI default. No covenant breach from revenue volatility. |
| **Eligibility** | Requires established revenue (₹20–30L/month minimum for most Indian platforms), limiting access for early-stage companies that might need it most. |
| **Data Exposure** | The lender needs API access to your bank account, payment gateway, and GST portal. Some founders are uncomfortable with this level of financial transparency. |
| **Use Case Fit** | Perfect for working capital, inventory cycles, and growth marketing. Poor fit for product development, team hiring, or long-horizon investments where revenue payoff is delayed. |
| **Provider Health** | RBF providers are themselves funded by investors and debt capital. If a provider faces a liquidity crisis, the outstanding advance terms could be affected. Know your lender's financial health. |

### Venture Debt

| Dimension | The Honest Picture |
|-----------|--------------------|
| **Runway Extension** | At its best: extends runway by 6–12 months without equity dilution, bought at the cost of interest and a small warrant. Trifecta portfolio companies report this as the primary use case. |
| **Cost** | 14–18% interest in India, plus 0.5–1.5% warrants. Total cost significantly below equity cost for successful companies. |
| **Prerequisite** | Requires existing VC backing. Not available to bootstrapped companies. The lender's underwriting depends on the equity investor's credibility as much as the company's. |
| **Covenants** | Real. Financial covenants (minimum revenue growth, minimum cash balance), reporting covenants (monthly financials), and event-based covenants (change of control clauses) all exist. Violating them has consequences. |
| **Default Risk** | If the next equity round doesn't happen, the debt is still due. This scenario — where the bridge doesn't reach the other side — can force a distressed sale. |
| **Discipline Effect** | Trifecta's founders explicitly say they use covenants to impose spending discipline. Founders who chafe at this find it frustrating. Founders who embrace it often say it made them better operators. |
| **Warrant Dilution** | Small (0.5–1.5%) but real. At high exit valuations, even 0.5% is meaningful. Negotiate it. |
| **Provider Stability** | InnoVen (Temasek/UOB-backed) and Trifecta (well-capitalized) are stable. New entrants in the venture debt space carry more provider-level risk. |

### Traditional Bank Loans

| Dimension | The Honest Picture |
|-----------|--------------------|
| **Cost** | 10–14% per annum. The cheapest formal debt available. HDFC Bank's working capital loans for established businesses can run at base rate + 2–3%. |
| **Collateral Requirement** | Hard collateral required — property, machinery, gold, or government-backed guarantees (CGTMSE). Eliminates most pure-tech and asset-light businesses. |
| **Speed** | 4–12 weeks from application to disbursement, assuming clean documentation. A startup without a dedicated finance team will take longer. |
| **Relationship Value** | A long-term bank relationship compounds. Once established (3+ years), a company with a clean banking history gets access to progressively larger credit facilities at lower rates. Worth building deliberately. |
| **Startup Incompatibility** | Banks evaluate based on historical profitability. A startup that's investing in growth and burning cash looks terrible to a bank, even if the economics are sound. |
| **MUDRA/CGTMSE** | Government guarantee schemes (CGTMSE covers loans up to ₹5 crore for MSMEs without collateral) exist and work. They are underused because most entrepreneurs don't know they exist. |

### Invoice Discounting

| Dimension | The Honest Picture |
|-----------|--------------------|
| **Precisely Targeted** | Solves one specific problem — large-corporate payment delays — better than any other instrument. Not a general-purpose financing tool. |
| **Cost** | 15–20% APR on the discounted amount. High in absolute terms, but appropriate when the alternative is missing payroll. |
| **No Collateral** | The invoice IS the collateral. The buyer's creditworthiness backs the transaction. A ₹25 lakh invoice from Infosys is far more secure collateral than any physical asset a small vendor might have. |
| **TReDS Advantage** | Through TReDS, competitive bidding among financiers pushes rates down toward the buyer's (large corporate's) borrowing rate. An MSME supplier to Infosys can borrow at near-Infosys rates. This is genuinely remarkable. |
| **Speed** | KredX and Cashflo routinely disburse within 24–48 hours of invoice verification. M1xchange (TReDS) is slightly slower due to buyer confirmation requirements. |
| **Concentration Risk** | If your revenue is concentrated in one or two large buyers, your invoice discounting capacity is too. This is fine while the relationship holds; dangerous if the buyer relationship changes. |
| **Buyer Cooperation** | Traditional invoice discounting requires the buyer to confirm the invoice. Some large corporations are slow or resistant. TReDS has made buyer confirmation mandatory for registered corporates. |

## The One Thing to Remember

> There is a confusion at the heart of how founders think about money that costs them, cumulatively, billions of rupees in unnecessary dilution.
>
> **Equity is permanent. Debt is temporary. The founder who confuses them pays for it twice.**
>
> When you give equity to solve a working capital problem — when you trade 18% of your company to bridge a 60-day receivables gap — you have made a permanent decision about the permanent ownership of your company to solve a temporary problem. That temporary problem would have resolved itself in 60 days. The equity decision doesn't resolve. It compounds, for the entire life of the company, at whatever rate of value creation you manage to generate.
>
> Revenue-based financing, venture debt, invoice discounting, and bank credit exist because not every capital need is a permanent-ownership problem. Some capital needs are simply bridge problems: you need money now, you'll have money in 90 days, you need someone to hand you that money and wait. Paying 15–20% for that bridge, and keeping 100% of your company, is almost always the right trade for a business that works.
>
> But — this matters — debt only works if your revenue can carry it. Before you click confirm on any debt instrument, know your revenue. Know your covenants. Know your lender's financial health. Know what happens if your growth misses by 30%. Model it honestly. If the answer is "we can still service the debt," you are probably right to prefer debt over equity for this capital need.
>
> If the answer is "we'd be in default," you are not ready for debt. And that's information you need before the crisis, not during it.

## How to Use This Tomorrow

These are not suggestions. These are actions. Do them in the order listed.

**Step 1: Calculate your working capital gap today.**

Working capital gap = (Days Sales Outstanding) – (Days Payable Outstanding) = X days × (Annual Revenue / 365) = ₹ gap.

Open your accounting software right now. Find your average collection period (how many days does it take customers to pay you on average?). Find your average payment period (how many days do you take to pay suppliers?). If the first number is larger than the second, you have a working capital gap. If it's 30 days or more, you have a structural problem that can be solved — but it needs to be named before it can be fixed.

**Step 2: Check your RBF eligibility.**

Do you have ₹20 lakh or more per month in revenue, for 6 consecutive months? If yes, get quotes from Velocity, GetVantage, and Recur Club this week. Not next month. This week. Fill out their online applications — they typically take 20 minutes. You'll have quotes within 48–72 hours. Compare the cap multiple and the revenue percentage. Run the true cost calculation: if you repay 1.35x over 5 months, what is your effective annual rate? Is that rate better or worse than giving up equity?

**Step 3: If you're VC-backed, have the venture debt conversation today.**

Call your lead investor or board member and ask explicitly: "Does venture debt make sense for us at this stage?" They may say no, and that's valid information. But if the answer is "we hadn't thought about it" or "that's worth exploring," follow up with Trifecta Capital, InnoVen Capital, and Alteria Capital. Their websites have intake forms. The conversation costs nothing and the intelligence gathered is invaluable for the next round discussion.

**Step 4: Register on TReDS if you have large-corporate receivables.**

Log onto the M1xchange website. The registration process for an MSME takes approximately 1–2 hours of documentation gathering. If you regularly wait 60–90 days for payment from large corporations, this will be the most valuable hour you spend this month. Even if you don't use it immediately, having the account set up means you can access it within 24 hours when you need it.

**Step 5: Audit your banking relationships this afternoon.**

Open your company's list of bank accounts. Count them. If the answer is one, you have a concentration risk that the SVB collapse made undeniable. Open a second business current account at a different institution — not a different branch of the same bank, a genuinely different institution. Keep 3–4 weeks of payroll and key operational expenses in the secondary account at all times. Set a calendar reminder to check the balance monthly. This takes one afternoon to set up and provides irreplaceable insurance.

**Step 6: Calculate the true equity cost of your last or next capital raise.**

Take the percentage equity offered in your last round (or the percentage you're planning to offer in your next). Multiply that percentage by ₹10 crore. That's the cost at a modest exit. Multiply by ₹100 crore. That's the cost at a significant exit. Multiply by ₹1,000 crore. Is that number visible on paper? That is what you've agreed to pay for the capital you've raised — contingent on success. Is there a debt alternative that would have been cheaper? Was there one available? Why didn't you explore it?

The exercise won't always produce a different decision. Sometimes equity is the right call. But knowing the real cost transforms how you negotiate, what you offer, and what you keep.

## Exercises

### Exercise 1: Map Your Cash Conversion Cycle

The cash conversion cycle (CCC) is the number of days between when your company spends cash and when it receives cash from customers. It is the single most important operational metric for understanding your working capital needs.

**The formula:** CCC = Days Inventory Outstanding + Days Sales Outstanding – Days Payable Outstanding

**Your task:**
1. Find your average inventory age (how long does inventory sit before it's sold?). If you're a service business or SaaS company, this is zero.
2. Find your average collection period (total accounts receivable ÷ (annual revenue / 365)).
3. Find your average payables period (total accounts payable ÷ (annual COGS or purchases / 365)).
4. Calculate CCC = DIO + DSO – DPO.

If your CCC is 45 days and your monthly revenue is ₹30 lakh, your working capital requirement is: (45/30) × ₹30 lakh = ₹45 lakh perpetually tied up in the cycle. This is the permanent working capital need that doesn't go away even when the business is profitable. Every growing business must fund this gap somehow. Now you have a number.

**Bonus calculation:** If you reduce your DSO from 45 days to 30 days (by offering a 2% early payment discount, improving collections, or using invoice discounting), how much cash does that free up?

### Exercise 2: The True Cost Comparison

You need ₹25 lakh of capital. You have four options in front of you.

**Option A:** An angel investor offers ₹25 lakh for 12% of your company.

**Option B:** An RBF lender offers ₹25 lakh with a 1.3x repayment cap (₹32.5 lakh total), collected as 7% of monthly revenue.

**Option C:** A venture debt fund (if you're VC-backed) offers ₹25 lakh at 15% per annum for 3 years with a 1% warrant on your post-money cap.

**Option D:** A bank offers ₹25 lakh at 13% per annum for 3 years with a personal guarantee and property collateral.

**Calculate the real cost of each option at these three company outcomes:**
- Company sells for ₹5 crore (modest outcome)
- Company sells for ₹50 crore (good outcome)
- Company sells for ₹500 crore (exceptional outcome)

For Option A, the cost is (12% × exit value). For Option B, the cost is flat ₹7.5 lakh regardless of outcome. For Option C, calculate total interest (₹25L × 15% × 3 years = ₹11.25L) plus warrant value (1% × exit value). For Option D, calculate total interest (approximately ₹10.3L) and ignore warrant.

**Discussion questions:**
- At what exit value does angel equity become more expensive than RBF?
- At what exit value does venture debt become more expensive than RBF?
- Under what circumstances is the bank loan the best option?
- Which option would you choose if you believed your company had a 60% chance of reaching ₹50 crore but a 30% chance of failing entirely?

### Exercise 5: The Covenant Stress Test

Imagine you have taken ₹2 crore in venture debt from a fund. The loan agreement (which you should read carefully — this exercise prepares you to do that) contains the following covenants:

- **Financial covenant:** Monthly revenue must not decline more than 20% versus the previous month's revenue for more than 2 consecutive months.
- **Cash covenant:** You must maintain a minimum cash balance of ₹50 lakh at all times.
- **Reporting covenant:** Audited financials must be submitted within 90 days of each fiscal year-end.

4. What actions could you take operationally to cure the covenant breach — i.e., bring yourself back into compliance — within 30 days?

## Further Reading

**1. *Venture Deals* by Brad Feld and Jason Mendelson (4th Edition)**

The definitive text on startup financing deals, including a robust treatment of debt instruments, warrant structures, and term sheet mechanics. Feld is a veteran VC at Foundry Group who writes with unusual clarity about instruments that most VCs prefer to keep opaque. The chapter on convertible debt and the discussion of bridge loans will change how you read any term sheet. Highly recommended alongside a legal dictionary — it is technical, but worth the effort.

**2. Trifecta Capital's Published Insights and Rahul Khanna's Interviews**

Rahul Khanna, co-founder of Trifecta Capital, has given extensive interviews to platforms including YourStory, Mint, and Inc42, and has written publicly about venture debt's place in the Indian startup ecosystem. Search "Rahul Khanna Trifecta venture debt" and read everything that comes up — the body of work is perhaps the best India-specific resource on how venture debt actually works in practice, what terms are standard, and what founders get wrong. This is practitioner knowledge from someone who has done 100+ deals.

**3. RBI's TReDS Guidelines and MSME Ministry Circulars**

Dry. Bureaucratic. Absolutely essential if you are an MSME with large-corporate receivables. The RBI's master circular on TReDS (available at rbi.org.in) explains the regulatory framework, eligible participants, and operational requirements. The MSME Ministry's circulars on mandatory TReDS registration for large corporates (companies with ₹250+ crore turnover) tell you which of your customers are required to allow you to discount invoices. This is boring reading that will earn you money.

**4. GetVantage's Annual State of D2C Financing Reports**

GetVantage publishes an annual report on the state of D2C brand financing in India, based on their actual portfolio data. Unlike consultant reports that synthesize publicly available information, this is real-world data from hundreds of Indian D2C brands: what they borrow, at what cost, for what purpose, and what outcomes result. The most India-specific financing data available in publicly readable form. Search "GetVantage State of D2C Financing [year]" on their website.

**5. *The Algebra of Wealth* by Scott Galloway**

Not specifically about startup financing — this is a broader book about building financial security. But Galloway writes with ferocious clarity about the true cost of financial instruments, the mathematics of compound costs, and the way that financially unsophisticated decisions made at 28 compound into economically damaging consequences at 45. The mental frameworks in this book — particularly around understanding real costs versus nominal costs — are directly applicable to every financing decision a founder makes. The chapter on diversification should be required reading after the SVB story above.

## Closing: What Comes Next

Every instrument in this chapter has had a cost. Sometimes a high one.

Revenue-based financing costs you a percentage of your sales, month after month, until a fixed multiple is repaid. Venture debt charges interest and takes a small equity warrant. Bank loans require collateral you may not have, charge interest you must repay on schedule, and demand personal guarantees that follow you beyond the company. Invoice discounting charges 15–20% annualized on the face value of your invoices. Merchant cash advances can run into triple-digit APRs that should make any financially literate person wince.

Even the cheapest formal debt has a cost. Even the most founder-friendly RBF deal is not free money.

But there exists a category of capital where the cost structure is fundamentally different. Not cheaper. Structurally different. A category where the capital provider doesn't want equity. Doesn't want interest. Doesn't want a revenue share. Doesn't want a warrant.

They want to see something built.

A government scheme that believes you're creating jobs in a sector the country needs. A philanthropic foundation that believes your innovation will solve a problem they've been funding for a decade. A startup competition where the prize money is real and the obligation attached to it is nothing but performance. A grant from an accelerator that believes in your thesis and is willing to pay you to prove it.

Chapter 6 is about this category: **Grants, Competitions, and Non-Dilutive Capital.** The money the government, the philanthropist, the competition organizer, and the accelerator will give you — often because you're building something they believe the world needs, and they're willing to pay you to find out if they're right.

No equity. No debt. No repayment.

Just a belief in what you're building, from someone who has decided that belief is worth funding.
