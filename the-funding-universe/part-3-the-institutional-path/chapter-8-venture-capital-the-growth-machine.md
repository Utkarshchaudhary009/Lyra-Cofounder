# Chapter 8: Venture Capital — The Growth Machine

---

## Opening Story: The Cheque That Changed Everything

Bengaluru, January 2010.

The city is mid-monsoon heat in January — which is to say, not hot yet, but already alive with a particular kind of energy that the technology corridors of Koramangala and Indiranagar carry in their bones. India has 525 million mobile subscribers. It has fewer than 50 million internet users. It has, perhaps, 17 million credit card holders in a country of 1.2 billion people. The idea that Indians will buy things online — that they will hand over their payment details to a website, wait 3–5 days for delivery, and trust that the product will arrive — is considered, by most serious retail observers, a curiosity at best.

At worst: a fantasy.

Sachin Bansal and Binny Bansal — not related, despite the surname — have been selling books out of their apartment since 2007. They worked together at Amazon before this. They know what an e-commerce company looks like from the inside. They know the operational choreography: warehousing, last-mile logistics, returns, customer service. What they are building in Bengaluru right now is, to the casual observer, a very small book-selling website. They have about ₹40 lakh in savings between them. They ship orders themselves, on bikes, because they can't yet afford a proper logistics partner. When a customer calls with a complaint, Sachin picks up the phone.

They call it Flipkart.

Subrata Mitra has seen a lot of pitches. He joined Accel India's Bengaluru office as a founding partner, and in 2010 he is evaluating early-stage internet companies with the specific hypothesis that India's digital future is arriving faster than the market consensus believes. He is looking for founders who are not building for the consensus. He is looking for founders who are building for a reality that doesn't exist yet but is, in their bones, inevitable.

He meets the Bansals. The pitch is not slick. It doesn't need to be. What Mitra later describes as "missionary zeal" fills the room before the slide deck does. These are not two people who want to build a business and sell it quickly. They are two people who have looked at what India's retail sector looks like — fragmented, inefficient, geographically constrained, chronically bad at serving the customer — and decided, with a certainty that borders on stubbornness, that they will fix it. Not as a side project. Not for the exit. Because India needs it fixed, and they are the people who know how.

The ask: $1 million.

The Bansals have done the math. A million dollars at the exchange rate of that era — roughly ₹4.5 crore — buys them: a proper office space in Koramangala, their first ten non-founder hires, server infrastructure that can actually handle growth, a working capital cushion for inventory, and the beginning of a marketing budget. It does not buy them comfort. It does not buy them a runway long enough to be complacent. What it buys them, specifically, is the ability to stop doing everything themselves and start building an organization.

Subrata Mitra writes the cheque.

What happens next — in the two years following that $1 million — is the real story. Flipkart moves from books to electronics to everything. They invent cash-on-delivery at scale because most of their customers don't have credit cards, and the workaround becomes the industry standard. They build a logistics network called Ekart because no third-party logistics provider can meet their service standards. They grow from ₹25 lakh in monthly revenue in early 2010 to ₹500 crore in annual revenue by 2012. The velocity is startling — not just to the market, but to the founders themselves. Capital, when applied to a business that has genuine product-market fit and a team that knows how to operate, creates a compounding effect that no spreadsheet fully captures until you're living inside it.

But here is what the funding announcement press releases don't tell you.

The $1 million from Accel was the beginning of a journey, not a destination. After Accel came Tiger Global — first a $10 million Series B in 2010, then $20 million, then $150 million. Then Naspers. Then DST Global. Then Sofina. Then Morgan Stanley. Then T. Rowe Price. Then SoftBank. Each round brought not just more capital but more investors with their own objectives, their own fund economics, their own views on what Flipkart should be and how fast it should get there. By 2015, Flipkart had raised over $3 billion. Its board was no longer a small room with founders and one trusted investor. It was a complex organism with multiple competing interests, each with legal rights — liquidation preferences, veto clauses, anti-dilution provisions — baked into the very structure of the company.

Sachin Bansal, who had shipped books on a bike in 2007, was by 2015 the chairman of a company valued at $15 billion.

And he was no longer running it.

We'll come back to what happened to Sachin — specifically what happened to him when the Walmart acquisition closed in 2018 and $16 billion changed hands. The answer to that question will tell you more about what VC capital actually does to a founder than any textbook. But first, you need to understand the machine itself: how venture capital works, why it works the way it works, and — most importantly for you — whether it is the right tool for what you are building.

Because Flipkart's story is not a success story about VC. It is a case study in how VC shapes the company you build, the control you retain, and the definition of "winning" you get to use. Those are different things. And the founder who can't distinguish between them will, at the moment of maximum consequence, be surprised.

## Part One: How VC Actually Works — The Economics Every Founder Must Understand

### The Machine Behind the Money

Before we talk about what VC does to founders, you need to understand what VC is doing for itself. This is not cynicism. It is the most important intellectual foundation for navigating this world.

A venture capital firm raises money from Limited Partners — LPs. These are the people and institutions who actually own the capital: pension funds, university endowments (Harvard Management Company, Stanford Management Company), sovereign wealth funds, family offices, and, at later stages, even other VC funds. When you take money from Sequoia India, you are not taking money from Shailendra Singh's personal savings account. You are taking money from a pool of capital that was raised from dozens of institutional investors who gave Sequoia the mandate to deploy it in exchange for returns.

The VC firm — the General Partner, or GP — manages this pool and makes investment decisions. The GP typically contributes 1–2% of the fund's capital themselves. It is a small number and a large commitment; a founding partner at a $500 million fund who contributes 1% personally has $5 million of their own money in the game. That matters. When a VC firm's fund performs badly, the partners lose their personal capital too.

Now, the fee structure. This is important.

The GP charges a 2% annual management fee on the fund size. A $500 million fund generates $10 million per year in management fees. Over a 10-year fund life, that's $100 million in fees — before a single investment returns a single rupee of profit. This pays salaries, rent, legal expenses, due diligence costs, and the general operations of the firm. A venture capitalist who makes no successful investments still draws a salary. This is not a dirty secret; it is necessary economics for a firm that needs to run for a decade without guaranteed income. But it means the VC is not purely incentivized by your success. They are also funded by their own fee structure, which continues regardless.

The real upside — the reason smart people spend careers in VC — is carried interest, or "carry." The GP earns 20% of profits above a hurdle rate, typically 8% annual return to LPs. On a $500 million fund that returns $1.5 billion, the profit is $1 billion, and the GP's carry is $200 million, divided among the partners. This is the wealth-creation mechanism in venture capital — but it only activates if the fund performs dramatically.

Here is the math that drives every single VC decision you will ever sit across the table from.

A $500 million fund must return $500 million to LPs before any carry is earned. To generate meaningful carry — to make the VC business worthwhile — a fund needs to return approximately 3x: $1.5 billion. A typical fund makes 20–30 investments. If you do the arithmetic, 2–3 investments must each return the entire fund. One company returning $500 million is not a great fund. It is a fund that breaks even and earns its management fees. Two companies each returning $300–400 million starts to look interesting. One company returning $2 billion on a $10 million investment — that is a fund that produces generational wealth for the GP.

This is the power law that governs venture capital. Not as a philosophy. As arithmetic.

A VC's job is not to help you build a great company. Their job is to return their fund. Sometimes those goals align perfectly. Sometimes they don't. The founder who can't tell the difference will be surprised at the worst possible moment.

### The Fund Life — The Clock You Don't See

VC funds have a fixed life, typically 10 years. The first 3–4 years are the investment period — this is when the GP is actively deploying capital into new companies. The remaining 6–7 years are the management and exit period: supporting existing portfolio companies, helping them reach exits (IPO, acquisition, or secondary sale), and returning capital to LPs.

This creates an invisible clock in every VC conversation.

A VC whose fund is in year 2 has a very different time horizon than a VC whose fund is in year 7. Year-2 money is patient money; the partner has time. Year-7 money is urgent; the exit window is narrowing, LPs are getting impatient, and the GP needs outcomes before the fund life terminates. When a VC sitting across from you asks about your "path to exit," they are not being aggressive. They are telling you where they are in their fund's lifecycle.

Ask every VC you meet: "When was this fund raised? Where are you in the fund life?"

If they've been managing this fund for 7 years and you're raising a Series A, they may not be the right partner for a 5-year journey. Not because they're bad investors. Because the math doesn't work for either of you.

This is the fund-life pressure that shapes every board meeting, every growth target, every "you need to accelerate revenue" conversation you will ever have with a VC who is, unbeknownst to you, three years from their fund's terminal date.

### Why This Matters to You

Sequoia India — now called Peak XV Partners — has raised successive funds, each larger than the last, reflecting their LP base's confidence in their performance. Their portfolio includes Zomato, BYJU's, Druva, Meesho, BharatPe, Pine Labs, Citius Tech, and dozens of others. For the power law to work at a $1 billion fund, a handful of these companies need to reach outcomes of $5–10 billion in enterprise value. A company worth $200 million at exit is a good outcome for the founder. For the fund, it barely registers.

This creates an incentive structure with specific implications. Sequoia needs you to be a $1 billion-plus company. If you'd be perfectly happy building a ₹500 crore revenue business with healthy margins and a comfortable leadership team — a genuinely excellent company by any measure — Sequoia India is the wrong partner. Not because they're bad people. Because their fund economics literally cannot be satisfied by your success at that scale.

This is not an argument against Sequoia. It is an argument for being clear-eyed about the relationship you're entering and whether the incentives are aligned with the company you actually want to build.

The founder who raises from a fund whose power law requires a $1 billion-plus exit, while privately hoping to sell for $100 million in year 5 to a strategic acquirer, has set a trap for themselves. The board will not support that exit. Not because they're cruel. Because the math doesn't work.

## Part Two: The Stages of VC — What Round Are You Raising?

### Mapping the Landscape

Venture capital is not a monolith. It operates across a spectrum of stages — each with different risk profiles, different investor types, different expectations, and different amounts of capital. Knowing exactly where you sit in this spectrum before you begin fundraising is the difference between targeting the right partners and wasting six months talking to people who will never write you a cheque.

**Pre-seed and Seed**

In the Indian context, seed funding typically ranges from ₹50 lakh to ₹5 crore, at pre-money valuations of ₹5 to ₹40 crore. Globally, this corresponds to $500K–$3 million at $3–15 million pre-money valuations. What is expected at this stage: an idea with evidence of insight, a team with relevant capabilities, possibly an MVP, maybe a few hundred early users. Revenue is nice. Traction of any kind is better. But at seed stage, the primary asset being valued is the team's ability to discover and solve the problem they're attacking.

The active Indian seed investors include Blume Ventures (the deepest seed-stage franchise in India, with a strong founder community), Kalaari Capital, Stellaris Venture Partners, Lightspeed India Partners making early bets, Titan Capital (Snapdeal founders Rohit Bansal and Kunal Bahl's vehicle), 100X.VC, and Surge — Sequoia India's accelerator program, which takes approximately 15 companies per cohort. Y Combinator takes Indian companies regularly and remains the most globally connected accelerator in the world.

BharatPe's early funding is instructive here. Ashneer Grover and Shashvat Nakrani raised their first institutional round when BharatPe was essentially a thesis: UPI transactions are growing exponentially, merchants are being underserved by the existing payment infrastructure, and a single QR code that accepts all payment apps could capture enormous volume. The thesis relied on understanding India's UPI architecture before most investors had figured out what UPI would become. Sequoia India backed the thesis at seed stage — not the product, not the revenue, not the metrics. The thesis and the team.

**Series A**

Series A in India currently runs from ₹15 crore to ₹80 crore, at pre-money valuations of ₹80 crore to ₹500 crore depending on the sector and metrics. This is where VCs expect something specific: evidence of product-market fit. Not perfection. Evidence. Users who come back. Revenue that's growing week-over-week. Unit economics that are ugly but improving. A clear hypothesis for why this business can scale to 10x its current size in the next 24 months.

The Freshworks story at Series A is worth sitting with, because it was not a clean process.

Girish Mathrubootham had founded Freshdesk in 2010 after a specific, maddening customer service experience — he'd tried to replace a broken TV and the support process was so infuriating that he quit his job at Zoho and decided to build better customer support software. By early 2011, Freshdesk had 150+ paying customers, almost entirely SMBs outside India (he had deliberately focused on international markets because Indian SMBs didn't pay for software). The product was working. The unit economics made sense. The market was real.

He approached twelve VCs. Eleven said no.

The rejections ranged from "the market is too crowded" to "Salesforce will eat you" to the quietly devastating "we don't think India can produce enterprise SaaS at global scale." Mathrubootham has spoken publicly about what those months felt like — the conversations with his wife about whether to keep going, the recalculation of the savings runway, the quiet terror of rejection when you can see, with your own eyes, that the product is working and customers are paying.

Shekhar Kirani at Accel India said yes. Freshdesk raised its Series A from Accel. Today Freshworks is listed on NASDAQ, has over $500 million in annual revenue, and is one of the most successful Indian SaaS companies in the world. Eleven partners at eleven VC firms passed on Girish Mathrubootham's Series A pitch. Nobody talks about those eleven any more.

Sequoia has backed Apple, Google, WhatsApp, and YouTube. They've also backed dozens of companies you've never heard of because they failed. The brand doesn't guarantee anything. It just changes who picks up the phone.

**Series B and Beyond**

Series B in India runs from ₹80 crore to ₹500 crore-plus. At this stage, VCs expect: proven scalability (revenue that has grown at least 2x year-over-year for two consecutive years), clear market leadership or a credible path to it, financial metrics — gross margin, customer acquisition cost, lifetime value — that support the valuation being discussed. The investors operating at this stage in India are a different breed: Prosus/Naspers, Tiger Global, SoftBank Vision Fund, General Atlantic, Warburg Pincus, DST Global. These are crossover investors and late-stage growth funds — not traditional early-stage VCs.

Zomato's fundraising trajectory is the most instructive Indian case study for how investor profiles change as a company scales. Zomato (originally Foodiebay) was founded by Deepinder Goyal and Pankaj Chaddah in 2008 as a digitized restaurant menu platform — they photographed menus and put them online. Sequoia India backed them early. Then Info Edge (the company behind Naukri.com) became a major investor. Then Alibaba's Ant Financial participated. Then Uber Eats India was acquired. By the time of Zomato's IPO in July 2021 — the first major Indian startup IPO, the one that opened the door for every subsequent listing — the investor table included entities from three continents, representing capital from dozens of countries.

Each investor came in at a different stage. Each brought different expectations, different network effects, different time horizons. Managing that table — keeping all of those interests broadly aligned while still running the company — is one of the least visible and most demanding aspects of being a late-stage founder. Deepinder Goyal has spoken about the board dynamics with a frankness unusual for Indian founders: it was not always harmonious. But the relationship held, and the IPO vindicated the journey.

### The 2021 Inflation Problem — And What It Cost Everyone

No chapter on VC in India can avoid this. 2021 was the year Indian startup fundraising lost its tether to reality, and the hangover is still being felt.

Global interest rates were near zero. Central banks in the US and Europe had flooded the financial system with liquidity after COVID-19. This capital had to go somewhere — and it went into venture. Funds with more money than deals competed to deploy at any valuation. Indian startups raised at 50x, 80x, even 100x revenue multiples. The number of Indian unicorns (companies valued above $1 billion) went from 38 at the start of 2021 to 100 by the end of it. Forty-four new unicorns in a single year. The previous decade had produced less than forty total.

BYJU's reached a $22 billion valuation. Paytm IPO'd at a $20 billion valuation — the largest Indian IPO in history — and lost 75% of its market capitalization in the following 12 months. Ola Electric's valuation at peak was disconnected from its manufacturing reality. CRED, a platform that rewards people for paying credit card bills on time, raised at a valuation most analysts couldn't justify against the revenue figures publicly available.

The founders who raised in 2021 inherited two things simultaneously: a lot of money and an impossible standard to justify the valuation they had accepted.

A company that raises at $500 million post-money in 2021 cannot raise its next round at $300 million without triggering a down round. And a down round — fundraising at a lower valuation than the previous round — is not just a financial event. It is a signal. It resets the cap table against founders, underwater options make recruiting harder, existing investors are diluted (which may trigger ratchet provisions that further dilute founders), and the market narrative shifts from "rocket ship" to "company in trouble." The psychological weight of that shift on the founding team cannot be overstated.

The BYJU's collapse is the sharpest available example — and we will examine it in full later in this chapter, because it deserves more than a paragraph. For now: the seeds of BYJU's catastrophic fall were planted not in 2022 when the problems became public, but in 2021, when the fundraising frenzy enabled a company to raise at a valuation that required outcomes its business could not deliver. Nobody in that chain — not the founders, not the investors, not the bankers, not the press — had sufficient incentive to say so at the time.

Taking VC money is not a milestone. It is a decision about what kind of company you want to build. Make it with your eyes open, not because everyone else is doing it.

## Part Three: The Term Sheet — Every Clause That Will Define Your Life

### The Document That Changes Everything

You have survived the pitch meetings. You have survived the partner meetings. You have survived the due diligence, which felt like having your entire company disassembled and examined under fluorescent lighting. And now a PDF has arrived in your inbox, and its subject line says "Term Sheet — [Your Company Name]."

Stop.

Do not celebrate yet. Do not send it to your co-founder in a moment of euphoria. Do not post it on LinkedIn. Do not sign it because you're exhausted from the fundraising process and want it to be over.

Read every word. Understand every clause. Because the term sheet is not a contract. It is a personality test. How the VC behaves during term sheet negotiation is how they will behave at every board meeting for the next ten years.

### Valuation: The Math Behind the Number

The headline number in any term sheet is the pre-money valuation. If a VC says "we'll invest ₹20 crore at ₹80 crore pre-money," here is the arithmetic: the post-money valuation is ₹100 crore. The VC owns 20% (₹20 crore of ₹100 crore post-money). You own 80%. Simple.

Except it is not simple, because of three things every first-time founder misses.

**Fully diluted share count.** The VC's ownership percentage is calculated on fully diluted shares — which includes not just outstanding shares held by founders, but also all outstanding stock options (whether vested or unvested), all convertible instruments (SAFEs, convertible notes, warrants), and any new ESOP pool expansion that the VC is requiring as part of this round. If your outstanding share count is 10 million shares but your fully diluted count including all outstanding options and convertibles is 12 million shares, your calculation is off by 20%. Run the fully diluted number. Every time.

**The ESOP pool shuffle.** This is the single most common term sheet trap that first-time founders fall into. VCs routinely require, as a condition of investment, that the company expand its Employee Stock Option Pool before the new investment closes. This expansion dilutes the existing shareholders — primarily the founders — before the new capital comes in. Here is the effect with numbers:

Pre-round: Founders own 100% of 10 million shares. VC agrees to invest ₹20 crore at ₹80 crore pre-money. But the VC requires a 10% ESOP pool expansion. 

The company creates 1.11 million new shares (10% of the post-expansion total of 11.11 million). Founders now own 10 million of 11.11 million = 90%. Then the VC invests, getting shares worth 20% of post-money. But the 20% is calculated on the post-expansion, post-investment fully diluted count, which now includes the new ESOP pool. 

The founders' effective dilution is greater than it appears from the headline valuation. The ESOP pool was created from their stake, not the VC's. This is not fraud. It is a standard practice. But the founder who doesn't model this before signing will discover the real numbers at closing, when it is too late to renegotiate.

### Liquidation Preference: Where Money Actually Goes When You Exit

This is the clause that determines who gets paid first when the company is sold. It seems abstract during a fundraising high. It becomes very concrete during an acquisition conversation.

**1x non-participating liquidation preference** is standard and reasonable. It means: when the company exits, the VC gets either (a) 1x their investment back, or (b) their pro-rata percentage of the total proceeds — whichever is higher. They choose. In a good exit (company sells for a large multiple of the investment), they'll choose pro-rata. In a bad exit (company sells for less than its post-money valuation), they'll choose 1x. This is fair.

**1x participating liquidation preference** is a double-dip. The VC gets their full investment back AND their pro-rata share of the remaining proceeds. Fight this.

**2x or higher non-participating preference** means the VC gets 2x their investment before anyone else sees a rupee. Common in bridge rounds, emergency rounds, and down rounds. Very expensive for everyone else on the cap table.

Let's do the math with a real scenario, because the abstract description doesn't communicate the weight of these numbers.

A company sells for ₹150 crore. Two investors:

- Investor A invested ₹30 crore for 25% with 1x non-participating preference.
- Investor B invested ₹50 crore for 30% with 2x participating preference.
- Founders own 45%.

**What happens:**

Investor B's 2x participating preference kicks in first: ₹100 crore (2x of ₹50 crore invested).

Remaining: ₹50 crore.

Investor A: 1x preference is ₹30 crore. Pro-rata of ₹50 crore remaining at 25% is ₹12.5 crore. They choose 1x preference: ₹30 crore.

Remaining after Investor A: ₹20 crore.

Now Investor B participates again in the remaining ₹20 crore (this is the double-dip — they already got their 2x back, but participating means they also get their pro-rata of what's left). Investor B gets 30% of ₹20 crore = ₹6 crore.

Founders get: ₹20 crore - ₹6 crore = ₹14 crore.

Out of a ₹150 crore acquisition, the founders who built the company receive ₹14 crore. Investor B, who put in ₹50 crore, receives ₹106 crore.

These numbers are not hypothetical. They describe what happens in a significant percentage of Indian startup acquisitions that get called "successful" in press releases. The press release says "company acquired for ₹150 crore." What it doesn't say is that the founders walked away with less than 10% of the stated purchase price.

Read your liquidation preference clauses. Understand them. Negotiate them. This is where the real negotiation happens — not on the headline valuation.

### Anti-Dilution: Protection Against Your Own Failure

Anti-dilution provisions protect investors if you raise a future round at a lower valuation (a down round).

**Full ratchet** is the most punishing form. If you raise at any price lower than the current round's price, the investor's share count is automatically adjusted upward — potentially all the way down to the new price — to maintain their effective ownership percentage. In a severe down round, this can result in catastrophic founder dilution. Avoid this clause if you can. It is unusual in standard Series A terms, but appears in bridge rounds and emergency financings.

**Weighted average anti-dilution** is the standard. It adjusts the investor's conversion price proportionally, based on how large the down round is relative to the existing capitalization. A narrow-based weighted average is more protective for investors. A broad-based weighted average is more founder-friendly. Both are better than full ratchet.

The anti-dilution clause you negotiate today will determine what happens to you if you ever face a down round. And in a market that saw widespread valuation inflation in 2021, the likelihood of that scenario is not academic.

### Board Composition: Where Real Power Lives

Read this section twice.

The board is not where strategy is discussed in the abstract. The board is where real power over the company is exercised. The board can fire the CEO. The board can approve or block major acquisitions. The board can approve or block new fundraising rounds. The board can change the company's direction over the founder's objection if the board votes are not in the founder's favor.

A founder-friendly board structure for a Series A: 2 founder seats, 1 investor seat, 2 independent seats (mutually agreed). The founders control the board with 2 votes out of 5 only if the independents are genuinely independent — and the founders should understand that independent directors, in practice, often side with investors on financial and governance matters.

An investor-friendly board structure: 1 founder seat, 2 investor seats, 1 independent seat chosen by the investors. In this structure, the founder is in a permanent minority on the board. Every major decision is made by people who were not the founders.

Somewhere between these two configurations is the real negotiation.

Understand the specific mechanics of your proposed board before you sign. Know what the threshold is for removing the CEO. Know what decisions require board approval vs. CEO discretion. Know whether the investors have consent rights (separate from board votes) over major decisions like acquisitions, new debt, or issuing new shares.

This brings us to the moment we withheld from the opening.

Sachin Bansal built Flipkart from a bike-delivered book business to India's most valuable startup. But the board that governed Flipkart by 2015 was not the board of 2010. Tiger Global, which had become Flipkart's largest investor after multiple rounds, eventually placed Kalyan Krishnamurthy — a Tiger Global operating executive — in the CEO role. Not in a coup. Not against Sachin's will in any single moment. But through a series of board decisions, each of which made sense at the time, that incrementally shifted operational control from the founders to the investors.

By the time of the Walmart acquisition in May 2018 — $16 billion, the largest e-commerce acquisition in history — Sachin Bansal had already been transitioned out of any operational role. He walked away with approximately $1 billion from the sale. He is, by any external metric, extraordinarily successful.

But Flipkart — the company he shipped books on a bike to create, the company he was building because India needed it — belonged, in the end, to Walmart. And the board votes that made that outcome possible were agreed to, clause by clause, in term sheets that Sachin Bansal signed when he was focused on the funding and the growth, not on the fine print about governance.

Each VC round changed the balance of power by a little. By the time the aggregate effect was visible, it could no longer be reversed. The lesson is not that Sachin made bad decisions. He made smart decisions at each individual step. The lesson is that you cannot evaluate a term sheet in isolation. You must evaluate what it will mean for the company and your role in it if five more rounds follow.

### Other Clauses That Matter

**Pro-rata rights** give investors the right to participate in future rounds to maintain their percentage. Generally favorable for investors who are adding value — they stay committed. Can be complicated if a hot later-stage round is oversubscribed and earlier investors are fighting for allocation.

**Information rights** specify what financial data you must share with investors and how frequently. Standard: monthly management accounts, quarterly board meetings. Non-standard: real-time dashboards, weekly calls, customer-level data. Know what you're agreeing to before you sign. Some investors are genuinely additive with this access. Others use it to micromanage.

**Drag-along rights** allow a majority of investors to force all shareholders — including founders and minority investors — to sell the company if they vote to do so. Without drag-along, a single investor with a small stake can block an acquisition by refusing to sell. With drag-along, the majority controls the decision. The details of the majority threshold and who counts toward it are the critical variables. A poorly written drag-along can force you to sell a company you built at a price you believe is too low.

**Right of first refusal (ROFR)** gives existing investors the first opportunity to buy shares if you (or another shareholder) want to sell to an outside party. Limits your ability to sell secondary shares for liquidity without VC approval. Relevant when you're six years into a company and your net worth is entirely in illiquid startup equity.

The term sheet is the beginning of a relationship, not the end of a negotiation. The founder who treats it as a formality to rush through is agreeing to terms they have not fully understood. The founder who engages with it seriously — ideally with a good startup lawyer and at least one experienced mentor who has seen these clauses before — is protecting themselves for the years ahead.

## Part Four: Choosing Your VC — The Founder's Due Diligence

### The Asymmetry Problem

VCs evaluate thousands of companies a year. A top-tier VC firm like Accel India or Sequoia India sees perhaps 2,000 inbound opportunities annually and invests in perhaps 10–15. By the time a VC partner sits across from you in a term sheet conversation, they have done this dozens of times. You, most likely, are doing it for the first time.

This asymmetry is the single biggest structural disadvantage a first-time founder faces in a VC fundraise. The investor knows what they're buying. You may not fully know what you're selling.

The answer is due diligence. And not just theirs on you. Yours on them.

VCs choose founders. But founders also choose VCs. This is not just a nice philosophical point. It is the practical reality of a relationship that will, if things go well, last 7–10 years — through your best months, your worst months, your biggest strategic mistakes, and your moments of greatest clarity. You will be in board meetings with this person during the 3am crises. You will call them when an acquisition offer arrives that you don't know how to evaluate. You will look across the table at them during the board conversation about whether you are still the right CEO for the company you founded.

Choose carefully.

### The Questions Every Founder Must Ask

**"Tell me about a company in your portfolio that failed. What happened?"**

This question reveals everything. A VC who cannot discuss failure honestly is either hiding portfolio problems or has insufficient self-awareness for an honest partnership. A VC who blames the founder entirely — "they just couldn't execute" — has no ability to reflect on their own role in outcomes, which means they will not be your ally when things go wrong. A VC who says "we struggled together, here's what I should have done differently" — that is someone you can call at 11pm.

**"What does your support look like beyond the capital? Give me three specific examples of how you've helped portfolio companies in the last year."**

Every VC deck says "we're value-added investors." Ask for evidence. Not the generic "we have a strong network." Ask for three specific instances: a hire they made possible, an enterprise contract they introduced, a strategic pivot they helped navigate. If they can't name three, they can't name any.

**"How many portfolio companies are you currently active with? How many board seats does this partner hold?"**

A partner with 8 active board seats will attend your board meeting once a quarter and respond to emails in 3 days. A partner with 4 active board seats will be meaningfully present. This is not judgment — it is reality. The attention you get is inversely proportional to the number of other companies competing for it.

**"What happens if we want to sell the company for ₹200 crore in year 3 but that doesn't return your fund?"**

This conversation is uncomfortable. Have it before you sign the term sheet, not in a board meeting three years later. You need to know: will this investor support a good-for-founders exit that doesn't work for their fund economics? The honest answer is: probably not. But knowing that going in changes how you think about the relationship.

**"May I call three of your portfolio founders, including one who had a difficult time?"**

If the investor hesitates, you have your answer.

### What to Ask Portfolio Founders

When you call portfolio founders for reference checks, resist the temptation to ask broad questions ("How is working with them?"). You will get vague positive answers. Ask specific questions that reveal behavior under pressure.

- "How was she during your worst six months?" Not "is she supportive" — during the specific moment when the company was in trouble, what did the investor do?
- "Did she make introductions she promised? Can you name one?" Test the specific claim they made to you about their network.
- "Did she respect your judgment when she disagreed with you?" What happened in a board meeting where the investor thought you were wrong and you held your position?
- "Would you raise from her again?" This is the question that compresses everything into one answer.

### The Indian VC Landscape — Honest Characterizations

The following is not rankings. It is an honest attempt to characterize how the major firms differ in style, so that you can choose the right partner for your specific situation.

**Sequoia India / Peak XV Partners:** The prestige brand in the Indian ecosystem. Deepest LP network. Best global connectivity. Known for being demanding and operationally engaged — they ask hard questions and expect prepared answers. Their portfolio brand opens doors: if Sequoia is on your cap table, global investors take your calls. They sit on boards and they use those seats. Appropriate for founders who want a highly engaged, highly demanding partner and are building for the largest possible outcome.

**Accel India:** One of the most founder-friendly reputations in the ecosystem, earned over fifteen years and across Flipkart, Freshworks, Swiggy, Urban Company, BrowserStack. Known for patience at early stages — they have held companies through multiple difficult years. Shekhar Kirani's relationship with Girish Mathrubootham at Freshworks is frequently cited as the model for what a VC-founder relationship should look like. Long-term relationship builders.

**Nexus Venture Partners:** Deep expertise in enterprise and SaaS. Their most distinctive strength: their US enterprise network, which is genuinely useful for Indian B2B companies selling to American customers. If you're building SaaS for US enterprises and need someone who knows Salesforce, ServiceNow, and Oracle buyers personally, Nexus is worth a serious conversation.

**Elevation Capital (formerly SAIF Partners):** Among the deepest roots in Indian consumer internet. Portfolio includes Meesho, Unacademy, ShareChat, OYO, Paytm. Patient capital — their fund structures allow for longer holding periods than typical VC funds, which is meaningful for Indian consumer companies that take longer to mature than US-equivalent companies.

**Blume Ventures:** The seed specialist. First institutional cheque for many Indian startups. Smaller fund size means more attention per portfolio company. Strong founder community — Blume's portfolio founders actively help each other. Appropriate if you're at the earliest stage and want an investor who will engage directly with the work, not just the metrics.

**Tiger Global:** Financial-first investor. Moves faster than any other firm — due diligence in days, not months. Writes large cheques at high valuations. Does not take board seats. Provides capital, not mentorship. This is not a criticism; it is a product feature. If you want a large amount of capital quickly from an investor who will not interfere in operations, Tiger Global is the most efficient path. If you want a board partner, Tiger Global is the wrong choice. Know which you need.

**SoftBank Vision Fund:** The most dramatic example of capital-as-strategy in startup history. SoftBank's approach under Masayoshi Son — deploy enormous capital to create market leaders, use the money to dominate before competitors can respond — produced some spectacular successes (ByteDance, Alibaba's early investment returned thousands of times). It also produced WeWork, OYO at extreme scale, and a series of valuations that were not connected to underlying business fundamentals.

OYO's story deserves specific treatment because it is the most instructive Indian example. Ritesh Agarwal's OYO went from a scrappy budget hotel aggregator to a global hotel chain in 5 years, with SoftBank writing cheques that totaled over $3 billion. The capital enabled a global expansion that covered 80 countries. It also enabled a growth-at-any-cost culture that outran OYO's operational infrastructure, resulted in hotel partner disputes across India, and forced a painful contraction that reduced headcount from over 25,000 employees to a few thousand. OYO survived — Ritesh Agarwal is still running the company, and it has reached profitability — but the path required a reset so severe that it constituted a different company than the one that received the SoftBank cheque.

Softbank gave WeWork $18.5 billion. What they actually gave Adam Neumann was permission to believe his own story without consequences. That is the most expensive thing a VC can give a founder.

## Part Five: The Board — What Nobody Tells You Before Your First Meeting

### What Actually Happens In There

The startup press describes board meetings as strategy sessions. Founders and investors gathering to set the direction of the company, debate ideas, emerge with clarity.

Here is what actually happens in board meetings.

The CFO presents the monthly financials. Someone asks why CAC went up 18% last quarter. Nobody has a clean answer. A VC partner asks whether the current Head of Sales is "the right person to lead through this next phase" — which is the board-speak for "I think you should fire your Sales VP." You defend your team. The VC says "just want to make sure we're asking hard questions." The meeting moves on. On the drive home, you wonder whether that was the beginning of a conversation you're not ready for.

That is the board meeting.

Not always. Sometimes they are genuinely strategic, genuinely energizing, a room full of people who care about the same outcome having a high-quality conversation. But that experience is earned — by founders who prepare meticulously, who surface problems before the board discovers them, who come with the question and the proposed answer. It does not happen automatically.

Brilliant founders manage their boards proactively. Not reactively. They send board materials five days before the meeting, not the night before. The board pack includes not just the financials and the metrics but the founder's honest assessment of what's working, what isn't, and what the company needs in the next 90 days. They surface difficult news in the board pack — not to panic investors, but because investors who learn bad news in a board meeting (rather than in advance) feel managed rather than included. And investors who feel managed become defensive.

The founders who have the best board relationships are also the founders who ask for help before they need it. "I'm evaluating two approaches to our enterprise sales motion. I'd value your thinking on this" — sent two weeks before the board meeting — is a very different signal than "we need to completely overhaul our sales strategy" announced in the meeting itself.

### The CEO Evaluation Conversation

Every VC-backed company goes through this conversation. Some go through it multiple times. The board evaluates whether the current CEO — often the founder — is the right leader for the next phase of growth.

This is not a betrayal. It is baked into the model.

The skills required to build a 5-person startup from zero are different from the skills required to manage a 500-person organization through a growth phase. The skills required to find product-market fit in a new market are different from the skills required to build the operational systems and management hierarchy of a mature business. Some founders grow. Some don't. The ones who don't are not failures as people; they may be spectacular at finding opportunities but less naturally equipped to manage the organizational complexity that their own success creates.

The founders who navigate this moment well are, almost universally, the ones who built around themselves before the board felt the need to have the conversation. They hired great people in roles they were weak in. They created systems that didn't require their constant presence. They developed as managers and leaders — by reading, by getting coaching, by recruiting executives who were better at operations than they were and learning from them.

Girish Mathrubootham at Freshworks is the most cited Indian example of a founder who made this transition consciously and well. As Freshworks scaled from 50 to 500 to 5,000 employees, Mathrubootham built an executive team around himself that compensated for his natural orientation toward product and customer and complemented it with operational rigor. He has spoken publicly about this process — the deliberate effort to become the CEO that a ₹5,000 crore company needs, not just the founder who built the first version. His relationship with his Accel board through that transition was, by his own account, one of genuine partnership: they challenged him, he challenged them, and both sides respected the process.

The founders who don't navigate this moment well are those who see every executive hire as a threat, every new system as a constraint, and every board conversation about organizational capability as a personal attack. They are not wrong to feel exposed — the conversation is threatening. But the response of closing down rather than opening up accelerates the very outcome they're trying to avoid.

The board will ask whether you're the right CEO. The founder who has already asked themselves that question, and has a thoughtful answer, is in a completely different position than the founder who is hearing it for the first time in a boardroom.

## Part Six: When VC Goes Wrong — The Three Failure Modes

### Failure Mode 1: The Company Outgrows Its Valuation

The math is simple and the consequences are brutal. You raise at a valuation of $1 billion based on growth projections that made sense when 2021's zero-interest-rate environment was inflating every number. Then growth slows. The projections don't materialize. Your revenue is real, your product is real, but you are not growing at the rate that your $1 billion valuation requires to be justified in your next round.

Now you face the down round.

A down round is not just the financial dilution — though that is real, and with anti-dilution provisions it can be extreme. It is the compound effect of that dilution on everything else. Employees whose options were granted at the inflated valuation price are now underwater: the options are worth nothing until the company exceeds its previous peak valuation. Recruiting becomes harder. Retention becomes harder. The founding team's stock, which was the primary currency of their own compensation, is worth less than it was.

BYJU's is the most complete and most devastating example available.

Byju Raveendran founded BYJU's in 2011 as a tutoring operation in Bengaluru. He had been tutoring CAT aspirants informally for years, and his students consistently outperformed. The transition to digital — first DVDs, then an app — happened gradually and then all at once. The app launched in 2015. By 2016, BYJU's was the most downloaded educational app in India. By 2019, it had raised $540 million from Sequoia Capital, Chan Zuckerberg Initiative, Tencent, General Atlantic, and Tiger Global. By 2021, its valuation had reached $22 billion, making it the world's most valuable edtech company.

The COVID-19 pandemic was the inflection point. Schools closed globally. Online learning was no longer optional. BYJU's user growth went vertical. The company raised again and again, each round larger than the last, each valuation higher. The money enabled a global acquisition strategy that was ambitious to the point of recklessness: White Hat Jr. for $300 million (a 20-month-old company that had never been audited). Toppr for $150 million. Aakash Educational Services for $950 million. Epic (a US children's reading app) for $500 million. Great Learning for $600 million. In roughly 18 months, BYJU's spent approximately $2.5 billion on acquisitions.

When the pandemic ended and schools reopened, the demand curve bent sharply downward. Organic growth slowed. The acquired companies turned out to have been valued at prices that assumed pandemic-level growth would continue indefinitely. The audited accounts for FY2021 — which were supposed to be filed in September 2021 — were not filed until September 2022. The auditor, Deloitte, resigned in June 2023. The CFO and two board members resigned the same month. 

By 2024, BYJU's had missed employee salaries multiple times, defaulted on loans, and faced NCLT (National Company Law Tribunal) insolvency proceedings. A company that was valued at $22 billion was being assessed for liquidation with assets insufficient to repay creditors.

Byju Raveendran is not a fraudster by nature. He was a founder who genuinely believed in what he was building — with a certainty that may have shaded into inability to see the distance between the story he was telling and the business he actually had. But the VC fundraising cycle enabled and amplified that inability. Each new round of capital, from increasingly prestigious investors at increasingly high valuations, functioned as institutional validation. Nobody in the room — not the investors, not the board, not the bankers — had sufficient incentive to say "this doesn't add up" at the moment when saying it would have mattered.

The VC fundraising cycle, when it works, amplifies genuine value creation. When it goes wrong, it amplifies the story at the expense of the substance. And by the time the gap between story and substance becomes undeniable, the amplification has made the consequences catastrophic.

### Failure Mode 2: The Cap Table Becomes a Cage

This failure mode is quieter than BYJU's but more common. A company raises multiple rounds from multiple investors. Each investor has liquidation preferences, pro-rata rights, information rights, consent rights. By round C or D, the cap table is a complex organism of competing interests.

An acquisition offer arrives. For the founders and employees, it is a genuinely good outcome. For some investors, it is below their liquidation preference threshold — they would receive less than their original investment. For other investors, the offer is fine, but they have pro-rata rights in the next round that they want to preserve, and selling now forfeits that. For yet another investor, the acquiring company is a competitor of one of their other portfolio companies.

The deal falls apart. Not because anyone is acting in bad faith. Because the legal structure of the cap table makes alignment impossible.

This scenario plays out more frequently than the startup media reports, because failed acquisitions are announced only when the deal has publicly leaked. The far more common version is the deal that never makes it to public announcement because the founding team already knows they cannot get the cap table to agree.

The founders who avoid this trap are those who, from the first round, think carefully about cap table cleanliness: how many investors are on the table, what the relative positions of their preferences are, and whether the investor group can make collective decisions efficiently. A cap table with five investors and clean documentation is more valuable than a cap table with twelve investors and complex cross-provisions. Not because more investors is bad, but because each investor is a stakeholder with independent rights, and assembling those rights into a coherent exit decision becomes exponentially harder with each addition.

### Failure Mode 3: The Investor Relationship Becomes Adversarial

The BharatPe story is the most dramatic and the most public example of what happens when a founder-investor relationship deteriorates into open conflict.

Ashneer Grover co-founded BharatPe in 2018 with Shashvat Nakrani. The company built a QR code-based merchant payment solution that rode India's UPI wave with genuine intelligence: one QR code, all payment apps. BharatPe grew rapidly. By 2021, it had raised over $700 million from Sequoia, Ribbit Capital, Coatue, Tiger Global, and others, reaching a valuation of $2.85 billion.

Then a series of events unspooled publicly.

In January 2022, an audio recording was leaked — allegedly of Grover verbally abusing a Kotak Mahindra Bank employee over an investment allocation process — and became a national media story. Within weeks, the board commissioned an internal investigation into alleged financial irregularities involving Grover and his wife, Madhuri. The investigation, conducted by Alvarez & Marsal, reportedly found evidence of misappropriation of funds and inflated vendor payments. In February 2022, Grover resigned as Managing Director. In March, he was asked to resign from the board. Legal proceedings followed on multiple fronts — BharatPe against Grover, Grover against BharatPe.

What is instructive here is not who was right and who was wrong — that question is before courts and may not be resolved for years. What is instructive is the structural breakdown. A founder-investor relationship contains enormous concentrations of power. When trust collapses — regardless of the cause — the investor holds more structural power than the founder. The board can commission investigations, can control communication with employees, can limit the founder's access to company systems. A founder who goes into battle with their investors on their own cap table is fighting on the investors' home ground.

The lesson is not to avoid VCs. The lesson is that the relationship must be built on transparency from the beginning, and that founders who behave consistently — in public and in private, in good months and in bad — are least vulnerable to this failure mode. The moment a founder starts managing information rather than sharing it, the relationship has begun a journey that is very hard to reverse.

## Part Seven: The Sequoia Exception — What a Great VC Partnership Looks Like

### The Anatomy of Partnership

It is too easy — and too misleading — to write about VC exclusively in terms of failure modes. The failure modes exist and they are common. But the reason venture capital persists as an industry, and the reason the best founders in the world actively seek VC backing, is that when the relationship works it creates outcomes that no other funding mechanism can produce.

The Zomato-Sequoia partnership, across a decade, is the closest Indian analog to the kind of legendary VC-founder story that defines what venture capital is supposed to be.

Deepinder Goyal launched Foodiebay in 2008 as a digitized restaurant menu directory — he and Pankaj Chaddah photographed physical menus and made them searchable online. The problem they were solving was modest: it was annoying to call restaurants to ask what they served. Sequoia India backed them early, when the company was still called Foodiebay and the ambition was not yet fully formed. The initial investment was small. The belief was in the founders.

What followed was a decade of pivots, each enabled by the previous one. Menu directory became restaurant ratings platform. Ratings platform became food ordering platform. Food ordering became food delivery — at which point the business became operationally and capital-intensive in ways that a directory never was. Delivery meant logistics, dark kitchens, hyperlocal demand forecasting, a fleet of delivery partners, and a burn rate that would have been inconceivable to the founders who photographed menus in 2008.

Sequoia's partnership through this journey involved genuine challenge. There were board disagreements. There were strategic debates about which markets to enter, how quickly to expand, how to think about profitability. Deepinder Goyal has described moments of genuine tension with his board — and described the resolution of that tension through honest conversation rather than capitulation on either side.

The Zomato IPO in July 2021 — the first major Indian startup IPO, the one that opened the floodgates for Nykaa, Paytm, CarTrade, and a dozen others — was a defining moment for the Indian startup ecosystem. Sequoia's stake, earned through a decade of patient and engaged partnership, produced a return that justified everything.

But the return is not what makes this story instructive. What makes it instructive is the anatomy of how the partnership worked: Sequoia challenged Goyal's assumptions without undermining his authority. They made introductions — to Chinese food delivery players, to global investors — that Goyal couldn't have made himself. They were a steadying force during crisis (there were many: food safety scandals, COVID-19, competition from Swiggy, regulatory challenges). They were honest about their fund economics and exit expectations from the beginning.

And Goyal, for his part, was transparent about problems before they became crises. He briefed his board proactively. He asked for help in the areas where he was uncertain. He maintained his authority on product and strategic direction while building a world-class operations team around himself.

This is what a great VC partnership looks like. Not a guarantee — a posture. A set of behaviors, on both sides, that creates the conditions under which the relationship can withstand years of uncertainty and emerge intact.

### The Checklist for a Partnership, Not a Transaction

When a term sheet arrives, the instinct is to evaluate the number. The valuation, the dilution, the money. These matter. But the most important due diligence you will do is not financial. It is relational.

Does this person make you think more clearly, or do they overwhelm you with their certainty? Do they ask questions that reveal genuine understanding of your business, or do they apply frameworks that fit most businesses and call it insight? When they disagree with you, do they disagree with your conclusion and explain why, or do they disagree with your authority to have reached it?

The VC who challenges your assumptions without undermining your confidence is the VC worth taking money from. Everything else — the brand, the portfolio, the press release — is secondary.

## The Founder's Decision Framework: Should You Take VC At All?

### The Central Question

Every founder who reaches the scale where VC becomes possible should ask this question with full seriousness: is this the right tool for the company I want to build?

Not the company the market expects you to build. Not the company that makes the best TechCrunch headline. The company you actually want to build — at the scale you want to build it, at the pace you can sustain, for the outcome that would constitute success for you personally.

VC is appropriate under specific conditions.

**Take VC if:**

Your market is winner-take-all or winner-take-most, and speed is the primary competitive advantage. If the company that gets to scale first captures the network effects that make the market defensible, then capital is the competitive weapon and VC is how you acquire it. Food delivery. Ride-hailing. Social platforms. Job marketplaces. In these markets, the race to liquidity is the business.

Your product requires significant upfront capital before the revenue arrives. Hardware. Biotech. Deep tech. Marketplaces that require both buyer and seller acquisition simultaneously. These categories have capital requirements that cannot be bootstrapped at meaningful scale.

You are genuinely building for a $1 billion-plus outcome and are willing to accept the governance structure and exit pressure that entails. This is not a casual aspiration. It means accepting board oversight, operating under the timeline of a VC fund's life, and potentially giving up the option to sell for a "good" number that doesn't meet your investors' return expectations.

Your specific VC adds genuine non-capital value that materially changes your odds. The introductions they make, the talent they help recruit, the strategic perspective they bring — if these are real and verifiable (see: reference check questions, above) — can be worth the dilution even in markets where bootstrapping is theoretically possible.

**Don't take VC if:**

Your business can reach profitability and meaningful scale without it. This is the Zerodha case — and it deserves its full treatment here.

Nithin Kamath founded Zerodha in 2010 with his brother Nikhil and co-founder Kailash Nadh. The business model was radical for Indian retail broking: flat fee per order, no percentage commissions. The incumbents — ICICI Direct, HDFC Securities, Kotak Securities — charged 0.5% of trade value. Zerodha charged ₹20 per order, regardless of size. For a high-frequency trader executing large orders, the savings were enormous.

The business grew. Slowly at first, then with increasing velocity as discount broking went mainstream. By 2020, Zerodha was handling over 15% of India's retail equity trading volume. By 2022, it was the most profitable startup in India — profitable margins that most VC-backed companies dream about, achieved without raising a single rupee from external investors. Zerodha's valuation is estimated at over $3 billion. Nithin Kamath owns the majority of it. He has never answered to a board he didn't control. He has never accepted a liquidation preference that could override his decision-making. He has never had to justify a strategy to investors whose fund economics required a $5 billion exit.

Vijay Shekhar Sharma at Paytm took the opposite path. Paytm raised from Alibaba's Ant Financial, from Berkshire Hathaway (Warren Buffett's firm — a ₹2,179 crore investment), from SoftBank, from dozens of others. The capital enabled an expansion into payments, banking, insurance, wealth management, and commerce that no bootstrapped company could have executed at the same pace. Paytm went public in November 2021 at a ₹1.34 lakh crore valuation — the largest Indian IPO ever at that point.

Within 12 months, Paytm had lost approximately 75% of its market capitalization. In early 2024, the Reserve Bank of India imposed severe restrictions on Paytm Payments Bank's operations, further constraining the business. The stock, which had listed at ₹1,955, was trading below ₹400.

Both founders — Nithin Kamath and Vijay Shekhar Sharma — built large, legitimate businesses. Both are genuine entrepreneurial successes by any meaningful standard. The difference is not in the quality of their execution or their intelligence. The difference is in the tool they used and the obligations those tools created.

Kamath is a billionaire who owns most of his company and runs it with complete independence. Sharma is a billionaire who owns a minority of a publicly traded company whose regulatory troubles have made its recovery uncertain.

Which path is better? That is the wrong question. Which path was right for each founder, given the specific market they were operating in and the specific kind of company they wanted to build? That is the right question. And it is the question you need to answer — honestly, specifically, without the influence of what everyone else in your ecosystem is doing — before you begin the VC fundraising process.

Don't take VC if you are not building for a $1 billion-plus exit and would be content with a smaller, profitable, independently valuable business. The VC-founder relationship is a contract that has no comfortable middle outcome. Either you build big enough to satisfy the power law, or you disappoint your investors. The comfortable $50 million revenue business with healthy margins and a happy team — which is a genuinely excellent outcome for a founder — is a disappointment for a VC fund.

Don't take VC if you prize autonomy over speed. The board relationship is real. The governance obligations are real. The reporting requirements are real. These are not minor administrative inconveniences. They are structural features of the relationship that will cost you time, energy, and decision-making freedom every week for the life of the company. If you are the kind of founder who needs to be able to make decisions at 9pm and implement them by 9am without running it past a board, VC is a very uncomfortable fit.

Don't take VC if your market is large but not winner-take-all — if there is room for multiple profitable players and you can be one without needing to race to dominate. In a market with this structure, bootstrapping to profitability and then growing at your own pace may produce a better outcome for you than racing for dominance with VC capital and arriving at a structure that constrains your eventual choices.

## The One Thing to Remember

> **VC is not a validation. It is a contract.**
>
> When you take venture capital, you are agreeing to build a specific kind of company — large, fast, exit-oriented — on a specific timeline, under specific oversight, with specific governance obligations that you cannot unilaterally remove. You are agreeing that the board will have a voice in decisions you currently make alone. You are agreeing that your exit will happen on a timeline influenced by someone else's fund economics. You are agreeing that the definition of success is not "great company that serves customers well and pays its team fairly" — it is "great company that reaches a valuation at which the VC's fund can generate the returns their LPs require."
>
> None of this is bad. All of it is real. The founders who thrive in the VC structure are not the ones who got the most prestigious term sheet. They are the ones who understood the contract they were signing — every clause, every implication, every power dynamic — and then built a company worthy of it. Eyes open. From day one. No surprises.

## How to Use This Tomorrow — 6 Concrete Steps

**Step 1: Map the VC landscape for your specific domain.**

Go to Tracxn, Crunchbase, or the individual firm websites of Sequoia India, Accel India, Nexus, Elevation, Blume, Matrix Partners India, and Lightspeed India. Filter by the sector you're operating in. Identify which firms have made investments in companies that look like yours — similar stage, similar business model, similar market. Those are your first 10 targets. Firms that have no portfolio in your sector are lower-priority; they'll spend your meetings learning the sector rather than evaluating you.

**Step 2: Before every VC meeting, do one hour of preparation.**

Read the firm's last 5 public investment announcements. What sectors are they doubling down on? What portfolio companies did they recently announce? What is the thesis they're expressing publicly right now? Walk into the meeting knowing what the VC's fund thesis is and why your company fits it. VCs talk to hundreds of founders a year. The ones who demonstrate genuine knowledge of the firm's portfolio and thesis immediately differentiate themselves.

**Step 3: Build your VC reference list before you need it.**

For the three VCs you're most interested in raising from, identify two portfolio founders each that you can approach for reference checks. This means six founders. LinkedIn is adequate for the introduction, but a mutual connection is better. Reach out now — before you're in a fundraise process — with genuine curiosity about their experience. Build the relationship. When you're in due diligence and the pressure is on, you'll have real reference points, not cold calls.

**Step 4: Model your fully diluted cap table today.**

Before you walk into any VC conversation, know exactly what you own, what your co-founders own, what the ESOP pool is (granted, outstanding, and unissued), and what any outstanding convertible instruments (SAFEs, convertible notes, warrants) will convert to at various valuations. Build a simple spreadsheet. At a ₹80 crore pre-money valuation with a 15% new ESOP pool expansion and a 20% investment, what do you own? At ₹120 crore? At ₹60 crore? Run these numbers before you're in the room. Never let the VC's model be more detailed than yours.

**Step 5: Write your non-negotiables before the process begins.**

Before you receive a term sheet, write down — literally, in a document — the three things you will not concede. Board composition (minimum: one founder seat with specific protections around CEO removal threshold). ESOP pool expansion (maximum percentage, and structured so it dilutes all shareholders pro-rata, not founders alone). Liquidation preference structure (1x non-participating; no participating preference). Know your lines before you're in a room being pressured to sign. Pressure in the moment of a term sheet negotiation is real. The only defense is knowing in advance what you will and will not accept.

**Step 6: Run the Decision Framework honestly.**

Before you begin the fundraising process, score your company on these questions with a yes or no:
- Is my market winner-take-all, and is speed the primary competitive advantage?
- Does my product require capital before revenue arrives?
- Am I genuinely building for a $1B-plus outcome, and can I articulate why that is achievable?
- Does the specific VC I'm targeting add verifiable non-capital value?
- Am I comfortable with board oversight, governance obligations, and the loss of unilateral decision-making authority?

If you answer no to more than two of these, examine bootstrapping, angel funding, or revenue-based financing before you pursue VC. The right tool matters as much as the quality of your execution.

## Exercises

### Exercise 1: The Power Law Math

A VC fund has raised ₹500 crore. It makes 20 investments, averaging ₹25 crore each. The fund target is a 3x return for LPs: ₹1,500 crore total.

**Questions:**
1. If the fund has 3 investments that become meaningful winners and 17 that return 0 (assume no middle outcomes), what must each of the 3 winners return in rupees for the fund to hit 3x?
2. If one investment returns 50x on a ₹25 crore investment, what is the rupee return? Does this single investment return the entire fund?
3. Research: in your specific sector (SaaS, consumer, fintech, edtech — choose one), how many Indian startups have reached exits above ₹750 crore (approximately the "fund-returning" threshold in this scenario) in the last 10 years? What does this tell you about the probability that your company becomes a fund-returner?

*The point:* Understanding the power law tells you whether your company's realistic outcome aligns with what a VC fund needs from you.

### Exercise 2: The Term Sheet Audit

Download a sample Indian startup term sheet. (The India Law Offices website, iSPIRT's resources, and Traxcn's blog have publicly available samples. Alternatively, search "Indian startup term sheet template 2024.")

Read the entire document and mark every clause that transfers any power, right, information, or decision from founder to investor. Count them.

**Questions:**
1. How many clauses transfer power from founder to investor?
2. Identify the five clauses that would most significantly affect you if the company underperforms.
3. Identify the five clauses that would most significantly affect you if the company exceeds all projections but investors want to wait for a larger exit.
4. Which clauses can you negotiate? (Research which provisions are "market standard" — i.e., every investor will insist on them — and which are variable.)

*The point:* You cannot negotiate a term sheet you haven't read, and you cannot read a term sheet you don't understand.

### Exercise 3: The Liquidation Preference Calculator

A company sells for ₹150 crore. Three groups of shareholders:

- **Investor A**: Invested ₹30 crore for 25% of the company. 1x non-participating liquidation preference.
- **Investor B**: Invested ₹50 crore for 30% of the company. 2x participating liquidation preference.
- **Founders + ESOP pool**: Own 45% of the company.

**Step-by-step calculation:**
1. Apply Investor B's 2x participating preference first. How much does Investor B receive from their preference?
2. What amount remains?
3. Apply Investor A's preference or pro-rata (whichever is higher for Investor A). How much does Investor A receive?
4. What amount remains?
5. Apply Investor B's participation in the remaining amount. (Investor B owns 30% of the fully diluted cap table and participates pro-rata in what's left after preferences.)
6. What do founders and ESOP holders receive?

**Bonus:** Redo the calculation assuming Investor B's preference is 1x non-participating instead of 2x participating. How much does the change in Investor B's terms affect what founders receive?

*The point:* Liquidation preferences are not abstract. Run the numbers before you sign.

### Exercise 4: The Board Composition War Game

You are offered the following board structure as part of a Series A term sheet: 2 investor seats (held by the lead VC), 1 founder seat (you), 1 independent director (to be mutually agreed).

Map every scenario in which you, as sole founder on the board, lose a vote:
1. The VC wants to replace you as CEO. How does the vote go if the independent director sides with the VC?
2. An acquisition offer arrives for ₹100 crore. The VC believes the company is worth more and wants to wait. You want to accept. How does the vote go?
3. The VC wants to hire a COO over your objection. Does the board vote on this, or is it the CEO's decision?

Then, negotiate in writing: what specific board composition changes would you ask for, and what is your fallback position if the investor refuses? Consider:
- A 5-person board: 1 founder, 1 investor, 1 independent mutually agreed, 1 independent founder-appointed, 1 independent investor-appointed.
- Specific protections: CEO removal requires 75% board vote, not simple majority.
- Independent director qualification: no current investor or advisor to any portfolio company.

*The point:* Board composition is the most negotiable and most important governance term. War-gaming it before you're in the room makes you dramatically more effective in the negotiation.

### Exercise 5: The VC Reference Check

Write the five questions you would ask a portfolio founder when doing a reference check on a VC. Write them specifically enough that a vague or positive answer would still be informative (the absence of a concrete answer is itself data).

Then: for each question, write what answer would make you comfortable proceeding, and what answer would make you walk away.

Example format:

*Question:* "Tell me about a time in the last two years when you and [VC] disagreed on a significant decision. What happened?"

*Answer that would make me comfortable:* A specific example, with a described resolution process, where the founder felt heard even if the decision went the VC's way. Indication that the VC engaged substantively with the founder's reasoning.

*Answer that would make me walk away:* "We've never really disagreed." (Indicates either an unusually passive founder or a board environment where founders don't feel safe expressing disagreement.) Or: "She just overrode me — it was her money so I went with it." (Indicates a power dynamic where founder voice is not genuinely respected.)

Develop five such questions with specific positive and negative response criteria. This is your reference check guide.

## Further Reading

**Venture Deals: Be Smarter Than Your Lawyer and Venture Capitalist** — Brad Feld and Jason Mendelson

The definitive guide to every clause in a VC term sheet. Feld co-founded Foundry Group and has backed hundreds of companies. Mendelson is a VC attorney with decades of experience on both sides of term sheet negotiations. Read this before you receive your first term sheet. Read it again after you receive it. Keep it on your desk during the negotiation. No other book demystifies the mechanics of VC financing with this combination of accuracy and accessibility. The examples are US-centric, but the clauses are universal — and the Indian market follows the same templates with minor adaptations.

**The Hard Thing About Hard Things: Building a Business When There Are No Easy Answers** — Ben Horowitz

The most honest account of what the VC-backed founder experience actually feels like from the inside. Horowitz co-founded Opsware, raised venture capital, managed through near-bankruptcy, navigated board conflicts, and eventually sold to HP for $1.65 billion. His account of the conversations with his board, the CEO evaluation moments, the 3am spreadsheet calculations, the conversations with his wife — these are the emotional anchors that most business books carefully avoid. Read it not to learn what to do but to understand what the experience actually feels like before you're in it.

**Girish Mathrubootham's SaaStr Annual Talks** (YouTube, multiple years)

The most articulate public account available of an Indian founder's relationship with his VC board through a decade of growth. Mathrubootham speaks about Freshworks's journey from the Chennai startup to NASDAQ listing with unusual candor: the rejection from eleven VCs before Accel, the board dynamics during the hypergrowth years, his evolution as a CEO, the specific things he did to manage his board proactively and to build trust with Shekhar Kirani over time. These talks are free, available on YouTube, and more practically useful than most books. Watch at least the SaaStr 2018, 2019, and 2022 editions.

**Shailendra Singh's Interviews on the Sequoia India Investment Thesis** (multiple platforms, 2019–2024)

Singh, former Managing Director of Sequoia India (now Peak XV Partners), has given extensive public interviews about how Sequoia India thinks about investments, what they look for in founders, how they approach board dynamics, and how the Indian startup ecosystem has evolved over the fifteen years he spent at the center of it. Listening to a top-tier investor explain their decision-making framework in detail — including the frameworks they use to evaluate whether a founder is "the right CEO for this stage" — is the most useful preparation for a VC conversation a founder can do.

**Entries on the SEBI DRHP filings of Zomato, Nykaa, Freshworks, and Paytm** (publicly available on SEBI and DRHP websites)

These are the documents that companies must file before going public, and they contain detailed cap table histories, investor names and stakes at each round, board composition history, and financial disclosures going back to the company's founding. Reading the Zomato DRHP, in particular, gives you a real-world example of what a decade of VC fundraising looks like on paper — who invested at what valuation, how the cap table evolved, how board seats were structured at each round. This is the closest thing to a free masterclass in VC deal structure that exists in the Indian market.

## Closing Transition: What Comes After the Startup Stage

Venture capital makes a specific bet: you might be extraordinary. The market you're targeting might be larger than anyone currently believes. The team you've assembled might be capable of building something that has never been built before. The VC is not buying a proven business. They are buying a probability distribution — the small but non-zero chance that you are Sachin and Binny Bansal running Flipkart out of that Bengaluru apartment in 2010, and the capital they provide is the accelerant that helps you prove it.

But there is another category of institutional capital that enters later — not in the apartment, not in the probability distribution, but after the product works, after the market has been proven, after the company has demonstrated that it can scale. This capital is not making bets on unproven ideas. It is buying stakes in proven businesses, at valuations grounded in real financial performance, with a different set of expectations, a different governance model, and a fundamentally different power dynamic.

Private equity. Growth equity. The professional money that gets serious.

PE and growth equity investors are not venture capitalists. They operate at higher valuations, with more rigorous financial scrutiny, with different instruments (often including leverage), and with expectations about management and operations that can be genuinely shocking to founders who have only ever known the VC world. The board dynamics are different. The exit horizon is different. The definition of "value creation" is different.

For founders who have reached product-market fit, proven scalability, and are ready for a different kind of partnership — or for founders who built outside the VC ecosystem and are now attracting institutional attention — the question of PE and growth equity is not theoretical. It is the next conversation.

Chapter 9 is that conversation.
