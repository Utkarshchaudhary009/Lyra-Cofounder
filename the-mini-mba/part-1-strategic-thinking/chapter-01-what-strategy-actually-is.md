# Chapter 1: What Strategy Actually Is

In the autumn of 1975, a twenty-four-year-old electrical engineer named Steve Sasson sat in a basement laboratory in Rochester, New York, staring at a contraption roughly the size of a toaster. It weighed eight pounds, used a Fairchild CCD image sensor, a lens borrowed from a Super 8 movie camera, and a tangle of analogue and digital circuits that he had assembled largely from spare parts. When he pressed the shutter, it took 23 seconds to capture a single image — a black-and-white portrait of a lab technician named Joy Marshall — and another 30 seconds to play it back on a television screen. The picture was grainy, barely recognizable, the visual equivalent of a rough draft.

But what Sasson had built was the world's first digital camera. And he worked for the most powerful photography company on earth: Eastman Kodak.

Sasson did what any ambitious young engineer would do. He cleaned up his prototype, wrote his report, and presented his invention to Kodak's management team. He demonstrated how the device worked, explained its potential, and waited for their reaction.

Their response — and this is verified, documented history — was essentially five words:

*"That's cute. Don't tell anyone."*

Not five words of dismissal from some mid-level bureaucrat who didn't understand technology. Five words from executives who understood perfectly well what they were looking at. They knew it was real. They knew it worked. They knew that someday, digital technology would change photography. Their problem was that they also knew something else: Kodak's film business was generating extraordinary margins. In 1976 — just one year after Sasson's invention — Kodak controlled approximately 90 percent of film sales in the United States and 85 percent of camera sales. The company's gross margins on film were roughly 70 percent. Film was one of the most profitable consumer products ever devised: a consumable product that people bought again and again every time they wanted to capture a memory, developed in labs that Kodak also owned, printed on paper that Kodak also sold.

Digital photography, if it ever took off, would destroy all of that. Every photograph taken digitally was a roll of film not sold, a development session not performed, a box of paper not needed. So Kodak's executives did what rational managers protecting a wildly profitable business often do: they kept the digital camera in a vault, funded just enough research to maintain the appearance of innovation, and went back to selling film.

This is not a story about a company that failed because it didn't see the future coming. Kodak saw it perfectly. The company even commissioned an internal study in 1981 that concluded digital photography would eventually kill the film business — and estimated the company had about ten years before the transition would become serious.

Ten years. A decade of runway. And Kodak used almost none of it.

Through the 1980s and into the 1990s, digital photography crept forward while Kodak kept doubling down on film, launching ever-more-elaborate chemical processing systems and premium film products. The company released its own digital cameras in the 1990s, but treated them as experiments rather than commitments — afraid, above all, of cannibalizing the business that paid everyone's salaries. The film business remained profitable enough, for long enough, that the urgency to change never felt quite real. The frog sat in the gradually warming water, and the water felt fine.

Then, in the early 2000s, consumer digital cameras went mainstream. Film sales, which had peaked around 1999, fell off a cliff. Not gradually — precipitously. Within a decade, a product that had been one of the most reliably profitable in American business history was essentially gone. Kodak filed for bankruptcy in January 2012, a company that had once employed more than 60,000 people in Rochester alone, that had been a pillar of the Dow Jones Industrial Average, that had invented the very technology that destroyed it.

---

Now let's go to Tokyo, to the headquarters of a company that was facing the exact same problem at the exact same time.

Fujifilm, Kodak's great rival, held about a quarter of the global photographic film market by the late 1990s. It understood digital photography just as well as Kodak did. When Shigetaka Komori became president of Fujifilm in 2000, he looked at the global trends and reached a conclusion that sounds obvious only in retrospect: digital photography was going to kill the film business, the film business generated approximately two-thirds of the company's profits, and therefore Fujifilm as currently constituted was going to die.

Komori did not respond to this conclusion by hoping things would turn out differently, or by treating it as a long-term problem that future management could address. He responded to it as an immediate existential crisis — which is exactly what it was. He began what he would later call a "Second Foundation" of Fujifilm: a systematic, ruthless process of diagnosing what the company actually was at its deepest level, and then redirecting those capabilities toward places where they could create new value.

The insight that made Fujifilm's transformation possible was deceptively simple: the company was not really in the film business. It was in the business of manipulating molecules at the nano-scale, stabilizing chemical compounds against light and oxidation, and mass-producing precision optical products. Those capabilities, it turned out, had applications far beyond photography.

Consider collagen. Photographic film is made partly from collagen — a protein that gives the emulsion its structure. Fujifilm had spent decades studying how collagen behaves, how it degrades, and how to stabilize it. Guess what else is largely made of collagen? Human skin. The company's deep expertise in collagen chemistry was directly applicable to anti-aging skincare.

Consider antioxidants. Film degrades when exposed to oxygen — the same process that causes human skin to age. Fujifilm's chemists had developed sophisticated techniques for preventing oxidation in photographic compounds. Those same techniques were directly applicable to cosmetics that prevented skin aging.

Consider nano-dispersion — the technique Fujifilm used to pack light-sensitive molecules into film emulsions with extraordinary precision. The same technology could be used to create cosmetic formulations where active ingredients penetrate the skin more effectively.

In 2006, Fujifilm entered the cosmetics market. In 2007, it launched **Astalift**, a premium anti-aging skincare line built around astaxanthin — a powerful antioxidant — delivered using the company's proprietary nano-dispersion technology. The line was a genuine scientific product, not a marketing exercise, because Fujifilm actually understood the chemistry of skin aging at a level that most cosmetics companies could not match.

But skincare was only one front. Komori launched an equally aggressive push into medical imaging — CT scanners, digital X-ray systems, endoscopes — where Fujifilm's optical and imaging expertise was directly applicable. He pushed into advanced materials for flat-panel displays, pharmaceutical ingredients, and regenerative medicine. He launched the VISION75 restructuring plan in 2004, cutting tens of thousands of jobs and closing dozens of film manufacturing plants, while simultaneously investing billions in new businesses.

By the time the full story was told, the contrast with Kodak was staggering. Fujifilm survived. Not just survived — thrived. Today, healthcare is the company's largest revenue segment. The Astalift skincare line sells across Asia, Europe, and North America. The company continues to grow.

Two companies. Same industry. Same disruption. Same information. Completely opposite outcomes.

This is not a technology story. It is not even an innovation story. It is a **strategy** story.

And it raises a question that most businesspeople, most MBA students, and most executives get wrong: what exactly is strategy, and how do you do it?

## What Strategy Is (And What It Isn't)

Ask ten senior executives what their company's strategy is, and most will give you one of three answers. The first type will recite a vision statement — "We aim to be the world's leading provider of customer-centric solutions." The second type will list a set of goals — "We want to grow revenue by 20 percent, enter two new markets, and launch three new products." The third type will describe an operational plan — "We're investing in our technology platform, improving our customer service, and strengthening our supply chain."

None of those things is a strategy.

A vision statement describes where you want to end up, but says nothing about how. Goals describe what you want to achieve, but not the choices you'll make to get there. An operational plan describes what you'll do, but not why those specific things rather than a hundred other things you could equally well do.

Strategy is something more specific, more demanding, and more useful than any of those things. Michael Porter, who has thought more rigorously about strategy than almost anyone alive, defines it with two words that carry enormous weight: **differentiation** and **trade-offs**.

Strategy is a set of choices about where to compete, how to compete, and — most importantly — what **not** to compete on. It is about being deliberately different from your competitors in ways that create value for a specific set of customers, in ways that your competitors cannot easily replicate, because replicating the key choices would require them to give up their own.

Let's unpack that definition carefully, because every word matters.

**"A set of choices"** — Strategy is not one choice. It is a system of mutually reinforcing choices. A single choice is just a decision. Strategy is what happens when many decisions line up behind a coherent logic. We'll see this most clearly in the Southwest Airlines case study later in this chapter.

**"Where to compete"** — Which customers? Which markets? Which segments? Which geographies? Strategy begins with the recognition that you cannot serve everyone equally well. IKEA has chosen to serve budget-conscious, design-aware, young urban households who don't mind assembling their own furniture. That choice excludes a lot of people — and that exclusion is not a failure of strategy. It is the strategy.

**"How to compete"** — What is your basis for winning? Lower cost? Superior product? Faster delivery? Deeper expertise? Stronger brand? Every company needs a reason for customers to choose it over alternatives, and that reason must be genuine and defensible.

**"What not to compete on"** — This is the hardest part, and it is where most strategies fail. Every YES implies a NO. If you're going to serve budget-conscious customers, you probably can't also offer white-glove luxury service. If you're going to operate with the lowest possible costs, you probably can't also invest heavily in cutting-edge R&D. Strategy is not about doing everything well. It is about doing specific things brilliantly and consciously accepting that certain other things will be done poorly or not at all.

## The Critical Distinction: Strategy vs. Operational Effectiveness

In 1996, Michael Porter published an essay in the *Harvard Business Review* called "What Is Strategy?" that remains one of the most important pieces of business writing of the twentieth century. Its core argument was a distinction that seems simple but has enormous implications: the difference between **operational effectiveness** and **strategy**.

**Operational effectiveness** means doing the same things your competitors do, but doing them better. You're faster, cheaper, more reliable, higher quality, or some combination of all four. Throughout the 1980s and into the 1990s, Japanese manufacturers achieved breathtaking levels of operational effectiveness — their factories were cleaner, their quality control was more rigorous, their inventory management was more precise, and their cycle times were shorter. Toyota's production system, which was being studied and emulated by manufacturers around the world, was a masterpiece of operational effectiveness.

Here is the crucial insight: operational effectiveness is necessary, but it is not strategy. When everyone in your industry improves their operational effectiveness, you get what Porter calls a "productivity frontier" — an outer limit of performance that keeps advancing as all competitors get better. The problem is that when all competitors are pursuing operational effectiveness, competition converges. Everyone ends up doing roughly the same things in roughly similar ways, just with varying levels of efficiency. Margins get compressed. Prices fall. The gains from being slightly more efficient get competed away.

You can see this dynamic playing out today in almost every commodity industry — airlines, banking, retail, logistics, professional services. Enormous effort goes into being slightly more efficient, slightly cheaper, slightly faster. But differentiation is minimal, because everyone is essentially running the same race.

**Strategy**, by contrast, means doing *different things* — or doing things *differently* in ways that serve a distinct set of customers better than alternatives. Strategy is not about running the same race faster. It is about choosing to run a different race.

The distinction matters enormously when we look at Kodak. Kodak's response to the digital threat, for most of the 1990s, was to improve its operational effectiveness — better film, better processing, better customer experience at photo labs. They kept getting better at a game that was becoming irrelevant. Meanwhile, Fujifilm asked a different question: given that the game is changing, what different game can we play?

| | Operational Effectiveness | Strategy |
|---|---|---|
| **Question asked** | "How do we do this better?" | "What should we be doing?" |
| **Focus** | Same activities as competitors, done better | Different activities, or different configuration |
| **Competitive advantage** | Temporary — can be copied | Durable — embedded in a system |
| **Risk** | Margin compression as all competitors improve | Risk of wrong choices; requires commitment |
| **Example** | Toyota improving assembly line speed | Southwest using secondary airports |

One more thing worth noting: operational effectiveness and strategy are not enemies. You need both. A company with a brilliant strategy but terrible operations will still fail. But operational effectiveness without strategy produces a company that is perpetually running harder just to stay in place.

## Strategy Is Made of Trade-offs

Here is the sentence that I want you to tattoo on the inside of your eyelids, or at minimum write on a Post-it note above your desk: **every strategic YES implies a strategic NO.**

Trade-offs are not a regrettable feature of strategy. They are the *mechanism* of strategy. They are what makes a strategic position defensible. If doing something didn't require you to give something else up, then every competitor could do the same thing, and you'd be back to operational effectiveness — a race with no permanent winners.

Think about it this way. Suppose a new restaurant opens in your neighborhood and its strategy is to serve the best food at the fastest speed for the lowest price. That is not a strategy. That is wishful thinking. You cannot simultaneously optimize for best quality, fastest speed, and lowest price, because these objectives pull against each other. The best ingredients cost more money. High-quality preparation takes more time. Lowest price means thin margins, which means you can't afford the best ingredients or the most skilled chefs.

A real restaurant strategy might be: serve exceptional food to customers who are willing to wait and who can afford premium prices. Or: serve fast, reliable food to busy professionals who don't care about culinary ambition. Or: serve affordable, hearty meals to families who want a comfortable environment. Each of these is a real strategy, because each involves real trade-offs.

The trades-off point is not just abstract. Trade-offs are what make a position defensible against competitors. If Southwest Airlines decides not to serve meals on flights, it's not just saving a few dollars per passenger. It's simplifying its entire operation in ways that ripple across hundreds of other decisions. Competitors can try to copy one element of Southwest's approach — but to fully replicate it, they'd have to give up the hub-and-spoke model that their entire route network depends on, retrain all their pilots, alienate their premium business travellers, and rewrite their labour contracts. The trade-offs protect the position.

This is why Porter argues that the deepest threats to a strategic position don't come from competitors who do something radically new — they come from competitors who start copying elements of your strategy without making the corresponding trade-offs. They want the benefits without paying the costs. And they often succeed, for a while, but ultimately end up caught between two positions and good at neither.

## The Kernel of Good Strategy: Rumelt's Framework

In 2011, UCLA professor Richard Rumelt published *Good Strategy Bad Strategy*, possibly the most practically useful book on strategy written in the last twenty years. (It is listed in the Further Reading section at the end of this chapter, and I mean it when I say you should read it.) Rumelt's central contribution is the concept of the **kernel of strategy** — a three-part structure that distinguishes genuine strategy from the fluffy, wishful substitute that passes for strategy in most corporate conference rooms.

The three elements are:

### 1. Diagnosis

The diagnosis defines or explains the nature of the challenge. A good diagnosis simplifies the often overwhelmingly complex reality of the situation by identifying certain aspects of the situation as critical. A great diagnosis doesn't just describe symptoms — it identifies the underlying structure of the problem.

Rumelt's key insight here is that most organizations mistake their symptoms for their problem. A company that is losing market share might diagnose the problem as "our marketing isn't strong enough" or "our sales team isn't performing." But the real problem might be that their product is five years behind the technology curve, or that their cost structure makes them unable to compete on price in a commoditized market. The wrong diagnosis leads to the wrong guiding policy, which leads to wasted action.

A powerful diagnosis is often uncomfortable, because it names something that the organization would prefer to avoid. This is precisely why Kodak never made the diagnosis that mattered: *the film business will disappear, and we need to build something different before it does.* Fujifilm's Komori made that diagnosis explicitly and early. Everything else that Fujifilm did followed from that honest reckoning with reality.

### 2. Guiding Policy

The guiding policy is the overall approach chosen to cope with or overcome the obstacles identified in the diagnosis. It's not a detailed plan — it's a direction, a ruling principle that channels action without defining every step.

A good guiding policy does two things. First, it rules certain actions out — it narrows the field of possible responses and prevents the organization from chasing every option simultaneously. Second, it creates leverage — it concentrates force where it will have the most effect. A guiding policy is not "we will innovate" or "we will focus on customers." Those are platitudes. A guiding policy might be: "We will leverage our existing chemical expertise to enter industries adjacent to photography" — as Fujifilm did. Or: "We will serve the short-haul leisure traveller with the lowest possible fares, funded by an ultra-lean operating model" — as Southwest did.

### 3. Coherent Actions

Coherent actions are coordinated, complementary steps that implement the guiding policy. The word "coherent" is important. Rumelt means not just that the actions are consistent with the guiding policy, but that they reinforce each other — that the actions form a system where each one makes the others more powerful.

This is the part where most strategies fall apart. Organizations develop a brilliant diagnosis and a sensible guiding policy, and then execute a set of disconnected initiatives that don't add up to anything. Each initiative is defensible on its own, but there's no thread connecting them, no shared logic that makes the whole greater than the sum of its parts.

The three-part kernel gives us a simple but powerful test for any strategy. Ask:
- **Diagnosis**: Do we have a clear, honest understanding of the core challenge we're facing?
- **Guiding Policy**: Have we chosen a coherent approach that focuses our efforts and explicitly rules things out?
- **Coherent Actions**: Are our specific actions mutually reinforcing, or are they just a list of good ideas?

## The Anatomy of Bad Strategy

If good strategy has a recognizable structure, so does bad strategy. And bad strategy is far more common. Rumelt identifies four hallmarks of bad strategy that, once you see them, you cannot unsee — you'll recognize them in boardroom presentations, annual reports, political speeches, and strategic plans from organizations of every size and type.

### Hallmark 1: Fluff

Fluff is strategy-shaped language that says nothing. It sounds strategic — it uses the right vocabulary, the right level of abstraction, the right tone of seriousness — but strip away the jargon and there's no real content underneath.

Consider this example (it is a composite, but I promise you have read something like it): *"Our strategy is to leverage our core competencies in value-added service delivery to create synergistic customer-centric solutions across our key market verticals."*

What does that actually mean? Nothing. Every word is either a tautology ("value-added" means it adds value; "customer-centric" means focused on customers), a placeholder for an unspecified idea ("synergistic"), or an undefined claim ("core competencies"). A strategy written in this language is not a strategy. It is a way of appearing to have a strategy while avoiding the difficult choices that real strategy requires.

The test for fluff is simple: can you replace any noun in the sentence with a different noun and have the sentence remain equally true? If "solutions" could just as easily be "products" or "experiences" or "capabilities," the word means nothing.

### Hallmark 2: Failure to Face the Challenge

This is perhaps the most common and most dangerous form of bad strategy. The organization acknowledges, in vague terms, that there is some challenge or problem — but the strategy doesn't actually engage with it. The real problem is glossed over, papered over with optimistic projections and generic improvement initiatives.

Kodak's strategy throughout much of the 1990s and 2000s was a masterpiece of this failure. The company acknowledged, annually, that digital photography was a growing force. But its strategic response — continuing to invest in premium film products, launching incremental digital cameras, improving its photo lab infrastructure — never engaged with the actual challenge, which was that the entire business model was obsolete. They were rearranging furniture in a building that was on fire.

### Hallmark 3: Mistaking Goals for Strategy

"We will be the number one company in our industry." "We will achieve 25 percent market share by 2030." "We will create world-class customer experiences." These are goals. They are not strategies. A goal tells you where you want to go. A strategy tells you how you plan to get there — and specifically, what choices you will make and what trade-offs you will accept.

The conflation of goals and strategy is almost universal in corporate planning. Ask most CEOs what their strategy is, and they'll describe where they want to be. Strategy consultants spend a shocking percentage of their time helping executives recognize that their "strategy document" is actually a wish list with no theory of how the wishes get granted.

The problem with mistaking goals for strategy is not that goals are bad — goals are essential. The problem is that without a coherent strategy, the goal provides no guidance for the thousand daily decisions that either advance or undermine it. "We will be number one" tells a product manager nothing about whether to invest in a new feature or reduce prices. A real strategy would.

### Hallmark 4: Bad Strategic Objectives

The final hallmark is strategic objectives that are either trivially achievable, impossibly vague, or disconnected from any coherent approach. The most common form is the long list of strategic priorities — six key pillars, eight transformation initiatives, twelve strategic goals. When everything is a priority, nothing is.

Rumelt has a memorable phrase for this: strategy should be "coherent and focused action." The coherence comes from having a guiding policy that explicitly rules things out. When you have twelve strategic priorities that are all supposedly equal, you have no guiding policy at all — you have an aspiration, dressed up as a plan.

## The Activity System: Why Strategy Is Hard to Copy

Porter's most elegant contribution to strategic theory is the concept of the **activity system** — the idea that sustainable competitive advantage doesn't come from any single choice or capability, but from a tightly interlocked web of choices that reinforce each other.

The logic goes like this. Any single strategic choice — serving a particular customer segment, setting a particular price point, using a particular technology — can, in principle, be copied by a competitor. What's much harder to copy is an entire system of mutually reinforcing choices, because copying one element in isolation doesn't help, and copying all of them requires dismantling your existing system and rebuilding from scratch.

Think of it like a jigsaw puzzle. You can easily look at a completed puzzle and identify any one piece. But you can't take that piece and use it to complete a different puzzle — it only fits in the context of all the other pieces. Strategic positions work the same way.

We'll explore this in depth in the Southwest Airlines case study, but the principle has broad application. When you analyse any durable competitive advantage, you almost always find not one source of differentiation but a system of ten or twenty mutually reinforcing elements, each of which makes the others stronger.

This is also why the most dangerous strategic mistake a company can make is what Porter calls "straddling" — trying to have elements of two different strategic positions simultaneously. Continental Airlines, watching Southwest's success in the 1990s, launched "Continental Lite" — a low-cost, no-frills service running alongside its full-service operation. It was a catastrophe. Continental couldn't fully commit to the low-cost model because it needed to maintain its hub-and-spoke network for its premium business customers. The two models undermined each other, costs remained too high for low-cost competition, and the service was too inconsistent for premium customers. Continental eventually abandoned the experiment at significant cost.

## Case Study 1: Kodak vs. Fujifilm — The Same River, Two Shores

### The Empire at Its Peak

To understand how completely Kodak dominated its world, you have to understand what it meant to be an American consumer in 1976. There were no smartphones. Photography meant film — rolls of chemical-coated plastic that you loaded into a camera, spent weeks exposing one frame at a time, dropped off at a drugstore or mail-order service for development, and eventually received back as a set of glossy 4-by-6 prints that you put in an album or a shoebox. The entire ecosystem of memory-making ran through Kodak.

At its peak, Kodak controlled roughly 90 percent of U.S. film sales and 85 percent of camera sales. The company employed more than 145,000 people worldwide. Its headquarters in Rochester, New York, was so central to the city's economy and culture that Rochester was nicknamed "Kodak Town." The company's stock was a fixture of the Dow Jones Industrial Average for most of the twentieth century — a blue-chip holding, as reliable and American as IBM or General Motors.

What made Kodak's position particularly formidable was the economics of its business model. Film photography was essentially a razor-and-blade business: the camera was the razor, sold at modest margins, and the film — bought repeatedly, developed repeatedly, printed repeatedly — was the blade, sold at extraordinary margins. Kodak's gross margins on film were estimated at roughly 70 percent. Every roll of film sold funded the next generation of research, the next wave of marketing, the next expansion of photo labs. It was a self-reinforcing machine.

### The Invention in the Basement

Sasson's 1975 prototype was not a practical product. It was a proof of concept — an 8-pound, cassette-tape-recording device that produced a 0.01 megapixel image in black and white. By any reasonable commercial standard, it was years away from anything a consumer could use.

But it was, unmistakably, the beginning of something.

When Sasson presented his invention to management, the response was not simple ignorance. The executives understood what they were looking at. The question they asked was not "does this work?" but "what do we do with it?" And the answer they arrived at — however quietly and without explicit discussion — was: we protect our film business.

To be fair to Kodak's management, this was not an obviously irrational decision in 1975. The film business was enormously profitable. Digital technology was primitive. The cost of digital cameras, even if the technology could be improved to consumer quality, would be prohibitive for most households. And the question of what people would do with digital photos — there was no internet, no email, no way to share images electronically — was genuinely unresolved.

The problem was not the decision made in 1975. The problem was the pattern of decisions made over the next twenty-five years, as each of those early objections became less and less valid, and Kodak kept finding new reasons to delay the reckoning.

Kodak did invest in digital technology. The company spent hundreds of millions of dollars on digital research and development throughout the 1980s and 1990s. It launched digital cameras. It developed digital photo printing systems. But each investment was constrained by the same implicit strategic logic: don't cannibalize the film business. Don't move too fast. Don't disrupt what's working.

The result was a company that was simultaneously too committed to digital to have the purity of focus required to dominate film forever, and too committed to film to have the boldness required to lead the digital transition.

### The Slow Collapse

Film sales peaked globally around 1999-2000. The descent that followed was not the gradual fade of a declining industry but the cliff edge of a disruption: global film sales fell roughly 90 percent over the next decade. By 2010, the film that had once generated 70-percent gross margins was essentially a niche product sold to professional photographers and nostalgia enthusiasts.

Kodak tried everything. It launched digital cameras — and was, for a while, one of the best-selling brands. It developed digital printing kiosks. It moved into commercial printing and document management. But none of these pivots were guided by a coherent strategy. They were reactive moves, each one designed to replace some portion of the lost film revenue without requiring the company to fundamentally reimagine itself. The diagnosis was wrong: management kept treating the problem as a revenue gap to be filled, rather than as an identity crisis to be resolved.

The company's debt load, its pension obligations to retired workers, and its shrinking cash flow from film combined to create a trap that became increasingly inescapable. In January 2012, Eastman Kodak filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection. The company that had invented digital photography died, at least in its original form, because of digital photography.

### Fujifilm's Reckoning

Let's now travel to Tokyo, to a board meeting in the early 2000s, where Shigetaka Komori was laying out a diagnosis that his board did not want to hear.

Komori, who had become president of Fujifilm in 2000, had done the arithmetic. He knew that Fujifilm's film business generated roughly two-thirds of the company's profits. He knew that global film demand was declining. And he knew — from studying the technology trajectories carefully — that the decline was not going to slow down. It was going to accelerate. Within a decade, he estimated, film would be a fraction of its current size.

"This is not a problem for the future," Komori later recalled thinking. "This is a problem for now."

The VISION75 restructuring plan, launched in 2004 (the "75" stood for Fujifilm's 75th anniversary), was built around a diagnosis that was honest to the point of being brutal: *the company's core business is dying, and we need to build new businesses using the capabilities that our core business developed.* Not "we need to expand into adjacencies while protecting our core." Not "we need to improve our efficiency to weather the transition." We need to *transform*.

The guiding policy that flowed from this diagnosis was deceptively simple: identify all the deep technical capabilities that the photography business had developed, and systematically find industries where those capabilities could be applied to create real competitive advantage.

This required something that most organizations find nearly impossible: genuine self-knowledge. Fujifilm had to ask itself, with clear eyes, "What do we actually know how to do?" — not in the marketing sense of brand identity, but in the scientific sense of deep capability.

The answers were surprising in their breadth. Fujifilm had world-class expertise in:

- **Collagen chemistry** (film emulsion is collagen-based)
- **Antioxidant stabilization** (preventing photo fading)
- **Nano-particle dispersion** (packing molecules with precision into coatings)
- **Optical engineering** (lenses, filters, light management)
- **High-precision coating technology** (applying ultra-thin, ultra-uniform layers)
- **Medical imaging** (the company already made X-ray film)

Each of these capabilities, applied to new markets, became a pillar of Fujifilm's transformation.

### The Pivot in Action

The Astalift skincare line, launched in 2006-2007, was perhaps the most visible example of this transformation — and the most instructive. The line was not a marketing exercise in which a photographic company slapped its brand on someone else's cosmetics. It was a genuine scientific product, built from the inside out.

Fujifilm's collagen research, developed to understand and control the film emulsion, gave the company deep knowledge of how skin's collagen degrades over time — knowledge that most cosmetics companies purchased from outside researchers. The antioxidant techniques developed to prevent color fading translated directly into formulations that prevented skin oxidation, the primary driver of visible aging. The nano-dispersion technology, developed to create precise film emulsions, allowed Astalift's active ingredients to penetrate the skin more effectively than competing products.

The result was a skincare line that had genuine scientific differentiation, backed by decades of relevant (if indirectly relevant) research. Astalift became a significant business, particularly in Japan and across Asia, where premium anti-aging skincare is a substantial market.

But Komori was not building a cosmetics company. He was building a diversified technology company. Medical imaging became an even larger bet. Fujifilm's existing expertise in X-ray film gave it a credible foundation to expand into digital medical imaging systems — CT scanners, digital X-ray systems, endoscopes. The company made a series of acquisitions and internal investments that built a genuinely competitive medical technology business. Today, the Fujifilm Medical Systems division produces endoscopic imaging systems used in hospitals around the world.

Advanced materials for flat-panel displays — leveraging the precision coating technology — became another major business. Pharmaceutical ingredients, optical films, and document management solutions (leveraging the company's printer and copier expertise) rounded out the portfolio.

The restructuring was painful. Fujifilm cut tens of thousands of jobs in its film manufacturing business, closing plants around the world. But unlike Kodak, where the job cuts came too late and were driven by crisis, Fujifilm's cuts were made proactively, generating cash that funded the new investments.

### Why Fujifilm Survived and Kodak Didn't

The surface-level answer is that Fujifilm diversified and Kodak didn't. But that explanation is too shallow. Kodak diversified too — into commercial printing, digital cameras, photo kiosks, and document management. The difference was not whether to diversify but *how*.

Kodak's diversification was reactive and disconnected. Each new business was chosen to fill a revenue gap, not because Kodak had genuine competitive advantage there. Kodak had no particular expertise in commercial printing that would allow it to beat established competitors like Xerox. It had no advantage in digital camera sensors against Sony and Canon. Its pivot attempts were not grounded in a coherent diagnosis of what the company actually was at its deepest level.

Fujifilm's diversification was proactive and grounded. Komori started by asking what Fujifilm genuinely knew how to do — then found markets where that knowledge was competitively valuable. The new businesses were not chosen arbitrarily; they were chosen because Fujifilm had real, defensible, hard-to-replicate advantages in those spaces.

The other critical difference was the willingness to cannibalize. Fujifilm did not manage its transition to minimize the pain to its existing film business. It managed the transition to maximize the success of its new businesses, even when that required aggressively shrinking film. Kodak, by contrast, was perpetually trying to find ways to slow the transition rather than lead it — to preserve the film margins for just a little longer, to fund digital investments without committing to them fully.

The Kodak story is often told as a failure of foresight. But it was actually a failure of strategic courage — the willingness to make the hard choices that a clear diagnosis demands. Kodak saw the future as early as 1975. What it couldn't do was choose the future over the present.

### What This Teaches Us

The Kodak-Fujifilm story illustrates Rumelt's kernel with painful clarity.

Fujifilm's diagnosis was honest and specific: *film will disappear, and we need to redeploy our capabilities before it does.* Kodak's implicit diagnosis was dishonest and self-serving: *film will decline slowly enough that we can adapt gradually.* The first diagnosis is uncomfortable. The second is comfortable. That comfort was lethal.

Fujifilm's guiding policy — leverage core technical capabilities to compete in high-margin adjacent industries — gave coherence to all the specific decisions that followed. Kodak's implicit guiding policy — find ways to extend the film business and supplement it with digital revenue — pointed in multiple directions at once and committed to none of them.

Fujifilm's actions were coherent because they all derived from the same diagnosis and the same guiding policy. Kodak's actions were incoherent because there was no consistent logic connecting them.

Both companies had the same information. Strategy is not about having information. It is about what you do with it — and whether you have the courage to let the diagnosis drive the decisions, even when the decisions are painful.

## Case Study 2: Southwest Airlines — The Most Profitable Trade-offs in Aviation History

### The Industry That Shouldn't Work

Commercial aviation is, as an industry, notoriously terrible at making money. Billions of dollars of capital are poured into aircraft, fuel, airport infrastructure, and training. Labour costs are enormous. Fuel prices are volatile. Demand is sensitive to economic cycles, geopolitical events, and, as 2020 demonstrated with devastating thoroughness, pandemics. Margins are thin, competition is fierce, and customers have almost no loyalty beyond price.

Warren Buffett — who has made roughly one investment in the airline industry in his career, hated it, and eventually made a reasonable profit mostly by selling — famously quipped that a farsighted capitalist at Kitty Hawk in 1903 should have shot Orville Wright down. "Investors have poured their money into airlines," Buffett has said, "and airline after airline has gone into bankruptcy."

Against this backdrop, one airline managed to be profitable for 47 consecutive years — from 1973 through 2019, before COVID ended the streak. That airline was Southwest.

Southwest didn't achieve this through superior customer service (it was a no-frills operation). It didn't achieve it through a premium product (no business class, no meals on most routes, no assigned seats for most of its history). It achieved it through a strategy of such radical consistency that it constitutes, as Porter argued, perhaps the most complete example of strategic positioning in modern business history.

### Herb Kelleher's Brilliant Constraints

Southwest Airlines was founded in 1967 by Rollin King and Herb Kelleher, initially to serve three Texas cities: Dallas, Houston, and San Antonio. The original concept was simple: make flying affordable for people who currently drive or take the bus. Not for business travellers chasing loyalty points. Not for vacationers crossing the country. For Texans who needed to get from Dallas to Houston, and who at the time had no practical alternative to a four-hour drive.

This founding insight — serve the underserved short-haul leisure traveller — was the foundation from which everything else followed. Kelleher, who would lead the company as CEO from 1981 to 2001, understood that having a clear customer in mind makes every subsequent decision easier, because you can always ask: "Does this serve our customer, given our cost constraint?"

The answer to that question, applied consistently for decades, produced a list of NOs that is breathtaking in its discipline.

**No hub-and-spoke routing.** Traditional airlines like American, United, and Delta organize their networks around major hubs — Atlanta, Chicago, Dallas — through which most connecting passengers flow. Hub-and-spoke maximizes network coverage and allows an airline to serve a vast number of city pairs using a relatively small number of routes. But it also creates complexity, delay, and cost. Southwest chose point-to-point routing exclusively — direct flights between city pairs, no connections required. This simplified operations enormously and allowed Southwest to compete on the specific routes it chose with a structural cost advantage.

**No assigned seats.** For most of Southwest's history, passengers received a boarding group (A, B, or C) and chose their seat on the aircraft when they boarded. This sounds like a minor inconvenience. But it had major operational implications. Because there was no seat assignment to manage, the boarding process was faster and required less computer systems overhead. Turnaround time at the gate — the window between when a plane arrives and when it pushes back again — was dramatically compressed.

**No meals on most flights.** Southwest served peanuts (famously) and soft drinks. No meals meant no catering infrastructure, no loading time, no in-flight preparation. More importantly, no meals meant shorter service time in the air, which meant less complex crew scheduling.

**No baggage transfers to other airlines.** If you were connecting on Southwest and needed to transfer to a different airline, you checked your bag yourself. Southwest didn't interline baggage. This is inconvenient for passengers making complex itineraries — but Southwest wasn't serving those passengers. It was serving people flying point-to-point, who didn't need connections to other carriers.

**No business class.** No first class. One cabin, all coach. This simplified the aircraft interior, reduced cleaning time during turnarounds, and eliminated the complex pricing structures and upgrade systems that create operational overhead at premium carriers.

**One aircraft type: the Boeing 737, exclusively.** Southwest has operated only Boeing 737 variants for its entire history. This is perhaps the most financially significant of all its NOs. A single aircraft type means:
- All pilots are type-rated for the same aircraft (no separate pools of pilots for different planes)
- All maintenance technicians know one aircraft (reducing training costs, increasing utilization)
- Spare parts are interchangeable across the entire fleet
- Scheduling and crew assignments are vastly simpler
- The company has enormous purchasing power with Boeing (ordering hundreds of identical aircraft)

The cost savings from fleet commonality alone are estimated to be worth hundreds of millions of dollars annually compared to a carrier operating multiple aircraft types.

**Secondary airports.** Southwest famously operates out of secondary or underutilized airports where possible. Dallas Love Field rather than DFW International. Chicago Midway rather than O'Hare. Baltimore/Washington International rather than Dulles or Reagan National. Secondary airports offer lower gate fees, less congestion, faster gate turnarounds, and more scheduling flexibility. The trade-off is that some business travellers prefer the convenience of major hub airports. Southwest accepted that trade-off.

**Short turnarounds.** All of these choices converged on a single obsessive metric: gate turnaround time. At legacy airlines, aircraft spend an average of 45-60 minutes at the gate between flights. Southwest's target was 25 minutes. The entire operational system — no meals, no assigned seats, secondary airports, one aircraft type, point-to-point routing — was designed to make this 25-minute turnaround possible. And 25-minute turnarounds meant aircraft were in the air more hours per day, generating revenue more hours per day, than competitors' aircraft.

### The Activity System Map

What makes Southwest extraordinary as a strategy case is not any one of these choices — it's how they reinforce each other. Porter famously drew an "activity system map" for Southwest that illustrated these interdependencies, and it's worth working through the logic.

Each element reinforces the others. The single aircraft type enables lower maintenance costs, which enables the 25-minute turnaround. The 25-minute turnaround is enabled by point-to-point routing (no connection baggage to worry about), no meals (no catering logistics), and no assigned seats (faster boarding). The lower costs from all these elements enable low fares. Low fares drive high load factors. High load factors improve profitability even with thin margins per seat.

Now ask: could United Airlines copy just one element of this? Could Delta introduce 25-minute gate turnarounds?

Not without dismantling half their operation. Delta operates multiple aircraft types — its pilots need to be rated for 737s, 767s, 777s, Airbus A320s, A330s, and more. Its hub-and-spoke model means thousands of connecting passengers whose bags must be transferred between flights, which takes time that can't be compressed to 25 minutes. Its premium business class passengers expect assigned seats, meals, and airport lounge access. Its network requires the major hub airports — O'Hare, JFK, Hartsfield-Jackson — where congestion prevents the kind of fast turnarounds Southwest achieves at secondary airports.

Delta could spend ten years and billions of dollars trying to reshape itself toward Southwest's model. In the process, it would destroy the premium product that its most profitable customers pay for. The activity system is a self-reinforcing trap — beneficial for Southwest, nearly impossible for competitors to replicate without abandoning their own position.

### The Financial Result

Southwest's 47-year streak of profitability (1973-2019) is not just an impressive statistic. It is a proof of concept for the idea that strategic consistency creates durable advantage. In an industry where virtually every major carrier went through bankruptcy at least once — United, American, Delta, US Airways, Continental — Southwest never filed.

The numbers behind the streak are equally striking. Southwest has historically operated with one of the lowest cost-per-available-seat-mile (CASM) ratios in the U.S. domestic industry. This cost advantage, derived from its activity system, allowed it to offer fares that legacy carriers couldn't match while still generating positive margins.

There is a postscript worth noting. Southwest has been under pressure in recent years — from labour cost increases, from Boeing 737 MAX delivery delays, and from changing customer preferences. In 2024, the company announced it would introduce assigned seating, departing from one of its longest-held NOs. Whether this represents a smart adaptation to changing customer demands or the beginning of a drift from strategic clarity is a question worth watching. Strategy is not static — it must evolve as the environment changes. But the danger of changing individual elements of an activity system is that the changes ripple through the entire system in unexpected ways. As of this writing, the jury is still out.

What is not in doubt is the 47-year lesson: a coherent set of trade-offs, rigorously maintained, can build an advantage that outlasts individual recessions, fuel shocks, industry restructurings, and competitive onslaughts. Strategy works.

## Case Study 3: IKEA — Every Decision Connects

### A Store That Shouldn't Exist

On the face of it, IKEA is a deeply strange business. It asks you to drive to a location that is almost certainly inconvenient, walk a maze-like store that takes an hour to navigate, select furniture that you cannot have delivered immediately and which, when you get it home, you will have to assemble yourself — with a wrench, following instructions that contain no words. And yet IKEA is the world's largest furniture retailer, serving roughly 800 million customers per year across more than 400 stores in 62 countries.

IKEA's success is not an accident of marketing or a product of particularly charismatic leadership. It is the result of a strategy of extraordinary internal consistency — a system in which every operational choice reinforces every other choice, and the whole is dramatically more powerful than the sum of its parts.

### The Origin Story: Flat-Pack Everything

Ingvar Kamprad founded IKEA in Sweden in 1943 — originally as a mail-order business selling household goods. The furniture dimension came later. The flat-pack insight came by accident, in 1956, when an IKEA employee named Gillis Lundgren was trying to fit a table into a car for delivery and, unable to make it fit, removed the legs. Kamprad, observing this, immediately grasped the implication: furniture that could be disassembled for transport and reassembled by the customer could be sold in flat packages, transported in greater quantities, stored more efficiently, and sold at dramatically lower prices.

The flat-pack wasn't just a packaging innovation. It was a strategic insight that restructured the entire value chain.

**Flat-pack furniture can be sold in self-service warehouses.** If customers are going to assemble the furniture themselves anyway, they don't need a salesperson to demonstrate it and carry it to their car. They need a warehouse where they can find their item, load it onto a trolley, and check out. This eliminates the labour cost of a traditional furniture store's delivery and assembly service.

**Self-service warehouses benefit from scale.** A traditional furniture showroom can be small — you only need enough space to display the items. A self-service warehouse needs to stock inventory. This creates a natural pull toward large stores, which create a natural pull toward suburban or out-of-town locations where land is cheaper and car access is easier.

**Suburban megastores become destinations.** Because customers are making a purposeful trip — not just browsing — the store can offer more variety and depth than a traditional furniture shop. This justifies the product range of roughly 10,000 items in a typical IKEA store. The variety, in turn, justifies the trip, creating a virtuous cycle.

**In-store restaurants keep customers engaged.** IKEA's food service — the famous Swedish meatballs, the hot dogs, the cheap coffee and cinnamon rolls — is not a side business or a customer amenity. It is a strategic element. Customers who are shopping a large, complex store for an extended period need sustenance. If they leave to eat, they may not return. IKEA's cafeterias are designed to keep customers in the store for hours, increasing the probability of additional purchases.

**Own-brand only increases margin and design control.** IKEA sells only IKEA-branded products. There are no third-party brands, no licensed products, no partnerships with other furniture makers. This allows IKEA to design specifically for the flat-pack constraint — to engineer furniture from the ground up to be beautiful, functional, and manufacturable in flat-pack form. It also allows IKEA to capture the full margin on every product rather than sharing it with a brand owner.

**Scandinavian design provides aspirational identity at budget pricing.** The IKEA aesthetic — clean lines, functional design, light wood tones, uncluttered silhouettes — is rooted in the Scandinavian design tradition but accessible at prices that make it democratic rather than elitist. Customers are not buying cheap furniture that looks cheap. They're buying affordable furniture that looks designed. This is a specific, defensible position: design-forward furniture at prices that don't require design-forward income.

**Limited customer service reinforces low price.** IKEA staffs its stores lightly. There are staff available to help, but the expectation is that customers are largely self-sufficient — navigating with maps, selecting items from catalogue numbers, loading their own trolleys, transporting their own furniture. This is not an accident or a cost-cutting failure. It is a deliberate trade-off: lower service in exchange for lower prices, which is exactly what IKEA's target customer prefers.

### The Internal Logic

The beauty of IKEA's strategy is that you can enter the system at any point and follow the logic all the way around.

Start with the customer choice: design-conscious, budget-sensitive, young or early-stage household. That customer implies a price point. The price point implies lean operations. Lean operations imply self-service. Self-service implies flat-pack. Flat-pack implies large warehouse format. Large warehouse format implies suburban locations. Suburban locations imply car-friendly, destination shopping. Destination shopping implies a full day's visit. A full day's visit implies a restaurant. The restaurant implies a comfortable, family-friendly environment. The environment reinforces the brand. The brand reinforces the customer choice.

Every element feeds every other element. You cannot pull one piece out without disrupting the whole.

```
IKEA's Activity System (Simplified)

        ↓
[Low Price Point] ←─────────────────────────────┐

        ↓                                        │
[Large warehouse format]                         │
        ↓                                        │

        ↓

        ↓

```

### IKEA in India: Strategy Meets Local Reality

In August 2018, IKEA opened its first Indian store in Hyderabad — a 400,000 square-foot megastore that was, at the time, the largest IKEA store in the world. The launch was watched closely by students of strategy, because it posed a genuine question: could a strategy built for affluent Scandinavian consumers translate to a market as price-sensitive, culturally distinct, and operationally complex as India?

The answer revealed something important about how good strategy adapts without losing its core logic.

IKEA made several India-specific adaptations:

**Lower price points on entry-level items.** Indian consumers, even in the urban middle class, have significantly lower purchasing power than IKEA's traditional European customer base. IKEA introduced a range of products priced at ₹200 (less than $3) or less, specifically for the Indian market. The goal was to give Indian consumers a point of entry that felt accessible rather than aspirational.

**Localised food.** The Hyderabad restaurant did not serve just Swedish meatballs. It served samosas, biryani, masala chai, and idlis alongside the Scandinavian staples. The logic was identical to Sweden — keep customers in the store longer — but the implementation was culturally appropriate. The Swedish meatballs, notably, were replaced with chicken and plant-based alternatives to accommodate Hindu and Muslim dietary preferences.

**Smaller format stores planned for city centres.** IKEA recognised early that the standard suburban megastore format had limitations in Indian cities, where land is expensive and middle-class consumers often lack the car access that IKEA's suburban model assumes. The company began planning smaller-format stores in Mumbai and other major cities — a genuine adaptation of the store format to local infrastructure conditions.

**In-store assembly services.** In many Western markets, IKEA's self-assembly model is a feature — customers enjoy it, or at least accept it. In India, where skilled labour is cheap and the cultural expectation for furniture is that it comes delivered and assembled, self-assembly was a potential barrier. IKEA partnered with local service providers to offer affordable assembly services, reducing this friction without abandoning the flat-pack model entirely.

What IKEA did *not* change was the core strategic logic: own-brand only, flat-pack design, large-format store, Scandinavian aesthetic, and the fundamental positioning as design-forward budget furniture. The adaptations were real but peripheral — they addressed local friction points without compromising the internal system that generates IKEA's advantage.

This is what strategic consistency in new markets looks like. It is not rigid refusal to adapt. It is adaptation at the edges while preserving the core. A company that changes its fundamental activity system for every new market doesn't have a strategy — it has a collection of local businesses. A company that adapts intelligently while maintaining the core activity system takes its strategic advantage into new territory.

By 2023, IKEA had plans for significant expansion in India — in Bengaluru, Mumbai, Delhi — and its Hyderabad store was regularly cited as one of the highest-footfall retail destinations in the country. The local adaptations worked. The core strategy worked. And the combination of the two demonstrated that a well-constructed activity system is portable — it can cross cultures, as long as the adaptations respect its internal logic.

### The Lesson from IKEA

IKEA's 80-year run as a strategic success story teaches three things.

First: a clear customer definition makes every decision easier. IKEA has never tried to serve everyone. It has always served a specific type of customer — design-conscious, price-sensitive, willing to invest some effort in exchange for significant savings. Every choice follows from that definition.

Second: the deepest competitive advantage comes not from any single element of the system but from the system itself. Any competitor can offer flat-pack furniture. Any competitor can open a large store. Any competitor can offer Scandinavian-style design. What competitors cannot easily replicate is the interlocked system that makes each element reinforce the others — the same lesson we drew from Southwest.

Third: strategy is about coherence, not completeness. IKEA does not try to be the best at everything. Its customer service is limited by design. Its prices are low partly because its customer service is limited. Its stores are in inconvenient locations because those locations allow the space and cost structure the model requires. Accepting these limitations is not a strategic failure. It is the strategy.

## Pulling It All Together: The Strategic Lens

Let's return to where we started — to that basement in Rochester, in 1975, and to the question that sits at the heart of this entire book.

Steve Sasson showed his managers the future. They saw it clearly. And they chose to protect the present.

This chapter has argued that strategy is not about having information, setting goals, or executing plans. It is about making coherent choices — choices about where to compete and where not to compete, how to win and what to sacrifice in the process. It is about building systems of mutually reinforcing activities that create positions competitors cannot easily replicate. And it is about having the courage to let an honest diagnosis drive those choices, even when the choices are uncomfortable.

The companies that do this well — Fujifilm, Southwest, IKEA — share a recognizable pattern. They define a specific customer. They make trade-offs that serve that customer brilliantly, at the cost of serving other customers poorly or not at all. They build activity systems where every element reinforces every other element. And they maintain the discipline to say NO to options that might seem attractive but would undermine the coherence of their position.

The companies that fail at strategy — Kodak is the tragic archetype, but the list is long — tend to fail in one of a few recognizable ways. They mistake operational effectiveness for strategy. They mistake goals for strategy. They refuse to make real trade-offs, trying to be everything to everyone. They let a comfortable diagnosis prevent an honest one. And they build strategies that are actually just lists of good ideas, without the internal coherence that turns a list into a position.

As you read the rest of this book, you will encounter many frameworks, tools, and concepts — from competitive analysis (Chapter 2) to financial strategy (Chapter 7) to organizational design (Chapter 10) to innovation management (Chapter 12). But every one of those topics connects back to the core question of this chapter: *What are you choosing? And what are you choosing not to do?*

Strategy is not a document. It is not a planning process. It is not a set of aspirations. Strategy is a set of integrated choices, made with courage and clarity, that position a company to create distinctive value for a specific set of customers in a way that competitors cannot easily replicate.

Everything else is just operations.

## The One Thing to Remember

> Strategy is not a plan, a vision, or a goal — it is a coherent set of integrated choices about where and how to compete, with trade-offs that make your position genuinely defensible against imitation.

## How to Use This Tomorrow

1. **Run the "goals vs. strategy" audit on your organization.** Pull out your current strategic plan or annual operating plan and highlight every sentence that describes a goal ("We will achieve X") versus a choice ("We will focus on Y and explicitly not pursue Z"). If your plan has more goals than choices, you have a goal list, not a strategy. Start a conversation about what you're actually choosing.

2. **Identify your activity system.** List the ten most important things your organization does operationally. Draw lines between any two elements that reinforce each other. If your activities are all independent — if removing one wouldn't affect the others — you don't have an activity system. You have a list of activities. Ask what would need to change to make them interdependent.

3. **Find your real trade-offs.** For every major strategic initiative on your roadmap, ask: "What would we have to give up to do this well?" If the answer is "nothing," either the initiative isn't truly strategic, or you're not being honest about the trade-offs. Real strategic choices always cost something.

4. **Apply Rumelt's kernel.** For any strategy you're currently involved in developing or executing, test it against three questions: (a) Is there a clear, honest diagnosis of the core challenge — one that names the real problem, not a comfortable version of it? (b) Is there a guiding policy that rules things out, or does it leave everything on the table? (c) Are the specific actions coherent — do they reinforce each other, or are they just a list of good ideas?

5. **Do the Fujifilm exercise.** If your organization's core business disappeared tomorrow — completely gone, like film photography — what would you actually know how to do? Not what products you make or what markets you serve, but what underlying capabilities and expertise you possess. Make a list. Then ask: where else could those capabilities create value? You may not face an existential threat today. But the companies that survive disruption are the ones that have answered this question before the disruption arrives.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1: Dissect a Strategy You Know**

Choose a company you interact with regularly as a customer — a supermarket chain, a bank, a clothing brand, a software product, a restaurant chain. Try to map its activity system. What are the ten most important things it does operationally? How do those activities reinforce each other? What customer is it serving, and what trade-offs has it made to serve that customer? Now ask: is this company actually making strategic choices, or is it trying to appeal to everyone? If you were a competitor, what would you have to do to replicate its position? What would you have to give up?

**Exercise 2: The Competitor Copy Test**

Think about your own organization's most distinctive capability or practice — the thing that feels most like a real competitive advantage. Now imagine a well-resourced competitor deciding to copy it. Walk through what it would take for them to replicate it exactly. What would they have to change about their own organization? What would they have to give up? If copying it seems easy, your "advantage" is probably operational effectiveness, not strategy. If copying it would require them to fundamentally restructure themselves, you may have a genuine strategic position.

**Exercise 3: Write a One-Page Strategy**

Using Rumelt's kernel as your structure, write a one-page strategy for either your current organization or a business you're thinking about starting. Force yourself to be specific: What is the honest diagnosis of the core challenge? What is the guiding policy — and crucially, what does it explicitly rule out? What are three to five coherent actions that implement the guiding policy and reinforce each other? When you're done, show it to someone who works in the organization and ask if it describes what you actually do, or what you wish you did. The gap between those two answers is your strategy problem.

**Exercise 4: The Kodak Thought Experiment**

Imagine you are the CEO of Kodak in 1999. Film sales have just peaked. Your internal research tells you that digital photography will become mainstream within five to ten years. You have $2 billion in cash, a world-class chemistry research organization, a brand that is recognized by 99 percent of American consumers, and a profitable but declining film business. What do you do? Write out your strategy in Rumelt's kernel format: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions. Then — and this is the hard part — identify specifically what you would have to sacrifice to execute it. Which businesses would you shrink or exit? Which constituencies would you disappoint? Which short-term profits would you forgo? The exercise is not to find the right answer (there isn't one) — it is to feel the weight of real strategic choice.

## Further Reading

- **Good Strategy Bad Strategy** by Richard Rumelt — If you read only one book on strategy in your life, read this one. Rumelt's distinction between genuine strategy and the "bad strategy" that fills most corporate planning documents is one of the most useful intellectual frameworks in business. The writing is clear, the examples are vivid, and the framework is immediately applicable. Every chapter contains something that will change how you think about what your organization is actually doing.

- **What Is Strategy?** by Michael Porter (Harvard Business Review, 1996) — This is not a book but an essay, and it is available freely online. At roughly 6,000 words, it is the most efficient statement of Porter's core ideas about strategy, competitive positioning, trade-offs, and the activity system. If you've read it before, read it again — it rewards rereading at different stages of a career. Porter's distinction between operational effectiveness and strategy is one of those ideas that, once understood, cannot be unseen.

- **Measure What Matters** by John Doerr — A complementary read to the strategy frameworks in this chapter. Doerr's book is about OKRs (Objectives and Key Results), the goal-setting framework used by Google, Intel, and hundreds of other organizations. The connection to strategy is this: strategy tells you *what to do* and *why*; OKRs help you translate that into measurable action and track whether you're actually executing your strategy or just doing a lot of things. Understanding both frameworks together — strategy as choice, OKRs as execution — gives you a complete picture of how good organizations turn intent into outcome.
