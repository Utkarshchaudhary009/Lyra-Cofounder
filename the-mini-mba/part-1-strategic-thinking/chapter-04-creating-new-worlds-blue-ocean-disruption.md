# Chapter 4: Creating New Worlds — Blue Ocean & Disruption

In 1984, a small group of street performers in Baie-Saint-Paul, Quebec, put on a show called *Le Grand Tour du Cirque du Soleil*. They juggled, they danced, they performed acrobatics under a tent that leaked when it rained. There were no animals. No ringmaster. No three-ring spectacle. The audience numbered in the hundreds. The performers were barely getting paid. By any conventional measure, it was a modest beginning.

Twenty-one years later, in 2005, Cirque du Soleil was generating more revenue than Ringling Brothers and Barnum & Bailey — the combined giants of the traditional circus industry, which had dominated that market for over a century. Cirque du Soleil achieved this with no animals, no star performers, and ticket prices that were roughly ten times higher than its competitors. The company was selling out shows in Las Vegas, New York, London, Tokyo, and dozens of other cities around the world. It had transformed a struggling regional art form into a global entertainment phenomenon.

How did a circus with no animals beat the animal circuses? How did performers with no names fill theaters that had been built for Broadway stars? How did a Canadian street-performance troupe out-earn the most famous entertainment brands in the world?

The answer, when you look at the details, is almost startling in its clarity.

Cirque du Soleil did not try to be a better circus. It did not invest in better animals, more famous performers, or bigger tents. It did not try to beat Ringling Brothers at the game Ringling Brothers was playing. Instead, it stopped playing that game entirely. It stopped competing in the circus industry and started competing in an entirely new space — a blend of circus, theater, opera, and dance that had never quite existed before. It didn't beat the competition. It made the competition irrelevant.

This is the essence of what two INSEAD professors, W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne, called **Blue Ocean Strategy**. And it is the first of two frameworks in this chapter that ask us to question something we take for granted: that competition is the natural state of business.

---

There is a second story I want to tell you, from a completely different industry.

In 1997, a thirty-seven-year-old software entrepreneur named Reed Hastings returned to his apartment in Santa Cruz, California, after a workout. He had rented *Apollo 13* from a Blockbuster video store and, as he later told the story (perhaps with some creative embellishment), he realized he had lost the tape. He was facing a $40 late fee.

The late fee, in Hastings's telling, set off a chain of thought. Why did video rental work this way? Why were customers forced to travel to a physical store, find a movie that might or might not be in stock, drive home, watch it, and then drive back to return it within a fixed window or pay a penalty? Why did the entire model depend on scarcity — one physical copy serving one customer at a time? What if you could rent movies by mail, through the postal system, and keep them as long as you wanted with no late fees?

Out of that question — a question about the *job* customers were trying to get done, a question about the assumptions embedded in an entire industry — came Netflix.

In 2000, four years after Hastings founded the company, Netflix approached Blockbuster with a proposal: Netflix would run Blockbuster's online brand for $50 million a year. Blockbuster's management laughed them out of the room. Blockbuster had 9,000 stores across America, $6 billion in revenue, a brand recognized by every household that owned a television, and a business model that had worked profitably for decades. Netflix had a few hundred thousand subscribers, a warehouse in Los Gatos, and a business model in which each DVD had to be mailed both ways — a model whose economics even its own employees were not entirely sure would work.

In 2010, Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy.

The surface story is simple: a nimble startup killed a slow-moving dinosaur. But the real story — the strategic story — is far more interesting. Netflix did not defeat Blockbuster at the video rental game. Netflix changed the game. And then, just when the world thought it understood what Netflix was, it changed the game again. And then again.

These two stories — Cirque du Soleil and Netflix — point toward something deeper than individual corporate success. They point toward a different way of thinking about how markets are created and destroyed. The traditional view of strategy, which we explored in Chapter 1, assumes that competition is the defining reality of business. You compete for customers. You compete for market share. You compete on price, quality, features, and service. Porter's framework is built on this assumption: the structure of the industry determines the intensity of competition, and your job as a strategist is to find a position within that structure where you can defend yourself from competitive forces.

But what if the assumption is wrong? What if the most powerful move is not to compete more effectively *within* an existing industry but to create an entirely new industry? What if the most dangerous competitor is not the one who beats you but the one who makes your entire category irrelevant?

That possibility is the subject of this chapter. We will explore two of the most influential strategic frameworks of the last thirty years — Blue Ocean Strategy and Disruption Theory — and we will look at where they overlap, where they disagree, and where they break down. We will examine companies that created new worlds (Cirque du Soleil, Netflix), companies that disrupted from unexpected directions (Tesla, Uber), and companies that changed the very structure of their industries (Airbnb, Amazon). And we will ask the question that matters most: *can you choose to create a new world, or is that something that only looks obvious in retrospect?*

## Part A: Blue Ocean Strategy

### Red Oceans and Blue Oceans

Kim and Mauborgne's central metaphor is simple enough to explain at a dinner table. Imagine a vast ocean divided into two parts.

The **red ocean** is the known market space. It is red because it is filled with the blood of competitors fighting over the same customers, the same limited pool of demand, the same familiar profit pools. In the red ocean, the boundaries of the industry are well-defined, everyone knows who the competitors are, and the basis of competition is understood by all participants. Companies try to beat each other on price, features, quality, or service — but the underlying assumption is that the market is a fixed pie. If one company gains share, another loses it.

Most of business strategy — including Porter's framework and most traditional MBA training — is about how to succeed in the red ocean. How do you position yourself to win a larger share of the existing market? How do you defend against the five forces that threaten your margins? How do you build operating efficiencies that allow you to compete on price? These are important questions, and they apply to most business situations most of the time.

The **blue ocean** is the unknown market space. It is blue because it is uncontested — free of the blood that colors the red ocean. In the blue ocean, demand is created rather than fought over. The boundaries of the industry have not yet been drawn. The rules of the game have not yet been written. The opportunity for profitable growth is enormous, not because margins are higher (though they often are) but because there is no competition.

Here is the crucial point, and it is one that is frequently misunderstood: a blue ocean is not a new technology. It is not a new product feature. It is a fundamental redefinition of the value proposition — a way of offering customers something they did not know they could have, at a price they did not think was possible, through a configuration of features that did not previously exist together.

Color television was a new technology. Streaming video was a new technology. Neither was a blue ocean by itself. A blue ocean emerges when a company redefines *what* an industry offers, *how* it offers it, and *to whom* — in a way that creates a new demand curve that did not previously exist.

This leads to a second crucial point: industries do not stay blue. Blue oceans eventually turn red, because successful new markets attract imitators. The question is not whether your blue ocean will stay blue forever. The question is whether you can create a blue ocean before your red one becomes unsustainable, and whether you can defend its boundaries long enough to capture its value.

### The Strategy Canvas

Kim and Mauborgne's primary analytical tool is the **Strategy Canvas** — a visual diagnostic that does for strategy what a heat map does for temperature. It reveals patterns that are invisible when you look at individual data points.

The Strategy Canvas is built on two axes. The horizontal axis lists the key competitive factors that the industry competes on — the features, attributes, and services that companies invest in to win customers. For a traditional circus, these would include: animals, star performers, aisle concessions, multiple rings, discounted tickets, family entertainment, humor and thrills, and so on. The vertical axis measures the level of offering buyers receive for each of these factors — how much the industry invests in each one.

When you plot the strategy canvas for an industry, you get a **value curve** — a line that shows how the industry as a whole allocates its resources. In most mature industries, the value curves of competitors cluster together. They converge on roughly the same set of competitive factors, invested in at roughly the same level. This is what strategy looks like when everyone is competing in the same red ocean.

The point of the Strategy Canvas is not to tell you where you stand within the existing industry structure. The point is to reveal where you can break away from that structure. A blue ocean strategy shows up on the canvas as a value curve that is dramatically different from the industry average — not just a little higher or lower on existing factors, but an entirely different shape.

The gap between the two curves is the strategic opportunity. It is not a gap that can be closed by doing more of what the industry already does. It requires a systematic restructuring of what the industry offers.

### The Four Actions Framework

How do you actually create a new value curve? Kim and Mauborgne offer a practical tool: the **Four Actions Framework**, which consists of four questions that force you to challenge every assumption your industry operates on.

**1. Which factors that the industry takes for granted should be ELIMINATED?**

This is the most radical question, and the one most companies never ask. They assume that if everyone in the industry does something, it must be necessary. But many "essential" industry features are actually historical accidents — legacies of assumptions that nobody has questioned. Eliminating them is not a cost-cutting exercise. It is a strategic move. It removes factors that the industry has competed on for years, making other companies' value curves obsolete.

**2. Which factors should be REDUCED well below the industry standard?**

Some factors are worth keeping but in smaller doses. The instinct in most companies is to add — more features, more service, more options. But more is not always better. Over-engineering a product or over-serving customers can create cost structures that make you vulnerable. Reducing certain factors and doing them just well enough can free up resources while still satisfying the core need.

**3. Which factors should be RAISED well above the industry standard?**

Some factors are worth emphasizing more than the industry does. But the goal is not incremental improvement. The goal is to raise a factor so dramatically that it redefines customer expectations. If the industry average for artistic quality in a circus is a 3 out of 10, raising it to a 9 is not just a product improvement. It is a category shift.

**4. Which factors should be CREATED that the industry has never offered?**

This is the other radical question — the one that creates genuinely new value. What could you offer that no competitor has ever thought to offer? What need are customers not even expressing because they don't know it can be satisfied? Creation is what changes the shape of the value curve most dramatically.

Let's see how this framework works by looking at an industry you know well.

#### The Four Actions Framework: A Before-and-After Example

Consider the wine industry in the 1990s. Most wine was sold through specialized stores, priced on a spectrum from cheap table wine to premium vintages, packaged in traditional glass bottles with cork stoppers, and marketed based on region, vintage, and varietal — all factors that the industry had competed on for centuries.

Enter Yellow Tail, an Australian wine brand launched in 2001. Yellow Tail did not try to compete on the established factors. Instead, it applied the Four Actions Framework:

- **Eliminated**: Varietal complexity, vintage labeling, regional prestige marketing, oak aging claims
- **Reduced**: Wine expertise required to choose a bottle (simplified the label to one animal logo per wine), price range (one price point)
- **Raised**: Accessibility and ease of selection (one shelf position, consistent taste), fun and approachability of branding
- **Created**: A "why choose?" approach — one red, one white, one price, one personality. A wine that did not require the customer to know anything about wine.

The result was a value curve that looked nothing like the traditional wine industry. Yellow Tail did not try to compete on quality (it deliberately targeted a mid-range quality level). It did not try to compete on prestige (the branding was deliberately playful). Instead, it created a new demand pool: casual drinkers who found wine intimidating, beer and spirits drinkers who wanted a simple alternative, and everyday consumers who wanted a reliable, affordable wine they could buy at the supermarket without thinking.

Within two years, Yellow Tail was the number-one imported wine in the United States — not because it beat the French or Italian wines at their own game, but because it stopped playing that game.

### Value Innovation: The Cornerstone

Now we arrive at the most important conceptual move in Blue Ocean Strategy: **value innovation**.

Here is the assumption that Blue Ocean Strategy challenges directly: *that you have to choose between differentiation and low cost.*

This assumption is embedded in Porter's framework, and it is one of the most widely accepted beliefs in strategic management. Porter argued that companies face a fundamental choice. You can either differentiate — offer superior value that justifies a premium price — or you can achieve cost leadership — offer adequate value at the lowest possible cost. If you try to do both, Porter warned, you risk getting "stuck in the middle" — not differentiated enough to charge a premium, not low-cost enough to compete on price. Being stuck in the middle is a recipe for mediocre performance.

Kim and Mauborgne do not dispute that a company trying to simultaneously differentiate and minimize costs within an existing industry structure will struggle. Their argument is more interesting: they say that the way out of this trap is not to find a better balance within the existing structure. The way out is to change the structure itself.

Value innovation is the pursuit of differentiation *and* low cost simultaneously — not as a compromise between the two but as a breakthrough that makes the trade-off unnecessary. How? By eliminating and reducing the factors that the industry has historically invested in (which reduces cost) while simultaneously raising and creating factors that the industry has not invested in (which increases value for customers).

When you eliminate animals and star performers from a circus, you reduce costs dramatically. When you raise artistic quality and create a theatrical theme, you increase value dramatically. The result is not a cheap circus or a premium circus but a new category — something that offers more value than a circus *and* costs less to produce *at the same time*.

This is not the same as being "different." It is not the same as being "innovative." It is a specific structural move: restructuring the set of factors that define the industry's value proposition, such that the trade-off between value and cost disappears.

But the framework does not account for the possibility of restructuring the industry itself. Blue Ocean Strategy is not a different way of playing the existing game. It is a way of changing the game.

## Part B: Disruption Theory

Now let me introduce a second framework that approaches similar territory from a completely different angle.

In the mid-1990s, I was researching a puzzle that had bothered me for years. Why did so many good companies — well-managed companies, companies that listened to their customers, invested in R&D, and did everything business schools taught them to do — still get destroyed by technological change? The question led to a framework that became known as **disruption theory**, and it has been taught, debated, critiqued, and revised for three decades. I want to walk through it carefully, because it is both more subtle and more limited than its popular reputation suggests.

### Sustaining vs. Disruptive Innovation

The first thing to understand is a distinction that is often lost in popular usage. There are two different kinds of technological change, and they behave very differently in competitive markets.

**Sustaining innovation** makes good products better. It improves performance along dimensions that existing customers already value. A faster microprocessor. A sharper camera lens. A longer-lasting battery. A more fuel-efficient engine. These are sustaining innovations. They are the normal, healthy work of competitive markets. The vast majority of innovation in any industry is sustaining.

Sustaining innovations tend to be developed by incumbent companies. This makes intuitive sense. Incumbents have the resources, the customer relationships, and the technical expertise to make products better. They know what their customers want because their customers tell them. And because they are competing with other incumbents to serve those customers, they have a powerful incentive to keep improving. Sustaining innovation is the engine of progress in a competitive market.

**Disruptive innovation** is different. It does not make good products better. It makes simpler, cheaper, more convenient products that initially underperform on the dimensions that existing customers value most. A disruptive product is, by the standards of the established market, objectively worse. It cannot beat the incumbents at their own game, because it is not good enough.

Here is the counterintuitive insight: that inferior product is the one that eventually wins.

The logic is not mystical. It is structural. Disruptive innovations do not compete for the mainstream market. They compete against **non-consumption** — against people who are not using the product at all because the existing solution is too expensive, too complicated, or too inconvenient. The first personal computers were not sold to companies that were already using mainframes. They were sold to hobbyists, students, and small businesses that could not afford a mainframe. The first digital cameras were not sold to professional photographers. They were sold to consumers who had never owned a darkroom and did not care about grain quality.

By targeting non-consumption, the disruptive entrant buys time — time to improve the product, time to expand the market, time to move upmarket while the incumbents are moving upmarket in a different direction.

### The Innovator's Dilemma

The dilemma that gives my book its title is both simple and devastating. It arises from a conflict between good management and disruptive change.

Here is the dilemma stated plainly: *the very management practices that enable successful companies to thrive in their core business — listening to customers, investing in the most profitable opportunities, allocating resources efficiently — are precisely what prevent them from responding to disruptive threats.*

Let me unfold this step by step.

**Step 1: The incumbent listens to its best customers.** This is what good companies do. You ask your most profitable customers what they need, and you invest in innovations that serve those needs. Your most profitable customers are the ones who buy the highest-margin products in the most demanding segments of the market. They want better performance. So you invest in sustaining innovations that improve performance.

**Step 2: The disruptor enters from below.** A startup appears with a product that is worse than yours by every measure your best customers care about. It is cheaper, simpler, and less capable. You ask your best customers if they would ever use such a product. They say no. You believe them. You are right to believe them — the product really is worse.

**Step 3: The incumbent's resource allocation processes naturally favor the sustaining path.** Your budgeting processes, your decision-making frameworks, your organizational structures — all of them evolved to allocate resources toward the highest-return opportunities in your core business. A low-margin, small-market, unproven product does not clear any internal hurdle. The very processes that make your company efficient and disciplined in its core market act as a filter that screens out disruptive investments.

**Step 4: The disruptor improves.** Because the disruptor is competing against non-consumption, it has room to improve. It adds features. It increases performance. It moves upmarket, step by step, into the segments that the incumbent has been serving.

**Step 5: The incumbent is trapped.** By the time the disruptor's product is good enough for the incumbent's core customers, the incumbent has lost so much ground that it cannot respond without abandoning its existing business model. The margins in the disruptor's market are lower than the incumbent's cost structure can support. The customers are different. The channel is different. The entire economic logic is different. The incumbent is caught.

This is the Innovator's Dilemma. The incumbent does not fail because it is lazy, stupid, or poorly managed. It fails precisely *because* it is well managed. It fails because it does what any rational manager would do: listen to customers, pursue the highest margins, and allocate resources to the most promising opportunities. The very practices that produce success in stable markets produce failure in the face of disruption.

The disruptor enters below the line that represents the performance that mainstream customers need. It is not good enough for those customers — yet. But because the incumbent keeps pushing upmarket (improving products faster than customers actually need), a gap opens. The disruptor's trajectory eventually crosses the threshold, and the incumbent's customers start to switch.

### The Disruption Model Summarized

The full model has three key elements:

1. **Entrants attack from below.** They offer simpler, cheaper, more convenient products that are not good enough for the mainstream market but are good enough for non-consumers who were not being served before.

2. **Incumbents ignore them for rational reasons.** The disruptor's market is too small, the margins too low, the customers too unappealing. Every rational resource allocation process tells the incumbent to focus on its core business.

3. **Disruptors improve faster than customers' needs evolve.** This is the critical dynamic. Incumbents often overshoot the market — they keep adding performance that customers do not actually need. Meanwhile, disruptors close the gap from below. Eventually, the disruptor's product becomes good enough for the mainstream, and the incumbent's advantage evaporates.

The model explains patterns that otherwise seem puzzling. Why did steel minimills — which produced low-quality steel from scrap metal — eventually overtake integrated steel mills? Why did discount retailers eventually displace department stores? Why did desktop publishing software put typesetting companies out of business? In each case, the same structural logic played out: entrants from below, ignored by incumbents, eventually became good enough.

### Jobs To Be Done

There is a companion idea to disruption theory that I want to introduce because it changes how you think about competition and markets. It is called **Jobs To Be Done**, and it emerged from a question I was asked by a researcher at a fast-food restaurant chain.

The chain wanted to understand how to sell more milkshakes. They had studied their customers extensively. They had asked what flavor preferences people had, what price points they would accept, what size they preferred. None of this data had produced a breakthrough in sales.

We suggested a different question: what job do people hire a milkshake to do?

When they followed customers out of the restaurant, they discovered something surprising. About half of the milkshakes were bought in the morning, by people driving to work. These customers were alone in their cars, facing a long commute. They had a job to do: they needed something to keep one hand occupied and the other hand on the wheel. They needed something that would last the entire commute — not be finished too quickly like a banana, not make a mess like a bagel, not leave crumbs. They needed something to make the commute less boring.

The milkshake, it turned out, was competing not against other milkshakes or even other beverages. It was competing against bananas, bagels, donuts, coffee, and silence. The job was "keep me occupied during my commute." The milkshake fit the job better than the alternatives.

The insight of Jobs To Be Done is that customers do not buy products. They "hire" products to do a job. The job is the fundamental unit of analysis, not the product category, not the customer demographic, and not the industry definition.

This reframes competition entirely. A company that thinks it is in the "milkshake business" will compete against other milkshake companies. A company that thinks it is in the "commute-occupying business" has a much broader set of competitors and a much wider range of opportunities. More importantly, understanding the job reveals what aspects of the product actually matter for the customer — which features to invest in and which to ignore.

The connection to disruption theory is direct. Disruptors succeed not just because they make simpler, cheaper products but because they understand the job that non-consumers are trying to do. Mainframe makers thought they were in the "computing business." The personal computer succeeded because it understood the job: "write documents and run spreadsheets at my desk." That job did not require the power of a mainframe. It required something the mainframe could not provide: individual ownership, ease of use, and enough capability for the work a single person needed to do.

Every disruptive innovation can be understood as a new solution to a job that an existing industry was not serving well — usually because the industry had overshot the needs of its core customers and was serving them with a product that was far more powerful, more expensive, and more complex than what many people actually required.

## Part C: Modern Debates and Updates

### Does Disruption Apply Everywhere?

The popularity of disruption theory has been something of a mixed blessing. The more widely the theory was applied, the more it was stretched. By the 2010s, "disruption" had become a buzzword used to describe almost any startup that challenged an established industry — which is not what the theory actually predicts.

Two cases became particularly important tests of the theory's limits: Tesla and Uber.

**The Tesla Challenge**

Tesla was founded in 2003 with the mission of accelerating the world's transition to sustainable energy. Its first product, the Roadster (launched 2008), was a high-performance luxury sports car that cost more than $100,000. Its second product, the Model S (2012), was a luxury sedan priced above $70,000. Its third, the Model X (2015), was a luxury SUV. Only with the Model 3 (2017) did Tesla enter a price range that could be called mass-market.

This is the opposite of the classic disruption pattern. Disruptors enter from below, with worse products that are cheaper and simpler. Tesla entered from the top, with a product that competed on performance and luxury — and was more expensive than almost anything in its category. The Roadster was not a "good enough" electric car. It was a Ferrari-beating electric car.

Does this mean disruption theory is wrong? Not exactly. What it means is that Tesla's success does not fit the disruption-from-below model. Tesla disrupted on different dimensions:

- **The fueling infrastructure dimension**: Tesla built a proprietary network of Superchargers that solved the range-anxiety problem differently than any other electric vehicle manufacturer. This was not a cheaper solution. It was a better solution on a dimension that mattered to anxious potential buyers.

- **The software dimension**: Tesla treated the car as a software platform, delivering over-the-air updates that improved performance, added features, and extended functionality long after the customer had driven off the lot. This made the car better over time, a reversal of the traditional automotive experience in which cars degrade.

- **The distribution dimension**: Tesla bypassed the franchised dealership model entirely, selling directly to consumers. This eliminated a layer of cost and friction that traditional automakers were bound by legal agreements to maintain.

- **The manufacturing dimension**: Tesla vertically integrated further than any automaker since the early days of Ford, building Gigafactories that produced batteries, powertrains, and eventually vehicles under one roof.

None of these is a classic disruption move. Tesla did not attack from below. It attacked from the side — changing what competition meant in the automotive industry by competing on dimensions that incumbents were not organized to match.

The lesson is that disruption theory is a framework, not a law. It describes one pattern of competitive change — a powerful one — but it does not describe all patterns. Entering from below is sufficient for disruption but not necessary for competitive success.

**The Uber Challenge**

Uber presented a different challenge. By many accounts, Uber disrupted the taxi industry. It offered a simpler, more convenient, and (often) cheaper way to get a ride. It targeted non-consumption — people who did not use taxis because taxis were unreliable, hard to find, and inconvenient to pay for.

But here is the complication: Uber's success was not primarily driven by technology. It was driven by regulatory arbitrage. Taxi companies were required to hold medallions, carry expensive insurance, meet vehicle standards, and comply with pricing regulations. Uber entered the market by ignoring many of those regulations — not because it had a better technology but because it operated in a regulatory gray area. When regulators caught up (and they did, in many cities), Uber's cost structure changed.

I wrote about Uber in the later editions of *The Innovator's Dilemma*, and I said that Uber was not a classic disruptor. The reason was not that Uber was unsuccessful or unimportant. The reason was that Uber's mechanism of competition was different from the mechanism that disruption theory describes. Disruption theory describes a specific pattern in which technology enables a simpler, cheaper solution to improve along a trajectory that eventually overtakes incumbents. Uber's pattern was different: it used a business model that sidestepped regulatory constraints, and its ability to maintain its advantage depended on whether those regulatory constraints could be permanently removed.

This distinction matters because different patterns of competition require different strategic responses. If you are an incumbent facing a classic disruptor entering from below, your best response is to create a separate organization that can compete on the disruptor's terms. If you are an incumbent facing a regulatory arbitrageur, your best response may be political — working with regulators to close the loopholes. The two situations call for completely different strategies.

### Platform Disruption

There is a third pattern of competitive change that neither Blue Ocean nor classic disruption theory fully captures: platform disruption.

A platform is a business model that enables value creation between two or more groups of users. Uber connects drivers and riders. Airbnb connects hosts and guests. Amazon Marketplace connects sellers and buyers. YouTube connects creators and viewers. In each case, the platform does not own the assets (cars, rooms, inventory, production studios) — it owns the connection between the people who have the assets and the people who need them.

Platform disruption works differently from product disruption. The platform does not necessarily offer a better product than the incumbent. Airbnb does not offer better hotel rooms than the Ritz-Carlton. YouTube does not offer better television shows than HBO. Instead, the platform offers an entirely different value proposition: access to a much larger, more diverse, and more flexible set of options, enabled by the network effects that connect the two sides of the market.

The disruption happens at the level of the **value chain**. In a traditional business, the company controls the entire chain from production to distribution to customer relationship. In a platform business, the company controls the matchmaking mechanism and lets the assets and the customers find each other. This restructures the industry's cost structure and economics in ways that are difficult for traditional businesses to match.

The strategic implications of platform disruption are still being worked out, but some patterns are emerging:

- Platform businesses tend to be winner-take-most markets, because the value of the platform increases with the number of participants on both sides (network effects).
- Platform businesses often operate at negative margins on one side of the market (subsidizing drivers or hosts) to build scale on the other side.
- Platform businesses are difficult to dislodge once network effects kick in, but they are also difficult to start because both sides of the market need to join simultaneously (the "chicken-and-egg problem").
- The most successful platforms — Amazon, Google, Apple — increasingly combine platform economics with vertical integration, creating moats that pure platforms cannot match.

### First-Mover vs. Fast-Follower

A final debate deserves attention. Is it better to create a new market (to be the first mover into a blue ocean) or to let someone else prove the market and then enter with a better product (to be a fast follower)?

The evidence is more nuanced than either side of the debate suggests. The "first-mover advantage" was long treated as conventional wisdom: being first gives you brand recognition, customer lock-in, and a head start on the learning curve. But the data tells a more complicated story. Studies by Gerard Tellis, Peter Golder, and others found that first movers fail far more often than conventional wisdom assumes. In their study of 50 product categories, first movers failed in about half the cases. The long-term market leaders were often those that entered later, having learned from the first mover's mistakes.

Apple is the archetypal fast follower. Apple did not invent the digital music player (Rio did), the smartphone (BlackBerry did), the tablet (Microsoft did), or the smartwatch (Pebble did). But Apple entered each category with a product that was dramatically better than the first mover's offering, and Apple captured the majority of the profit in each category. The pattern is consistent: Apple lets the first mover prove the market, absorb the — experimentation costs, and learn what works. Then Apple enters with a product that is more polished, more integrated, and more user-friendly.

But this is not the same as being a copycat. Apple's fast-following works because they change the value proposition: they redesign, reimagine, and re-engineer the product to address the specific shortcomings of the first mover's offering. It works because they identify the job differently than the first mover did.

The lesson is not that first-mover advantage is a myth. It is that first-mover advantage is conditional. It depends on:
- Whether the first mover can build barriers to entry (patents, network effects, brand)
- Whether the market is a winner-take-most market (platforms) or a fragmented market
- Whether the first mover's mistakes create an opening for a better-designed product
- Whether the first mover can maintain the lead long enough for the market to mature

In blue ocean terms, creating a blue ocean is valuable — but capturing the value of that blue ocean requires more than being first. It requires building the moats that keep the ocean from turning red too quickly.

### The Critique of Blue Ocean

Now let me offer a critique that I believe is important.

Blue Ocean Strategy has been enormously influential, and it contains genuine insight. But it has also been used to justify strategies that are not blue oceans at all.

The central critique is this: are genuine blue oceans rare? And when they exist, do they stay blue long enough to matter?

Consider the evidence. Many "blue oceans" turn red very quickly. Groupon created the daily-deals category. Within eighteen months, there were hundreds of Groupon clones. The category became a red ocean of price competition, low margins, and customer fatigue. Was Groupon's blue ocean real or illusory? It was real for a moment — but not long enough to build a durable business.

This is a pattern. "Create a new category" is one of the most common strategic prescriptions, but most new categories do not stay new. Competitors swarm. The competitive factors that the pioneer eliminated or reduced get reintroduced by imitators. The blue ocean becomes a red ocean faster than the pioneer can build a defensible position.

The response from Kim and Mauborgne is that creating a blue ocean is not enough. You also need to build barriers to imitation — brand, network effects, proprietary technology, economies of scale — that make it hard for competitors to follow. But this response raises a further question: if you need barriers to imitation to sustain your blue ocean, then you are not really creating a market with no competition. You are building a competitive advantage within a market that happens to be new.

There is a second, more fundamental critique: are most so-called blue oceans actually just early-mover advantage in a market that was previously underdeveloped? The mobile payments industry, the digital health industry, the plant-based meat industry — are these blue oceans, or are they nascent markets that will eventually become as competitive as any other?

I think the honest answer is that most "blue oceans" are temporary. The value of Blue Ocean Strategy is not in finding permanent uncontested spaces (which probably do not exist) but in identifying opportunities to restructure value propositions in ways that temporarily create an uncontested space. The time you buy with that temporary advantage is what you use to build the barriers that will protect you when the competition arrives.

## Case Study 1: Netflix — The Three-Act Disruption

### Act 1: DVD-by-Mail Disrupts Blockbuster

When Netflix launched its DVD-by-mail service in 1998, the video rental industry was dominated by Blockbuster, a company with nearly 9,000 stores, $6 billion in annual revenue, and a business model that had worked for two decades. Blockbuster's model was built on a simple economic logic: you pay a few dollars to rent a movie, watch it, and return it within a few days. If you are late, you pay a penalty. The late fees were enormously profitable — they accounted for approximately 16 percent of Blockbuster's revenue and an even larger share of its profit.

Netflix's model could not have been more different. There were no stores. There were no late fees. You paid a monthly subscription fee (initially around $20) and received DVDs by mail, one at a time, with prepaid return envelopes. You could keep the DVDs as long as you wanted. When you mailed one back, Netflix mailed you the next in your queue.

By traditional measures, Netflix was worse than Blockbuster. You could not walk into a store and pick up a movie immediately. You had to wait one to three days for the DVD to arrive. You could not browse shelves; you had to plan your queue online. The selection — Netflix's initial inventory — was a fraction of what Blockbuster offered.

But Netflix was not competing against Blockbuster's core customers. It was competing against non-consumption. There were millions of people who found video rental inconvenient — people who did not live near a Blockbuster, people who resented late fees, people who wanted to discover movies from a broader selection than their local store could offer, people who wanted the convenience of not having to return anything. Netflix served those people.

Blockbuster had the opportunity to respond. In 2000, Netflix approached Blockbuster with a proposal to run Blockbuster's online brand for $50 million a year. Blockbuster declined. The logic was straightforward at the time: Blockbuster's core business was generating enormous cash flow, the online model was unproven, and the margins in the online business were lower than the margins in the store business. A rational resource allocation process — the same process that had made Blockbuster successful — said no.

By 2004, Netflix had 3 million subscribers and was growing fast. Blockbuster finally launched its own online DVD service. But Blockbuster was trapped by its own business model. The online service was priced to compete with Netflix, but its costs included the infrastructure of 9,000 stores and the overhead of a publicly traded company with decades of accumulated organizational structure. Blockbuster could not make the economics work because the online business did not cover the costs of the store business, and Blockbuster could not shut down the stores because the store business was still generating the majority of its revenue.

In 2010, Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy. Netflix had over 20 million subscribers and was worth more than $10 billion.

### Act 2: Streaming Disrupts DVD

This is where the story becomes more interesting for our purposes.

By 2007, Netflix was the clear winner in the DVD-by-mail market. It had the largest selection, the best recommendation engine, the most efficient logistics. It had won.

And Reed Hastings, Netflix's CEO, decided that the company needed to cannibalize its own business before someone else did.

The logic was clear to Hastings. DVD-by-mail was a transitional technology. Streaming video would eventually replace physical media — not in five years, not in ten, but eventually. And when that happened, Netflix's hard-won advantage in logistics, fulfillment, and postal optimization would become worthless. The company needed to build new advantages in streaming before the old advantages became irrelevant.

In 2007, Netflix launched its streaming service — initially as a free addition to the DVD subscription. The streaming catalog was small, the video quality was inconsistent, and most customers continued to rely on DVDs. But Netflix was making a deliberate strategic bet: invest in streaming now, accept the cannibalization of the DVD business, and build the infrastructure (content licensing, streaming technology, recommendation algorithms) that would be the foundation of the future business.

The bet required Netflix to make choices that were painful in the short term. Content licensing costs were enormous — billions of dollars in streaming rights for content that would be viewed by relatively few subscribers in the early years. The streaming technology required constant investment in infrastructure, bandwidth, and engineering talent. And the inevitable effect of building a successful streaming service was that fewer subscribers would pay for DVD service — reducing the revenue that funded the streaming investment.

This is the opposite of the Innovator's Dilemma. Netflix was not trapped by its own success. It actively undermined its own business model. It chose the future over the present.

By 2011, Netflix had separated the DVD and streaming businesses into different pricing plans — a decision that was unpopular with customers and temporarily hurt subscriber growth. But the strategic logic was clear: Netflix needed to understand the standalone economics of streaming, separate from the DVD subsidy. The "Qwikster" debacle (the ill-fated attempt to rebrand the DVD business) was a PR disaster, but the underlying strategy was sound.

By 2015, streaming had become Netflix's dominant business. DVD subscriptions were in terminal decline. Netflix had successfully transitioned its entire business model while continuing to grow. The company had disrupted itself.

### Act 3: Original Content Disrupts Hollywood

The third act began around 2012, when Netflix started to realize that its streaming business faced a structural vulnerability: it was entirely dependent on other companies for content.

Netflix's streaming library was built on licensing deals with studios like Disney, NBCUniversal, Warner Bros., and ViacomCBS. These studios controlled the content that Netflix's subscribers wanted to watch. They could — and eventually did — raise licensing prices, pull content to launch their own streaming services, and limit Netflix's access to the most valuable programming.

The strategic logic was clear: Netflix could not build a durable competitive advantage as a pure distributor of other people's content. It needed to own the content itself.

In 2013, Netflix launched *House of Cards*, its first major original series. The bet was enormous — $100 million for two seasons of a political drama starring Kevin Spacey (before the allegations that later ended his career) and directed by David Fincher. Netflix had no data on whether a streaming-only original could compete with traditional television. But the strategic logic was sound: own the content, own the relationship with the creative talent, own the intellectual property.

*House of Cards* was a hit. It was followed by *Orange Is the New Black*, *Stranger Things*, *The Crown*, and dozens of other originals across every genre and market. Netflix went from producing zero original content in 2012 to producing more original content than any studio in the world by 2020. In 2024, Netflix's content budget exceeded $17 billion — more than most countries' national broadcasters.

The move into original content transformed Netflix's competitive position. Instead of being a distributor dependent on studio licensing, Netflix became a vertically integrated entertainment company that controlled both production and distribution. The moat was not just the content itself but the data-driven approach to content creation — using subscriber viewing data to decide what shows to make, which talent to hire, and how to market each title.

The irony is profound. The company that disrupted Blockbuster by competing against non-consumption became, through three acts of strategic evolution, one of the most powerful incumbents in the global entertainment industry. Netflix is now the company that startups dream of disrupting.

### What Netflix Teaches Us About Disruption

Three lessons stand out.

**First: the most successful disruptors are willing to disrupt themselves.** Netflix did not try to preserve its DVD business as long as possible. It actively invested in the technology that would make DVDs obsolete. This is the hardest strategic move a successful company can make, because it requires accepting short-term pain for long-term survival. Most companies cannot do it.

**Second: disruption is not a single event but a sequence of strategic choices.** Netflix disrupted Blockstarter from below, then disrupted itself from the side, then disrupted Hollywood from above. Each disruption opened a new chapter. The company that looked like a disrupter in Act 1 looked like an incumbent by Act 3. The lesson: disruption is a process, not a state.

**Third: the barriers to imitation in each act were different.** In Act 1 (DVD), the barrier was logistics — the operational excellence of managing millions of discs, thousands of titles, and hundreds of distribution centers efficiently. In Act 2 (Streaming), the barrier was technology and licensing relationships. In Act 3 (Original Content), the barrier was capital, creative talent, and data. Netflix had to build entirely new capabilities for each act. The company's ability to learn new skills — to "re-found" itself repeatedly — was its deepest competitive advantage.

## Case Study 2: Cirque du Soleil — The Blue Ocean Made Real

### The Industry It Left Behind

Before Cirque du Soleil, the global circus industry was in a state that Kim and Mauborgne describe as "structural decline." The industry had been dominated for more than a century by Ringling Brothers and Barnum & Bailey, whose shows — elephants, lions, clowns, three rings, and the famous "Greatest Show on Earth" — had defined what a circus was. By the 1980s, that formula was fading. Audiences were shrinking, production costs were rising, and children — the core audience — had increasingly sophisticated entertainment alternatives in video games, theme parks, and television.

The industry's response to decline was predictable: cut costs, merge with competitors, and squeeze the remaining profit out of a shrinking market. Ringling Brothers merged with Barnum & Bailey. Other circus companies consolidated. The strategic focus was on doing the same thing more efficiently.

This is what a red ocean looks like at the end of its life: everyone competing for a shrinking pie, everyone fighting over the same customers with the same value proposition, everyone investing in the same factors (animals, star performers, multiple rings) while those factors generate less and less return.

### The Four Actions in Practice

Guy Laliberté, the founder of Cirque du Soleil, was a street performer who had never run a circus. That lack of industry knowledge may have been his greatest advantage. He did not know what a circus was "supposed" to look like. He did not know which factors were essential and which were optional. He approached the circus not as an industry problem — how to make a better circus than Ringling Brothers — but as a creative problem: what kind of entertainment experience would move people?

The result of that creative approach can be mapped directly onto the Four Actions Framework.

**ELIMINATED**

Cirque du Soleil eliminated three factors that had been central to the circus industry for over a century:

- **Animals.** No lions, elephants, tigers, or horses. This was transformative. Eliminating animals eliminated enormous costs: veterinary care, handlers, transportation, insurance, special facilities, and the legal and regulatory burden of animal welfare compliance. It also eliminated one of the most controversial aspects of the circus — animal rights objections that were increasingly turning audiences away. By 2015, Ringling Brothers had also eliminated animals, but it was too late.

- **Star performers.** Traditional circuses relied on the draw of famous names — the Flying Wallendas, Gunther Gebel-Williams. Cirque du Soleil deliberately did not promote individual performers. The troupe was the star. This eliminated the risk of losing key talent and allowed Cirque du Soleil to treat performers as interchangeable members of an ensemble, which made casting and scheduling dramatically simpler.

- **Aisle concessions.** The traditional circus model made significant profit from selling cotton candy, popcorn, and souvenirs to captive audiences. Cirque du Soleil did not eliminate concessions entirely but did not make them a central part of the experience. The focus was on the show, not the ancillary revenue.

**REDUCED**

Cirque du Soleil reduced two factors dramatically:

- **Humor and thrills.** Traditional circuses relied heavily on humor (clowns, slapstick) and thrills (dangerous stunts, the "will she fall?" tension of high-wire acts). Cirque du Soleil did not eliminate these entirely but reduced their prominence. The humor became more sophisticated, less reliant on physical comedy. The thrills became more artistic — the danger was there, but it was presented as part of an aesthetic experience rather than as raw spectacle.

- **Multiple rings.** The traditional circus operated in three rings simultaneously, competing for audience attention. Cirque du Soleil reduced this to a single stage. This was not a cost-cutting measure. It was a creative choice. A single stage allowed for a more focused, theatrical experience — one story, one mood, one visual composition at a time. It allowed Cirque du Soleil to compete with theater rather than with other circuses.

**RAISED**

Cirque du Soleil raised two factors to levels the industry had never seen:

- **Artistic richness.** The shows were not collections of acts but integrated artistic productions with original music (often performed live), elaborate costumes, sophisticated lighting design, and choreography that blended influences from ballet, contemporary dance, and performance art. The artistic quality was comparable to — and often exceeded — what audiences expected from Broadway theater.

- **Venue sophistication.** Traditional circuses performed under tents (the "big top") that were functional but not luxurious. Cirque du Soleil raised venue quality dramatically. The permanent shows in Las Vegas — *O* at the Bellagio, *Mystère* at Treasure Island, *Ka* at the MGM Grand — performed in theaters built specifically for them, with technical capabilities (hydraulic stages, aerial rigging, immersive sound) that rivaled the most sophisticated opera houses in the world.

**CREATED**

This is the most important quadrant. Cirque du Soleil created factors that had never been part of the circus industry:

- **Theme.** Each show had a narrative thread — a theme that unified the acts, music, costumes, and visual design. *O* was inspired by water. *Ka* told a story of twin siblings on an epic journey. *Mystère* explored the origins of life. The theme transformed the circus from a variety show into something closer to theater — an experience with emotional coherence, not just a parade of disconnected performances.

- **Refined environment.** Cirque du Soleil created an atmosphere of elegance and sophistication. The tent was not a striped canvas structure but a custom-designed pavilion. The staff wore costumes consistent with the show's theme. The food and beverage service, when available, was upscale. Everything communicated: this is not a circus. This is high art.

- **Multiple productions.** Traditional circuses had one show that toured. Cirque du Soleil created multiple productions simultaneously — permanent shows in Las Vegas and touring shows across the globe. Each production was a distinct artistic creation with its own theme, music, and visual identity. This allowed Cirque du Soleil to grow revenue not just by selling more tickets to the same show but by creating new shows.

### The Strategy Canvas

The power of Cirque du Soleil's strategy becomes visible on the Strategy Canvas. Plot the traditional circus value curve: high on animals, star performers, humor and thrills, and concessions. Now plot the Cirque du Soleil value curve: zero on animals and star performers, low on humor and aisle concessions, high on artistic richness and venue sophistication, high on the newly created factors of theme, refined environment, and multiple productions.

The two curves do not just differ in level. They differ in *shape*. The traditional circus curve is peaked in one set of factors; Cirque du Soleil's curve is peaked in a completely different set. The shape difference is the strategic insight made visible.

### Has Cirque du Soleil Sustained Its Blue Ocean?

This is an honest question, and the answer is more complicated than the romantic version of the story.

Cirque du Soleil grew spectacularly for two decades. From a single show in 1984, it expanded to dozens of productions, employed over 5,000 people, and generated annual revenues estimated at over $1 billion. It was sold in 2015 to a consortium led by TPG Capital and Fosun, in a deal that valued the company at over $2 billion.

But the blue ocean has not remained pristine. In the late 2010s, the company began to face challenges. Some of its Las Vegas shows were aging and losing audience appeal. The touring market became more competitive. The cost structure had grown significantly — Cirque du Soleil had become a large, complex organization with the overhead of a global entertainment company, not the lean creative startup it once was. The COVID-19 pandemic dealt a devastating blow: all shows were canceled, the company filed for bankruptcy protection in Canada and the United States, and thousands of employees were laid off.

The company emerged from bankruptcy in 2021 under new ownership and has slowly rebuilt. But the question remains: can a blue ocean be sustained indefinitely? The evidence suggests no — or at least, not without a constant process of renewal. Cirque du Soleil's original blue ocean was based on a specific value innovation that required fewer resources (no animals, no stars) and delivered more value (artistic quality, theme, venue sophistication). Over time, as the company grew, the cost structure crept up. The value proposition stayed strong, but the cost advantage eroded.

This is the natural life cycle of a blue ocean. It is not a permanent state. It is a window of opportunity that must be used to build a defensible position before the competition arrives and the cost advantage fades. Cirque du Soleil built a defensible brand and a unique creative capability (few competitors can match its scale of production). But the pure blue ocean — the uncontested market space — became less blue over time.

## Case Study 3: Tesla — The Disruption Debate

### The Problem Tesla Poses for Disruption Theory

Let me state the problem directly, because it is important for understanding both the power and the limits of disruption theory.

In 2003, when Tesla was founded, the global automotive industry was one of the most stable and concentrated industries in the world. A handful of companies — Toyota, Volkswagen, General Motors, Ford, Honda, Nissan — dominated the market. They had been competing on roughly the same dimensions for decades: horsepower, fuel efficiency, reliability, safety, design, and price. The basis of competition was well understood. The industry structure was stable. Innovation was incremental.

Into this settled industry came Tesla, a company whose founding premise was that electric vehicles could be better than gasoline cars — not just as good, not just cheaper, but better. The Roadster, launched in 2008, was faster, more efficient, and more technologically advanced than almost any production car on the market. It cost over $100,000.

This violates the classic disruption pattern in two ways.

First, Tesla did not enter from below. The Roadster was a premium product, competing at the high end of the market. The disruption theory pattern predicts that entrants succeed by starting with a simpler, cheaper product that incumbents ignore. Tesla did the opposite: it started with a product that was arguably more sophisticated and definitely more expensive.

Second, Tesla did not target non-consumption. There was no group of people who wanted a car but could not afford one, or for whom existing cars were too complicated. Tesla targeted the most demanding, most discerning customers in the industry — the buyers of high-performance luxury sports cars. These were the customers that incumbent automakers valued most.

If disruption theory predicted that Tesla would fail (because it did not follow the classic pattern), then disruption theory was wrong. Tesla succeeded spectacularly. By 2020, Tesla was the most valuable automaker in the world by market capitalization. In 2023, the Model Y was the best-selling vehicle on the planet.

So what went wrong with the theory?

### Disruption on Different Dimensions

The answer, I believe, is that Tesla disrupted the automotive industry on dimensions that the classic disruption framework did not fully capture. The theory was not wrong — it was incomplete.

Tesla's disruption happened on at least four dimensions that the traditional automotive industry was not organized to compete on:

**The powertrain dimension.** The internal combustion engine is one of the most complex and expensive systems in a car. It requires thousands of parts, sophisticated manufacturing, a complex supply chain, and decades of accumulated engineering expertise. The electric powertrain is dramatically simpler: a battery, a motor, and a controller. This simplicity has profound cost implications. A fully electric powertrain has roughly 90 percent fewer moving parts than an internal combustion engine and transmission. It requires less assembly labor, fewer suppliers, and less manufacturing complexity. Traditional automakers had spent the better part of a century optimizing their powertrain manufacturing — and that entire investment becomes a liability when the technology shifts.

**The software dimension.** Tesla treats the car as a software platform. The vehicle receives over-the-air updates that add features, improve performance, and fix problems — exactly like a smartphone. This inverts the traditional automotive model, in which the car's capabilities are fixed at the factory and degrade over time. Tesla's cars get better with age. Traditional automakers' cars do not. The software dimension also changes the economics: Tesla can generate ongoing revenue from software features (Full Self-Driving, acceleration boost) that traditional automakers have no equivalent for.

**The distribution dimension.** Tesla sells directly to consumers, bypassing the franchised dealership network that every other automaker is legally required to use. This is a structural cost advantage that is difficult for incumbents to replicate because it would require changing state laws in most of the United States. The direct model eliminates the dealer margin, gives Tesla control over the customer experience, and enables a build-to-order model that reduces inventory costs.

**The charging infrastructure dimension.** Tesla built its own network of Superchargers — a proprietary fast-charging infrastructure that (until recently) was exclusive to Tesla vehicles. This solved the chicken-and-egg problem that had plagued earlier electric vehicles: customers would not buy electric cars without charging infrastructure, and companies would not build charging infrastructure without electric cars on the road. Tesla built both simultaneously, investing billions in a network that gave its customers something no other electric vehicle could offer: the ability to travel long distances reliably.

These four dimensions of change do not fit neatly into the classic disruption-from-below model. But they share a common structure: in each case, Tesla competed on a dimension that incumbents were structurally unable to match, because responding would require them to cannibalize an existing competitive advantage.

The incumbents' dilemma was this: if they invested heavily in electric vehicle technology, they would cannibalize their profitable internal combustion engine business. If they invested in direct-to-consumer sales, they would disrupt their relationship with the dealership networks that were their primary sales channel (and, in many states, legally protected middlemen). If they invested in proprietary charging infrastructure, they would be building something whose value depended on selling electric vehicles — which competed with their core business.

Tesla succeeded not because it entered from below (it did not) but because it restructured the competitive dimensions that defined the industry, and the incumbents were structurally unable to respond quickly enough.

### The Vertical Integration Moat

There is one more element of Tesla's strategy that deserves attention: vertical integration.

Most automotive companies are primarily assemblers. They design vehicles but outsource the manufacturing of components to suppliers — Bosch for brakes, Denso for electronics, ZF for transmissions, LG for batteries. This model spreads risk, reduces capital requirements, and allows automakers to access the best technology from specialized suppliers.

Tesla chose the opposite path. It builds its own batteries (Gigafactories). It builds its own electric motors. It builds its own power electronics. It builds its own seats. It writes its own software. It operates its own retail stores and service centers. It built its own charging network. It even developed its own insurance.

Vertical integration at this depth is expensive in the short term. It requires enormous capital investment and absorbs management attention across a vast range of activities. But it creates a competitive advantage that is extraordinarily difficult for competitors to replicate. Traditional automakers cannot quickly build Gigafactories — they have supply contracts with battery manufacturers that run for years. They cannot easily hire software engineers at the scale needed to match Tesla's capabilities. They cannot quickly replace their dealer networks with direct sales.

This is not disruption in the classic sense. It is a different kind of strategic move: building a system of activities that is so tightly integrated, so internally consistent, and so capital-intensive that competitors simply cannot copy it in a reasonable timeframe.

### What Tesla Teaches Us About Disruption

Three lessons emerge from the Tesla story.

**First: disruption theory describes one important pattern, but it does not describe all patterns of competitive change.** Entering from below is a sufficient condition for disruption. It is not a necessary condition for competitive success. Companies can succeed by entering from the top, from the side, or by changing the dimensions of competition entirely.

**Second: the most powerful disruptors do not just offer a better product. They change what "better" means.** Tesla did not make a car that was slightly better on the dimensions that Toyota and Volkswagen competed on. It made a car that was worse on some traditional dimensions (range, refueling convenience, interior fit-and-finish in the early models) but better on dimensions that incumbents were not even competing on (software, charging infrastructure, direct sales experience). The disruption happened at the level of the competitive dimensions, not just the product.

**Third: disruption theory is a framework, not a law.** It is a way of seeing patterns in competitive markets. Like any framework, it illuminates some things and obscures others. The danger is not that the framework is wrong. The danger is that we assume it applies everywhere and in every situation, and we miss the patterns it does not capture.

I said this many times during my career: disruption theory is a theory, not a prophecy. It describes a mechanism that has been observed repeatedly in many industries. It does not predict that every successful startup is a disruptor, or that every disruptor must follow the same path. The world is more complex and more interesting than any single framework can capture.

## When These Frameworks Fail You

I have spent this chapter making the case for two powerful strategic frameworks. Let me now make the case for their limitations.

**The Blue Ocean critique: are black oceans really blue?**

The most honest assessment of Blue Ocean Strategy is that it identifies a real phenomenon — the possibility of creating new market space by restructuring value propositions — but overstates the frequency and durability of that phenomenon.

Most new markets are not uncontested. They are nascent markets that will eventually become just as competitive as any other. The start-up that thinks it is creating a blue ocean is often just early to a market that will turn red as soon as competitors arrive. The "blue ocean" label can be a way of avoiding competitive reality — a comforting story that allows strategists to ignore the fact that competitors are coming (or are already there).

The data supports this skepticism. A study by Zook and Allen of the world's largest companies found that less than 10 percent of sustained profitable growth came from entering new markets. Most growth came from defending and extending positions in existing markets. Blue oceans are real, but they are rare — and getting rarer as global competition intensifies and new markets are contested faster than ever.

**The Disruption critique: the theory is over-applied**

I said this earlier, and I will say it again because it matters: disruption theory is a specific theory about a specific mechanism of competitive change. It is not a synonym for "any successful startup" or "any innovation that challenges incumbents."

The theory has been over-applied. Not every startup failure by an incumbent is the "Innovator's Dilemma." Sometimes a product just is not good enough. Sometimes the market was not ready. Sometimes the execution was poor. Sometimes the incumbent failed for reasons that have nothing to do with the structural dynamics that disruption theory describes.

A 2014 study by Andrew King and Baljir Baatartogtokh examined 77 cases that had been described as "disruptions" in academic and popular literature. They found that fewer than 10 percent of them met all the conditions of the theory. Most cases described as disruptions were actually something else: sustaining innovation, market evolution, or simple competitive displacement.

This does not invalidate the theory. But it means we must use it carefully. When you see an incumbent struggling with a new competitor, ask: does the disruptor's product actually underperform on the dimensions that mainstream customers value? Is the disruptor targeting non-consumption? Is the incumbent ignoring the threat for rational economic reasons? If the answer to these questions is no, disruption theory may not be the right framework for understanding what is happening.

**Christensen's own response**

I have been asked many times whether the critiques of disruption theory bother me. They do not. I have always believed that theories are tools, not truths. A good theory simplifies reality to make it understandable and useful. No theory captures all of reality. If a theory does not have limitations, it is not a theory — it is a tautology.

When King and Baatartogtokh published their study, I wrote a response in the *Harvard Business Review* acknowledging that the theory had been over-extended and clarifying the conditions under which it applies. When people pointed to Uber and Tesla as counterexamples, I engaged with the arguments and adjusted my thinking. The purpose of theory is not to be right in every case. The purpose of theory is to be useful — to help people see patterns they would otherwise miss, and to give them a framework for making decisions under uncertainty.

When the theory stops being useful in a particular situation, put it down. Use a different framework. The best strategists are not the ones who have found the one true framework. They are the ones who have many frameworks and know which one fits which situation.

**The danger of using these frameworks to avoid competitive reality**

There is one more danger worth naming explicitly. Both Blue Ocean Strategy and Disruption Theory can be used to justify a dangerous form of strategic complacency.

"We are creating a blue ocean, so we don't need to worry about competitors." This is usually wrong. Competitors are coming. The question is not whether they will come but how fast and with what advantages.

"We are disruptors entering from below, so the incumbents will ignore us until it is too late." This assumes the incumbents have not read the same books you have. By the time the theory became popular, incumbents were already learning to spot disruptors early and respond. The theory changes the behavior of the people who have read it.

The best use of these frameworks is not to predict the future with confidence. It is to ask better questions. Instead of "is this a blue ocean?" ask: *what would it take to create a value proposition that no competitor is currently offering, and how long would that advantage last?* Instead of "are we being disrupted?" ask: *what non-consumption exists in our market, and what would it take to serve those customers profitably?*

The frameworks are tools for questioning, not for concluding.

---

## Pulling It All Together

Let me step back and connect the threads.

Blue Ocean Strategy and Disruption Theory start from different assumptions and use different language. Kim and Mauborgne emphasize the creation of new market spaces. I emphasize the dynamics through which simple, cheap products enter from below and eventually overtake incumbents. These are not the same theory, and they do not always lead to the same strategic conclusions.

But they share a deeper insight that I believe is the most important idea in this chapter: **the structure of an industry is not fixed. It can be changed. And changing it is often more powerful than competing within it.**

This is the insight that the traditional strategy frameworks miss. Porter's Five Forces analysis (which we will cover in Chapter 2) tells you how to navigate within an existing industry structure. It is excellent at that task. But it does not tell you how to change the structure itself. Blue Ocean and Disruption theory do.

The companies that create new worlds are the ones that understand this. Netflix understood that the structure of the video rental industry was not a law of nature. It could be changed — by eliminating stores, eliminating late fees, and creating a subscription model. Cirque du Soleil understood that the structure of the circus industry was not fixed. It could be restructured — by eliminating animals, raising artistic quality, and creating a theatrical experience. Tesla understood that the structure of the automotive industry was not immutable. It could be reshaped — by competing on software, charging infrastructure, and direct sales.

In each case, the strategic insight was not incremental. It was structural. The companies did not ask: how do we improve our position within the existing industry? They asked: how do we change the industry so that our position is inherently advantageous?

This is the essence of strategy at its most creative. It is not about winning the game. It is about changing the game.

But let me end this section with a note of caution. Structural change is powerful, but it is also difficult and rare. Most companies should not try to change their industry. Most companies should focus on building a strong position within the industry they already understand. The blue ocean that never materializes and the disruption that never arrives can be just as destructive as the ones that do.

The skill is knowing which situation you are in. And that skill — the skill of reading the structure of a situation — is what this book is really about.

---

## The One Thing to Remember

> The most powerful strategic move is not competing within an existing industry structure — it is changing the structure itself by creating new value propositions, serving non-consumers, or competing on dimensions that incumbents are structurally unable to match.

## How to Use This Tomorrow

1. **Map your industry's strategy canvas.** Identify the six to ten factors that your industry competes on. Plot the value curves of the three largest competitors. Where do they converge? Now ask: what would it take to create a value curve with a different shape — higher on some factors, lower on others, introducing factors that no one in the industry currently offers?

2. **Run the Four Actions Framework.** Take your own product or service and run it through the four questions. Which factors could you eliminate entirely? Which could you reduce? Which should you raise dramatically? What could you create that the industry has never offered? The hardest question is the first one: elimination. Most companies cannot bring themselves to eliminate anything they have always done. Start there.

3. **Identify your non-consumption.** Who is not using your product or service, and why? Are there people who could benefit from what you offer but cannot afford it, do not have access to it, or find it too complicated? These non-consumers represent your most promising disruptive opportunity. What would it take to serve them profitably at a lower price point and a simpler value proposition?

4. **Ask: are you overshooting your customers?** Plot your product's performance trajectory against your customers' actual needs. Is there a gap between what you are delivering and what customers can actually use? If you are overshooting, you are creating space for a disruptor to enter from below.

5. **Consider your "jobs to be done."** Pick one of your products and ask: what job do customers actually hire this product to do? Not the job you think it does, but the real job — the one you would discover if you followed customers home and watched what they do. Now ask: what other products could do that job? Your competition may be much broader than you think.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1: Draw Your Industry's Strategy Canvas**

Choose an industry you know well — ideally one you work in or interact with regularly. Identify the six to eight key competitive factors that define how competitors in that industry compete. On a scale of 1 to 10, rate the typical level of offering for each factor. Now draw the industry value curve by connecting the dots.

Next, imagine you are entering this industry as a new competitor. Using the Four Actions Framework, design a new value curve that diverges from the industry. Which factors will you eliminate? Reduce? Raise? Create? Draw your new value curve alongside the industry curve. Where do they diverge? What would it take to make your new value curve a reality?

**Exercise 2: The Disruption Diagnostic**

Identify a startup or new entrant that is competing in an established industry. Evaluate it against the disruption criteria:

- Is the entrant's product simpler, cheaper, or more convenient than the incumbents' offerings?
- Is the entrant targeting non-consumption — people who could use the product but are not currently served by the existing market?
- Are the incumbents ignoring the entrant because the market is too small, the margins too low, or the customers too unattractive?
- Is the entrant's product improving along a trajectory that will eventually meet mainstream customer needs?

If the answer is yes to all four, you have a classic disruptor. If not, what is the actual mechanism of competition? Is it platform disruption? Regulatory arbitrage? A change in competitive dimensions? The exercise is not to force the theory onto the situation — it is to use the theory to understand what is actually happening.

**Exercise 3: The Job-to-Be-Done Audit**

Pick a product you use regularly — a coffee maker, a fitness app, a note-taking tool. Follow three users of that product (or think about your own usage if you cannot observe others) and try to understand the job they are hiring the product for. What specific circumstances trigger the use? What functional, emotional, and social needs is the product fulfilling? What do they use when the product is not available? What alternatives do they compare it against?

Now ask: if you were reimagining this product from scratch based on the job, what would you change? What features are unnecessary because they do not serve the job? What features are missing because no one has thought about the job holistically?

**Exercise 4: The Disrupt-Yourself Challenge**

Imagine you are the CEO of your current organization. A startup has just entered your market with a simpler, cheaper version of your product that is targeting the customers you currently do not serve profitably. The startup is losing money on each customer but is improving rapidly. You have five years before the startup's product is good enough for your core customers.

What do you do? The obvious answer — "launch our own simpler, cheaper version" — is harder than it sounds, because your existing cost structure, margin requirements, and internal processes will resist it. Design a response that acknowledges the structural obstacles. Would you create a separate business unit? Acquire the startup? Accept lower margins in exchange for market share? There is no perfect answer. The exercise is to feel the weight of the dilemma.

## Further Reading

- **Blue Ocean Strategy** by W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne — The original book that introduced the concepts of red oceans, blue oceans, the Strategy Canvas, the Four Actions Framework, and value innovation. It is well-written, richly illustrated with examples (including the Cirque du Soleil case), and has been updated with new research and case studies in later editions. Read it for the framework and the examples; apply it with the skepticism I have suggested in this chapter.

- **The Innovator's Dilemma** by Clayton M. Christensen — The book that introduced disruption theory to the world. It focuses on the hard disk drive industry (among others) and builds the case that good management is the cause of failure in the face of disruptive change. I remain deeply attached to the arguments in this book, but I encourage you to read it alongside the critiques that have been published since. The theory has evolved, and so has my understanding of it.

- **The Innovator's Solution** by Clayton M. Christensen and Michael E. Raynor — A later book that goes beyond the problem (the Dilemma) to offer solutions: how to create disruptive growth, how to build organizations that can respond to disruption, and how to spot disruptive opportunities before they arrive. This is the more practical companion to *The Innovator's Dilemma*.

- **Competing Against Luck** by Clayton M. Christensen, Karen Dillon, Taddy Hall, and David S. Duncan — The most accessible book on Jobs To Be Done theory. It develops the theory in depth and offers a practical methodology for identifying the jobs that customers are hiring products to do. The milkshake story is in this book, along with many others.

- **"Why the Lean Start-Up Changes Everything"** by Steve Blank (Harvard Business Review, 2013) — A short, powerful essay that connects many of the themes in this chapter. Blank argues that startups are not smaller versions of large companies but fundamentally different entities that operate under conditions of extreme uncertainty. The lean startup methodology (build-measure-learn, minimum viable product, pivot) is, in many ways, a practical playbook for the kind of experimentation that both Blue Ocean Strategy and Disruption Theory call for.

- **"Stop Making Sense"** by Bill Janeway (also published as part of his book *Doing Capitalism in the Innovation Economy*) — A thoughtful critique of some of the assumptions underlying both Blue Ocean Strategy and Disruption Theory. Janeway argues that the role of the state and financial speculation in creating new markets is systematically underappreciated. It is a useful corrective for anyone who finds the frameworks in this chapter a little too neat.
