# Chapter 6: The Vital Signs — Reading Financial Statements

In the spring of 2001, *Fortune* magazine ran its annual "Most Admired Companies" survey. At number one on the list of "Most Innovative" was a company that had won that exact same honor for six straight years — the only company in the survey's history to hold the title that long. Its stock had returned nearly 1,500 percent over the previous decade. It employed 21,000 people across 40 countries. Wall Street analysts called it the future of American business. Its CEO, Jeffrey Skilling, had been named one of the 100 best managers in the world by *Industry Week*. The board's audit committee was chaired by a Stanford Business School dean. The company's accounting firm was Arthur Andersen — one of the five most respected audit firms on earth.

The company was Enron. And at the moment *Fortune* was publishing its praise, Enron was already a fraud.

Not a small fraud. Not a temporary accounting hiccup. It was the largest corporate bankruptcy in American history at the time — $74 billion in shareholder value, gone. Thousands of employees lost their life savings, much of which was invested in Enron stock that became worthless the day the company collapsed. The accounting firm that certified Enron's books, Arthur Andersen, was convicted of obstruction of justice and went out of business, putting 85,000 people out of work. The pension reform law that followed — the Sarbanes-Oxley Act — reshaped corporate governance for a generation.

Here's what makes the Enron story essential reading for anyone who wants to understand business. The fraud was not hidden in secret offshore accounts that investigators had to dig up with subpoenas. The fraud was hiding *in plain sight* — in Enron's financial statements. The tools Enron used — mark-to-market accounting, special purpose entities, off-balance-sheet debt — were not illegal in themselves. Many were, at the time, technically within the rules. But they were used to create a picture of the company that was fundamentally dishonest: a picture of steady growth, brilliant innovation, and endless profit in a business that was, underneath the accounting, struggling to generate real cash.

The Enron collapse is often described as a failure of regulation, or a failure of auditing, or a failure of Wall Street's due diligence. All of those things are true. But at a deeper level, Enron was a failure of *financial literacy*.

Thousands of sophisticated investors — pension funds, mutual funds, university endowments — held Enron stock. Dozens of Wall Street analysts followed the company. The board of directors included some of the most accomplished businesspeople in America. And virtually all of them were fooled, because they didn't know how to read a financial statement critically. They looked at the numbers and saw what Enron wanted them to see. They didn't ask the hard questions: Where is the cash? What are the related-party transactions? Why is revenue growing faster than receivables? How is the company recognizing revenue? They accepted the story the numbers told, instead of interrogating the numbers to find the real story.

The lesson is one I have learned over seventy years of reading financial statements: **financial statements are the vital signs of a business.** They tell you if the patient is healthy, sick, or dying. But like any set of measurements, they can be misleading if you don't know what you're looking at. A clever doctor can make a patient appear healthier than they are by measuring at the right time, choosing favorable metrics, or treating temporary symptoms. And a clever accountant — or a dishonest one — can do the same thing to a company.

This chapter is about learning to read the vital signs. Not just to see the numbers, but to understand what they mean. To know what "healthy" looks like. To spot the warning signs when something is wrong. And to recognize — as every honest investor must — what the numbers can tell you, and what they cannot.

I taught myself accounting when I was nineteen years old, working through the textbooks on my own because I knew that if I wanted to understand businesses, I needed to understand how the scorecard worked. I've been reading financial statements for more than seventy years now. I still read them the same way: cover to cover, line by line, with a skeptical eye and a simple question: *Does this story make sense?*

The three financial statements — the income statement, the balance sheet, and the cash flow statement — are not complicated once you understand what each one is designed to tell you. They are not mysterious. They are not beyond the reach of anyone willing to spend a few hours learning the basics. They are, quite simply, the scoreboard of business. And if you can't read the scoreboard, you can't tell who's winning.

---

## What We're Measuring — And Why

Before we look at any specific numbers, we need to understand what a financial statement actually is.

A financial statement is a report card on a business. It measures three things:

1. **How much money the business generated** over a period of time (the income statement).
2. **What the business owns and owes** at a specific point in time (the balance sheet).
3. **Where the cash actually went** during that period (the cash flow statement).

Think of it like your personal finances. You can measure your financial health in three ways. First, you can look at your income — how much you earned from your job, your investments, and any side businesses last month. That's your income statement. Second, you can look at what you own (your house, your car, your savings account) versus what you owe (your mortgage, your car loan, your credit card balance). That's your balance sheet. Third, you can look at your bank statements and see where your cash actually went — did you spend it on rent? On groceries? On investments? That's your cash flow statement.

A business is the same thing, just bigger. And just like your personal finances, you cannot understand the full picture by looking at any one measure alone. A person with a high income might be spending everything they make and saving nothing. A person with a lot of assets might be drowning in debt. A person with plenty of cash in the bank might have a terrible job with no future. Each statement tells you something different, and you need all three to understand the full story.

Let's start with the one most people think they understand — and most people get wrong.

---

## Section A: The Income Statement (Profit & Loss)

The income statement is the first financial statement most people look at, and the most commonly misunderstood. It is also the easiest to manipulate.

**What it tells you:** How much revenue the company generated over a specific period (a quarter or a year), how much it spent to generate that revenue, and what was left over as profit.

**What it does NOT tell you:** Where the cash actually went. This is the single most important thing to understand about the income statement. It uses something called "accrual accounting," which means revenue is recorded when it is *earned*, not when cash is received, and expenses are recorded when they are *incurred*, not when cash is paid. The income statement tells you whether the company *created value* for its shareholders. It does NOT tell you whether the company generated cash. And those two things are often very different.

I'll say it again because it's that important: **Net income is not cash flow.** A company can report enormous profits and go bankrupt. A company can report losses and be generating enormous cash. If you take only one thing from this chapter, take that.

---

### Revenue (The Top Line)

Revenue is the simplest concept in business. It is the money that comes in when you sell something. A lemonade stand sells cups of lemonade for $1 each. If it sells 100 cups, its revenue is $100. A car manufacturer sells vehicles for $40,000 each. If it sells 250,000 vehicles, its revenue is $10 billion. Simple.

But revenue is not as simple as it sounds, for one reason: **revenue recognition**. Companies get to decide *when* to record a sale as revenue. And those decisions can dramatically change the story the income statement tells.

The basic rule is that revenue is recognized when it is earned — meaning when the company has delivered the product or service to the customer, and the customer is obligated to pay. But "earned" is a squishier concept than it sounds.

Consider a software company that sells a three-year subscription for $36,000, paid upfront. When does the company record the revenue? If it records all $36,000 in the first month, the first year looks fantastic and the next two years look terrible. If it records $1,000 per month for 36 months, the revenue is steady. The actual "right" answer under accounting rules is to spread the revenue over the life of the subscription, because the company hasn't *earned* the full $36,000 until it has delivered three years of service. But companies have discretion, and aggressive companies push the boundaries.

This matters enormously. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, many technology companies were booking revenue from long-term contracts upfront, treating the full value of a multi-year deal as revenue in the quarter it was signed. This made their growth look spectacular. It was also, in many cases, misleading — because the costs of delivering those contracts hadn't been incurred yet, and some of those customers would never pay.

Enron took revenue recognition to an extreme with something called **mark-to-market accounting**. Under this approach — which was legal for energy trading companies at the time — Enron would sign a long-term contract to supply natural gas or electricity, estimate the total profit it expected to make over the life of the contract (say, 20 years), and book that entire estimated profit as revenue *in the current quarter*. Not the revenue from delivering the gas. The *estimated revenue from the entire future of the contract*.

You can see the problem. If a company can estimate its own future profits with no one checking whether those estimates are realistic, it can make any quarter look profitable. Enron used this to create an illusion of steady growth. When the actual contracts turned out to be less profitable than estimated — or when the counterparties couldn't pay — the illusion collapsed.

The lesson: revenue is real when a customer hands over cash for a delivered product. Everything else is a judgment call — and judgment calls can be manipulated.

---

### Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

Directly below revenue on the income statement, you find Cost of Goods Sold. This is the direct cost of producing whatever the company sells. If the lemonade stand buys lemons, sugar, water, and cups, those are its COGS. If the car manufacturer buys steel, glass, rubber, and the labor to assemble them, those are its COGS.

The more important number, though, is what comes next.

---

### Gross Profit and Gross Margin

**Gross Profit** = Revenue — Cost of Goods Sold.

This is the first and most important measure of profitability. It tells you how much money the company makes from its products *before* accounting for the overhead of running the business.

**Gross Margin** = Gross Profit / Revenue (expressed as a percentage).

This is the single most important number in the income statement. It tells you how much of each dollar of revenue the company keeps after paying the direct costs of production.

A high gross margin — say, 60 percent or more — tells you the company has pricing power. It can charge significantly more than it costs to make the product. This is characteristic of strong brands (Apple, Nike, Coca-Cola), software companies (Microsoft, Adobe), and businesses with proprietary technology.

A low gross margin — say, 10 percent — tells you the company is in a commodity business where price is determined by competition, not by the company's own power. Grocery stores operate on razor-thin gross margins. Airlines do too. These businesses are harder to make money in, because any increase in costs hits the bottom line directly.

Gross margin is the first place I look when I'm evaluating a business. A company with sustainably high gross margins has a moat. A company with low or declining gross margins has a problem — it's getting squeezed by competition, rising costs, or both.

Here's what the gross margin section of an income statement looks like:

```
REVENUE (Sales)                         $100,000,000
Cost of Goods Sold                      $ 40,000,000
────────────────────────────────────────────────────
GROSS PROFIT                            $ 60,000,000
Gross Margin                                   60%
```

---

### Operating Expenses (SG&A, R&D, Marketing)

Below gross profit, we get into the costs of *running* the business — the overhead that exists regardless of how many products the company sells.

**SG&A (Selling, General & Administrative):** This covers salaries of executives, salespeople, and administrative staff. Rent on offices. Marketing and advertising. Legal fees. Accounting. The corporate jet. Everything that isn't directly tied to making the product.

**R&D (Research & Development):** Money spent on designing new products and improving existing ones. For technology companies and pharmaceutical companies, this is often the largest expense. For a consumer packaged goods company, it's relatively small.

The important thing about operating expenses is that they are largely fixed — they don't go up and down with sales the way COGS does. A company can double its revenue without doubling its SG&A. That's the magic of operating leverage. When revenue grows and operating expenses stay flat, all the incremental profit drops to the bottom line.

---

### Operating Income (EBIT)

**Operating Income** = Gross Profit — Operating Expenses.

This is also called **EBIT** — Earnings Before Interest and Taxes. It tells you how much profit the company is generating from its *core operations*, before accounting for how the business is financed (debt vs. equity) and before taxes.

Operating income is the purest measure of whether the business itself is profitable. If a company has high net income but low operating income, it's making money from financial engineering — debt, investments, one-time gains — not from its core business. That is usually temporary and often dangerous.

---

### Interest and Taxes

Below operating income, we find two items that are not part of the core business.

**Interest Expense:** The cost of debt. If the company has borrowed money from banks or bondholders, it pays interest on that debt. This is listed separately from operating expenses because it's a financing decision, not an operating cost. A company with a lot of debt will have a large interest expense, which reduces net income.

**Income Tax:** The portion of profits paid to the government. Tax rates vary by country, industry, and corporate structure. The effective tax rate (actual taxes paid divided by pre-tax income) is often lower than the statutory rate because of deductions, credits, and tax-advantaged structures.

---

### Net Income (The Bottom Line)

**Net Income** = Revenue — All Expenses (including COGS, Operating Expenses, Interest, and Taxes).

Net income is what's left for the owners of the business — the shareholders. It is the most headline-grabbing number on the income statement. Analysts, journalists, and executives focus on it obsessively. And it is, by itself, almost meaningless.

I said earlier that net income is not cash flow. Let me be specific: net income is an *accounting construct*. It depends on a series of estimates, judgments, and assumptions:
- How long will the company's equipment last? (Depreciation)
- How many customers will fail to pay their bills? (Bad debt reserves)
- How much revenue from long-term contracts should be recognized this quarter? (Revenue recognition)
- When should the company record expenses for warranties, returns, and legal settlements? (Accruals)

Every one of those judgments can change the net income number. And every one of them can be manipulated by a management team that wants to tell a particular story.

This is why I never look at net income in isolation. I look at operating income, gross margin, and — most importantly — the cash flow statement, which we'll get to in Section C.

---

### Earnings Per Share (EPS)

**Earnings Per Share** = Net Income / Number of Outstanding Shares.

EPS is the most watched number on Wall Street. Every quarter, analysts forecast what EPS will be, and companies are judged by whether they "beat" or "miss" those forecasts. Executives' bonuses are often tied to EPS targets. Stock prices rise and fall based on EPS surprises.

Here's what you need to know about EPS: it is net income divided by shares outstanding. So it can be increased in two ways: (1) increase net income (hard), or (2) reduce the number of shares outstanding (easy, by buying back stock).

Many companies boost their EPS by borrowing money to repurchase their own shares. This reduces the number of shares, which pushes EPS higher, even if net income doesn't grow at all. This is not necessarily a bad thing — if the stock is undervalued, buybacks create value for remaining shareholders. But it means you have to look past EPS to understand whether the company is genuinely growing.

---

### A Worked Example: The Lemonade Stand Grows Up

Let's run through a complete income statement with numbers you can follow. I'll use a business that everyone understands.

**Sally's Lemonade Stand, Inc.**

This year, Sally sold 10,000 cups of lemonade at $2 per cup. She bought lemons, sugar, and cups for $0.50 per cup. She hired her little brother to help for $5,000 a year. She paid $2,000 for the rights to sell at the corner lot. She borrowed $10,000 from her parents at 5% interest to buy a fancy lemonade machine. And she pays 25% of her profit in taxes.

Let's build the income statement:

```
SALLY'S LEMONADE STAND, INC.
INCOME STATEMENT, YEAR ENDED DECEMBER 31

REVENUE (10,000 cups × $2)                          $20,000
Cost of Goods Sold (10,000 cups × $0.50)            $ 5,000
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
GROSS PROFIT                                         $15,000
Gross Margin                                           75%

Operating Expenses:
  Wages (little brother)                              $5,000
  Licensing fee                                       $2,000

TOTAL OPERATING EXPENSES                              $7,000

OPERATING INCOME (EBIT)                               $8,000
Interest Expense ($10,000 × 5%)                       $  500

PRE-TAX INCOME                                        $7,500
Income Tax (25%)                                      $1,875

NET INCOME                                            $5,625
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Shares Outstanding                                    1,000
Earnings Per Share                                    $5.63
```

This is a healthy business. Gross margin of 75% is excellent — Sally has pricing power. Operating income is 40% of revenue, which is strong. Net income is $5,625 on $20,000 of revenue. That's a 28% net profit margin. Most companies would kill for that.

But notice what the income statement does NOT tell us. It doesn't tell us how much cash Sally actually has in her pocket. She might have collected all $20,000 in cash (it's a lemonade stand, so she probably did). Or she might have sold 5,000 cups on credit to the office building across the street and not collected the money yet. The income statement treats both scenarios the same — revenue is $20,000 either way. The income statement doesn't tell you whether the little brother was paid in cash or if Sally still owes him. It doesn't tell you whether Sally spent the $10,000 borrowed from her parents on the lemonade machine or put it in her savings account.

That's why we need the other two statements.

## Section B: The Balance Sheet

If the income statement is the movie — showing what happened over time — the balance sheet is the photograph. It shows what the company owns and owes at a single moment in time.

**What it tells you:** The company's financial position at a specific date. Its assets (what it owns), its liabilities (what it owes), and its shareholders' equity (what's left for the owners).

**What it does NOT tell you:** Whether the company is generating cash (that's the cash flow statement). Whether the company is profitable (that's the income statement). It only tells you the stock, not the flow.

---

### The Fundamental Equation

Every balance sheet in the world obeys one equation:

**Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity**

This is not a suggestion. It is an identity. It must always be true. If it isn't, the balance sheet is wrong.

Let me explain why this works with a simple example. Suppose you buy a house for $300,000. You put $60,000 of your own money down and borrow $240,000 from the bank.

Your "balance sheet" looks like this:
- **Asset:** House worth $300,000
- **Liability:** Mortgage of $240,000
- **Equity:** Your $60,000 down payment

$300,000 = $240,000 + $60,000. The equation balances.

The logic is that the company's assets were paid for either by borrowing (liabilities) or by the owners (equity). Every dollar on the left side was supplied from somewhere on the right side. This is the fundamental insight of double-entry bookkeeping, which has been the basis of accounting since Luca Pacioli invented it in 1494.

---

### Current Assets vs. Fixed Assets

Assets are listed on the balance sheet in order of liquidity — how quickly they could be turned into cash.

**Current Assets** are assets that will be converted to cash within one year:

- **Cash and Cash Equivalents:** Money in the bank and very short-term investments. This is the most important asset on the balance sheet. It tells you how much breathing room the company has.

- **Accounts Receivable:** Money that customers owe the company for products sold on credit. This is revenue that has been earned (recorded on the income statement) but not yet collected in cash. Growing receivables faster than revenue is a red flag — it means the company is recognizing sales but not collecting money.

- **Inventory:** Finished goods, work-in-progress, and raw materials. Inventory is cash that hasn't been collected yet — it's sitting on shelves or in warehouses. If inventory grows faster than sales, it suggests the company is producing more than it can sell, which is a warning sign.

- **Prepaid Expenses:** Things the company has paid for in advance — insurance premiums, rent, subscriptions. These are assets because the company has the right to receive future services.

**Fixed Assets (or Property, Plant & Equipment):** Assets that will last longer than a year — buildings, factories, machinery, computers, vehicles, furniture. These are listed at their purchase price, minus accumulated depreciation (more on depreciation in Section D).

**Other Assets:** Goodwill (the premium paid for acquisitions — we'll talk about this in the red flags section), intangible assets (patents, trademarks, copyrights), and long-term investments.

---

### Current Liabilities vs. Long-Term Debt

Liabilities are obligations the company owes to others.

**Current Liabilities** are due within one year:

- **Accounts Payable:** Money the company owes to its suppliers. This is essentially free financing — the company receives goods or services and pays for them later.

- **Short-Term Debt:** Loans, lines of credit, and the current portion of long-term debt that must be repaid within 12 months.

- **Accrued Expenses:** Expenses that have been incurred but not yet paid — wages earned by employees but not yet paid, taxes owed but not yet remitted, interest accrued but not yet paid.

**Long-Term Liabilities** are due after one year:

- **Long-Term Debt:** Bank loans, bonds, and other borrowings with maturities beyond one year.

- **Deferred Tax Liabilities:** Taxes that will be owed in future years because of differences between accounting rules and tax rules.

- **Pension Obligations:** Promises to pay future retirement benefits to employees. These can be enormous for older companies with unionized workforces.

---

### Shareholders' Equity

Shareholders' equity is the residual — what's left after subtracting all liabilities from all assets. It represents the owners' stake in the business.

**Common Stock:** The money raised when the company originally sold shares to investors.

**Retained Earnings:** The accumulated profits the company has earned over its entire history, minus any dividends paid out. This is the single most important number in the equity section. It tells you how much value the company has created and kept over its lifetime.

If a company started with $1 million in capital and has earned $500 million in total net income over its history, paying out $200 million in dividends, its retained earnings would be $300 million. That $300 million belongs to the shareholders but has been reinvested in the business.

**Treasury Stock:** Shares the company has repurchased from investors. This is a subtraction from equity — the company spent cash to buy back its own shares, reducing the ownership base.

---

### Working Capital

**Working Capital = Current Assets — Current Liabilities.**

This is the fuel in the tank. It tells you whether the company has enough short-term assets to cover its short-term obligations.

Positive working capital means the company can pay its bills for the next twelve months. Negative working capital means it can't — it needs to borrow, sell assets, or raise more capital.

A company with growing sales needs growing working capital. If sales double, the company needs more inventory, more cash to cover receivables before customers pay, and more capacity to cover payables. This is why fast-growing companies often run out of cash even when they're profitable — their working capital needs are consuming all their cash.

Some businesses, remarkably, operate with negative working capital. Think about a supermarket chain like Walmart or a warehouse club like Costco. They buy products from suppliers on credit, sell them for cash within days, and don't pay their suppliers for 30-60 days. By the time the supplier is paid, Walmart already has the cash from the customer. This is a beautiful business model — the company's working capital is being financed by its suppliers. Negative working capital is a competitive advantage when it's sustainable.

---

### Book Value

**Book Value = Total Assets — Total Liabilities = Shareholders' Equity.**

Book value is what the accountants say the company is worth, based on the historical cost of its assets minus all the money it owes. It is often dramatically different from the company's market value (the stock price times the number of shares).

Why the gap? Because accountants value assets at their purchase price, adjusted for depreciation, not at their current market value. A company that bought a factory in 1985 for $5 million might have it on its books at $1 million after depreciation — even if the factory could be sold today for $50 million. More importantly, the most valuable assets of many companies — brand, customer relationships, proprietary technology, management talent — don't appear on the balance sheet at all, unless they were acquired.

Coca-Cola's brand is worth tens of billions of dollars. It doesn't appear on Coca-Cola's balance sheet, because the brand was built internally, not purchased. Berkshire Hathaway's insurance operations have decades of accumulated wisdom about risk pricing. That wisdom doesn't appear on the balance sheet either.

This is why book value is a floor, not a ceiling, for most great businesses. The market value is higher because the market recognizes assets that accounting rules don't capture.

### A Worked Example: The Lemonade Stand Balance Sheet

Let's continue with Sally's Lemonade Stand. At the end of the year, Sally has:

- $12,000 in cash (from her profits plus the borrowed money)
- $1,000 in accounts receivable (the office building hasn't paid yet)
- $500 in inventory (lemons, sugar, cups she hasn't used)
- A lemonade machine she bought for $10,000 (now worth $9,000 after a year of depreciation)
- $2,000 she owes to her brother (she pays him next week)
- $500 in taxes she owes but hasn't paid yet
- $10,000 she still owes her parents (the loan)
- She started the business with $5,000 of her own money, and has earned $5,625 in profit

Let's build the balance sheet:

```
SALLY'S LEMONADE STAND, INC.
BALANCE SHEET, DECEMBER 31

ASSETS
Current Assets:
  Cash                                            $12,000
  Accounts Receivable                             $ 1,000
  Inventory                                       $   500
  Total Current Assets                            $13,500

Fixed Assets:
  Lemonade Machine (cost $10,000                   $10,000
    less accumulated depreciation of $1,000)
  Net Fixed Assets                                $ 9,000

TOTAL ASSETS                                      $22,500

LIABILITIES
Current Liabilities:
  Accounts Payable (owed to brother)              $ 2,000
  Taxes Payable                                   $   500
  Total Current Liabilities                       $ 2,500

Long-Term Liabilities:
  Loan from Parents                               $10,000

TOTAL LIABILITIES                                 $12,500

SHAREHOLDERS' EQUITY
  Common Stock (Sally's original investment)      $ 5,000
  Retained Earnings (profits kept in business)    $ 5,000

TOTAL EQUITY                                      $10,000

LIABILITIES + EQUITY                              $22,500
```

Check the equation: $22,500 (Assets) = $12,500 (Liabilities) + $10,000 (Equity). It balances.

Notice a few things. Sally has $12,000 in cash, which is strong — she could pay all her current liabilities ($2,500) with cash and still have almost $10,000 left. Her working capital is $13,500 - $2,500 = $11,000 — plenty of fuel in the tank. And her book value (equity) is $10,000, which is more than double her original investment of $5,000. The business has created $5,000 of value on paper.

But the balance sheet is a photograph. To understand whether this business is really creating value, we need to see whether the profit on the income statement is translating into actual cash.

## Section C: The Cash Flow Statement (The Most Honest Statement)

I have been reading financial statements for over seventy years. I've owned dozens of businesses. I've made more bad investments than I'd like to admit. And if I could only look at one financial statement when evaluating a company, it would be the cash flow statement.

The cash flow statement is the most honest of the three statements. It is harder to manipulate than the income statement. It is harder to obscure than the balance sheet. Cash is a fact. Accounting is an opinion. The cash flow statement tells you where the cash actually came from and where it actually went.

Let me say this plainly: **net income is not cash flow.** I've seen profitable companies go bankrupt because they had no cash. I've seen companies report losses for years while generating mountains of cash. If you only look at net income — as most investors do — you are looking at the wrong number.

---

### Why the Cash Flow Statement Exists

The income statement uses something called **accrual accounting**. Under accrual accounting:
- Revenue is recorded when it is earned, not when cash is received.
- Expenses are recorded when they are incurred, not when cash is paid.

This is useful for measuring economic performance, but it creates a gap between profit and cash. Consider a simple scenario:

A construction company signs a contract to build a $10 million office building. It expects the project to take two years and cost $8 million, leaving a $2 million profit.

Under accrual accounting, the company might recognize $1 million of profit in year one (based on the percentage of the project completed) and $1 million in year two. The income statement shows steady profits.

But in reality, the company might spend $5 million on materials and labor in year one before receiving any payment from the client. In year one, the company is cash-negative. When the client pays upon completion in year two, the company becomes cash-positive.

The income statement shows $1 million profit each year — smooth, steady, reassuring. The cash flow shows negative $5 million in year one and positive $7 million in year two — lumpy, stressful, revealing.

The cash flow statement is designed to show you the reality that the income statement obscures.

---

### The Structure of the Cash Flow Statement

The cash flow statement is divided into three sections, each telling a different story.

---

#### 1. Cash Flow from Operating Activities

This is the most important section. It tells you how much cash the company's core business generated.

Operating cash flow starts with net income from the income statement, then adjusts for all the non-cash items and changes in working capital that make net income different from cash.

The most common adjustments are:

**Add back depreciation and amortization:** Depreciation is the gradual expensing of a fixed asset (a $100,000 machine that lasts 10 years is "depreciated" by $10,000 per year). But the cash was spent when the machine was bought — not spread out over 10 years. Depreciation is a non-cash expense, so it gets added back to net income.

**Add back other non-cash charges:** Stock-based compensation, write-downs of assets, and similar items are expenses on the income statement but don't involve cash leaving the company.

**Adjust for changes in working capital:** When accounts receivable increase, it means customers bought products but haven't paid yet — so cash is lower than the revenue recorded. When inventory increases, it means cash was spent to build inventory that hasn't been sold yet. When accounts payable increase, it means the company hasn't paid its suppliers yet — so cash is higher than expenses recorded.

Here's the key: **operating cash flow is the real profit of the business.** A company with strong and growing operating cash flow is healthy. A company with operating cash flow that consistently lags net income has an accounting problem.

---

#### 2. Cash Flow from Investing Activities

This section shows cash spent on or received from investments. The most important line item is **Capital Expenditures** (CapEx) — cash spent on buying or improving fixed assets like factories, equipment, and technology.

Capital expenditures are necessary for most businesses to grow and maintain their operations. But heavy CapEx requirements reduce the cash available to owners. A business that requires constant reinvestment (think airlines, steel mills, oil refineries) is less valuable than a business that generates cash with minimal reinvestment (think software, insurance, brand-name consumer goods).

This section also includes cash spent on acquisitions and cash received from selling assets or businesses.

---

#### 3. Cash Flow from Financing Activities

This section shows cash flows related to how the company is financed:

- Borrowing money (cash in) or repaying debt (cash out)
- Issuing new shares (cash in) or buying back shares (cash out)
- Paying dividends (cash out)

This section tells you how the company is funding itself. Is it relying on debt to grow? Is it returning cash to shareholders through buybacks and dividends? Is it raising new capital from investors?

---

### Free Cash Flow

**Free Cash Flow = Operating Cash Flow — Capital Expenditures.**

This is the cash available to the company after it has invested in maintaining and growing its asset base. It is the most relevant measure of cash generation for an investor, because it represents the cash that could theoretically be returned to shareholders.

A company with growing free cash flow is compounding its value. A company with flat or declining free cash flow is not creating value, regardless of what the income statement says.

---

### Why Profitable Companies Die

Here's a scenario that kills more businesses than any other: the company is profitable on paper but runs out of cash.

How can this happen? Easily. A company signs a big contract with a customer who will pay in 90 days. To fulfill the contract, the company needs to buy inventory, hire workers, and pay for equipment — all in cash, all before the customer pays. The income statement shows a nice profit. But the bank account is draining. If the company can't borrow enough to cover the gap, it's dead.

This is why rapid growth is dangerous. When a company is growing fast, its working capital needs can easily consume all its cash. Every new dollar of revenue might require 50 cents of investment in inventory and receivables before the cash comes in. The company needs to keep finding cash to fund growth — and if the cash stops, the growth stops.

The cash flow statement reveals this dynamic. If operating cash flow is negative while net income is positive, the company is "growing broke." If operating cash flow is negative while the company is buying back stock or paying dividends, management is borrowing from the future to satisfy today's shareholders.

---

### A Worked Example: Sally's Cash Flow Statement

Let's complete the picture for Sally's Lemonade Stand. We know from the income statement that net income was $5,625. We know from the balance sheet that:
- Cash increased from $0 (start of year) to $12,000
- Accounts receivable increased by $1,000
- Inventory increased by $500
- Accounts payable increased by $2,000
- Taxes payable increased by $500
- The lemonade machine was bought for $10,000
- $10,000 was borrowed from parents

Let's build the cash flow statement:

```
SALLY'S LEMONADE STAND, INC.
CASH FLOW STATEMENT, YEAR ENDED DECEMBER 31

CASH FLOW FROM OPERATING ACTIVITIES
  Net Income                                         $5,625
  Adjustments:
    Depreciation (the lemonade machine)              $1,000
    Increase in Accounts Receivable                  ($1,000)
    Increase in Inventory                             ($500)
    Increase in Accounts Payable                     $2,000
    Increase in Taxes Payable                        $  500

NET CASH FROM OPERATING ACTIVITIES                   $7,625

CASH FLOW FROM INVESTING ACTIVITIES
  Purchase of Lemonade Machine                      ($10,000)

NET CASH FROM INVESTING ACTIVITIES                  ($10,000)

CASH FLOW FROM FINANCING ACTIVITIES
  Loan from Parents                                  $10,000
  Dividends Paid                                          $0

NET CASH FROM FINANCING ACTIVITIES                   $10,000

NET CHANGE IN CASH                                    $7,625
  Cash at Beginning of Year                                $0
  Cash at End of Year                                $12,000
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
```

Notice what this tells us that the income statement did not. Sally's business generated $7,625 in cash from operations — more than the $5,625 in net income, because depreciation was a non-cash expense and because she delayed paying some of her bills. She spent $10,000 on the lemonade machine. She borrowed $10,000 from her parents. The net result is $7,625 more cash than she started with, ending at $12,000.

Free Cash Flow was $7,625 - $10,000 = negative $2,375. That's not a problem in year one because Sally borrowed to cover the machine purchase. But if she had to keep buying expensive equipment every year without borrowing, her free cash flow would be negative and the business would eventually run out of cash.

The cash flow statement is the truth teller. Look at the gap between net income and operating cash flow. In a healthy business, they should roughly track each other over time. If net income consistently outpaces operating cash flow, the company is using accounting magic to dress up its earnings. That magic always runs out eventually.

## Section D: How the Three Statements Connect

The three financial statements are not independent documents. They are three views of the same business, and they interconnect in specific, predictable ways.

Once you see these connections, you cannot unsee them. They are the skeleton of financial understanding.

### Connection 1: Net Income Flows to Retained Earnings

Net income from the income statement becomes part of retained earnings on the balance sheet. Specifically, retained earnings at the end of the year equals retained earnings at the beginning of the year, plus net income, minus dividends paid.

This is how the income statement connects to the balance sheet. The profit the company earns becomes part of what the company owes to its shareholders.

In Sally's case: she started the year with $0 in retained earnings. Net income was $5,625. She paid no dividends. Ending retained earnings: $5,625. That's exactly the number on the balance sheet.

### Connection 2: Cash on the Balance Sheet = Cash on the Cash Flow Statement

The "ending cash" number on the cash flow statement is the exact same number as "cash and cash equivalents" on the balance sheet.

In Sally's case: the cash flow statement shows cash ending at $12,000. The balance sheet shows cash of $12,000. They are the same number. If they weren't, the financial statements were prepared incorrectly.

### Connection 3: Depreciation Appears on All Three Statements

Depreciation is a fascinating creature because it shows up on every financial statement, but in different ways:

- **Income Statement:** Depreciation is an expense, reducing net income.
- **Balance Sheet:** Accumulated depreciation is subtracted from fixed assets, reducing their book value.
- **Cash Flow Statement:** Depreciation is added back to net income, because it was a non-cash expense.

This is not accounting magic. It is simply recognizing that when a company buys a $100,000 machine, the cash goes out in the year of purchase (cash flow statement), but the expense is spread over the machine's useful life (income statement). The balance sheet tracks the remaining value of the machine as it gets used up.

In Sally's case: the income statement includes $1,000 in depreciation expense. The balance sheet shows accumulated depreciation of $1,000 on the lemonade machine. The cash flow statement adds back $1,000 to net income. The three statements are telling the same story from three different angles.

### Connection 4: Working Capital Changes Tie Everything Together

When accounts receivable, inventory, or accounts payable change, those changes appear in two places: the balance sheet (the actual balance changes) and the cash flow statement (the change is an adjustment to net income).

Think about it. If a company's accounts receivable increase by $5,000, that means the company recorded $5,000 more in revenue than it collected in cash. On the balance sheet, receivables are $5,000 higher. On the cash flow statement, operating cash flow is reduced by $5,000 to reflect that the cash wasn't collected. The increase on the balance sheet and the reduction on the cash flow statement are the same $5,000, recorded in two different ways.

This is the beauty of double-entry accounting. Every transaction is recorded in two places. Once you understand the connections, you can trace any number through all three statements.

---

### The Interlocking Logic

Here's the complete picture of how the three statements connect:

1. The **income statement** starts with revenue, subtracts expenses, and arrives at net income.
2. Net income goes to two places: some may be paid as dividends to shareholders, and the rest flows into **retained earnings** on the **balance sheet**.
3. The **cash flow statement** starts with net income, adjusts for non-cash items (depreciation, changes in working capital), and shows the actual cash generated by operations, investing, and financing.
4. The ending cash on the cash flow statement equals **cash** on the balance sheet.
5. The balance sheet shows everything the company owns and owes, reflecting the cumulative effect of all income and cash flow since the company was founded.

```
                       ┌──────────────────┐
                       │  INCOME          │
                       │  STATEMENT       │
                       │                  │
                       │  Revenue         │
                       │  — Expenses      │
                       │  = Net Income ───────┐
                       └──────────────────┘   │
                                              │
                                              ▼
┌──────────────────┐              ┌──────────────────────┐
│  CASH FLOW       │              │  BALANCE SHEET        │
│  STATEMENT       │              │                       │
│                  │              │  Assets               │
│  Operating CF    │              │   Cash ◄──────────────┼── (same number)
│   (includes NI,  │              │   Receivables         │
│    depreciation, │              │   Inventory           │
│    W/C changes)  │              │   Fixed Assets        │
│                  │              │                       │
│  Investing CF    │              │  Liabilities          │
│                  │              │   Payables            │
│  Financing CF    │              │   Debt                │
│                  │              │                       │
│  Change in Cash  │              │  Equity               │
│                  │              │   Retained Earnings ◄─┼── (net income flows here)
└──────────────────┘              └──────────────────────┘
```

Once you see this, financial statements become a map rather than a mystery. You can start anywhere — on the income statement, the balance sheet, or the cash flow statement — and trace any number to its counterpart on the other statements. The map is complete.

## Section E: Red Flags & Warning Signs

Over seventy years of reading financial statements, I've developed a set of instincts — things I look for, things that make me suspicious, things that tell me to dig deeper before I invest a dollar.

Here are the most important red flags. Most of them are not signs of fraud. They are signs of *weakness* — companies that are struggling, stretching, or misleading. But some of them are signs of fraud. And in both cases, the right response is the same: walk away.

---

### Red Flag 1: Revenue Growing Faster Than Receivables

Accounts receivable is money customers owe the company. When revenue grows, receivables normally grow with it — more sales mean more customers buying on credit.

But if revenue is growing at 20 percent while receivables are growing at 40 percent, something is wrong. It means the company is selling to customers who can't pay, or it's recognizing revenue before the cash is actually collectible.

This is one of the most common warning signs before companies collapse. As I have often said: "Turnaround seldom turns. You should never count on a troubled company being rescued. If a company has a lot of accounts receivable, you have to wonder whether they are ever going to collect."

---

### Red Flag 2: Net Income Growing Faster Than Operating Cash Flow

This is the single most important ratio to watch.

Compare net income to operating cash flow over a period of several years. In a healthy business, they should move roughly in the same direction. If net income is consistently and significantly higher than operating cash flow, the company is using accounting to create earnings that don't exist in cash.

Over the long run, operating cash flow cannot diverge from net income. Accounting catches up with reality eventually. The question is whether you notice the divergence before the market does.

---

### Red Flag 3: Goodwill Ballooning

Goodwill is created when a company buys another company for more than the fair value of its net assets. It goes on the balance sheet as an asset. The theory is that the company paid for something intangible — brand, customer relationships, future growth — that will generate future profits.

The reality is that goodwill is often a sign that management overpaid for an acquisition. And goodwill that was created by issuing stock is especially dangerous — the company swapped real ownership in the business for an overpriced acquisition. The acquiring company's shareholders are the ones who ultimately pay.

When I see a balance sheet where goodwill is a significant percentage of total assets — say, more than 25 percent — I get suspicious. I want to know what the company bought, why it paid so much, and whether the acquired businesses are actually earning their cost of capital.

---

### Red Flag 4: Related-Party Transactions

When a company does business with entities controlled by its own executives or directors, the transactions are rarely at arm's length. The company is almost certainly overpaying for services or selling assets at below-market prices.

Enron's special purpose entities were exactly this — partnerships run by Enron's own CFO, Andrew Fastow, that did business with Enron. The conflict of interest was flagrant. But the board approved the transactions anyway, and the auditors signed off.

If a company's financial statements reference related-party transactions, read the footnotes carefully. If the footnotes are vague, assume the worst.

---

### Red Flag 5: Frequent Changes in Accounting Methods

Companies that routinely change how they recognize revenue, depreciate assets, or value inventory are companies that are managing their numbers rather than running their business.

A legitimate company might change accounting methods once — when it adopts a new standard or when a fundamental change in its business makes the old method inappropriate. A company that changes methods every few years is trying to engineer better results. Run.

---

### Red Flag 6: Non-GAAP Metrics That Differ Significantly from GAAP

In every annual report and earnings release these days, you'll find "adjusted" or "non-GAAP" metrics — adjusted earnings, adjusted EBITDA, adjusted free cash flow. These are numbers that management has calculated using its own rules, not the standard accounting rules.

Some adjustments are legitimate. EBITDA (Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization) can be a useful measure of operating performance. But many companies adjust away things that are real costs — stock-based compensation, restructuring charges, goodwill impairments — and present a picture that is significantly rosier than GAAP reality.

I have a simple rule: compare the non-GAAP metrics to the GAAP numbers. If they're significantly different, ask why. If the company is consistently reporting "adjusted" earnings that are double its actual earnings, the only thing being adjusted is reality.

---

### Red Flag 7: The Buffett Rule

I have a rule I've shared before: "Beware of companies that need to explain their numbers."

If management spends pages of the annual report explaining why their carefully calculated GAAP earnings are not the "real" earnings, or why their accounting choices are not the "real" picture of the business, the numbers almost certainly don't tell a good story.

Great businesses don't need to explain their numbers. The numbers speak for themselves. A company that has to talk you out of what the numbers actually say is a company whose numbers are telling the truth — and the truth isn't what management wants you to hear.

---

## The Limitations of Financial Statements

I have spent most of this chapter telling you how to read financial statements. Now let me tell you what they can't tell you.

This is important. I have seen investors — including some very smart ones — make the mistake of believing that if they could read a financial statement perfectly, they would understand a business perfectly. They won't. Financial statements are necessary, but they are not sufficient. They are the scoreboard, not the game.

### What Financial Statements Don't Tell You

**1. They're backward-looking.** Financial statements tell you what happened in the past. They don't tell you what will happen in the future. The most beautifully profitable company in the world today could be disrupted by a technology that doesn't exist yet. The balance sheet is a photograph of yesterday, not a roadmap for tomorrow.

**2. They don't capture intangible assets.** The most valuable assets of most great businesses don't appear on the balance sheet. Coca-Cola's brand. Apple's ecosystem. Berkshire Hathaway's culture of capital allocation. The talent and enthusiasm of a company's people. Accounting rules are designed to measure things that can be bought and sold, not things that are built from within.

**3. They don't tell you about management quality.** The best numbers in the world won't save you if management is dishonest, incompetent, or both. And the worst numbers can be turned around by exceptional management. Charlie Munger and I have always said that we'd rather invest in a great business with an average manager than an average business with a great manager — but we'd much rather have a great business with a great manager. The financial statements don't tell you which kind of manager you're dealing with.

**4. They don't capture competitive position.** A company can have beautiful financial statements today and be losing its competitive position. The disruption might not show up in the numbers for years. Kodak's financial statements looked fine well into the 1990s. The digital camera was already eating its business, but the revenues and profits from film hadn't collapsed yet. The financial statements were telling a truth that was rapidly becoming irrelevant.

**5. They can be legally manipulated.** Everything I've described in this chapter — revenue recognition choices, depreciation schedules, accrual estimates, one-time charges — can be manipulated within the rules. GAAP is a set of guidelines, not a straitjacket. Creative accountants can make a company look much healthier than it really is, and they can do it legally.

I have said this many times, and I will say it once more here: **"You should invest in a business that even a fool can run, because someday a fool will."** Why? Because management changes. People retire, leave, or reveal themselves. But if the business itself has deep, durable competitive advantages — a moat — it can survive bad management long enough to find good management.

The financial statements can tell you whether the business has those advantages. High and stable gross margins, strong operating cash flow, a fortress balance sheet, and growing free cash flow per share — those are the signatures of a business with a moat. But the financial statements can only tell you about the past. Whether the moat will hold in the future is a question you have to answer yourself, by understanding the business, its industry, its competition, and the durability of its advantages.

## Case Study 1: Enron — The Anatomy of a Financial Fraud

Let's go back to the story I opened with. Enron was not a fraud that was hidden so cleverly that only forensic accountants could find it. Enron was a fraud that was hiding in plain sight. The tools were sophisticated, but the patterns were recognizable — if you knew where to look.

### What Enron Actually Was

Enron began life in 1985 as a regulated natural gas pipeline company. It owned physical assets — pipelines that carried gas from producers to utilities. This was a boring, steady, capital-intensive business. It generated reliable cash flow but limited growth.

Starting in the 1990s, Enron transformed itself into something else — an energy "trading" company. The idea was that Enron's expertise in energy markets could be applied to buying and selling energy contracts, not just transporting gas. Enron would become the middleman — the market maker — in a newly deregulated energy industry.

This was not a bad idea in theory. Enron's early success in energy trading was genuine. The problem was that Enron's management — particularly Jeffrey Skilling and Chairman Ken Lay — decided that the company needed to show 20 percent earnings growth every quarter, without fail. And when the actual business couldn't deliver that, they turned to accounting to create it.

### The Toolbox of Fraud

Enron used three primary accounting tricks, each of which distorted the financial statements in specific ways.

**Trick 1: Mark-to-Market Accounting**

I explained this earlier, but let's go deeper, because it's the most important accounting manipulation to understand.

In 1992, Enron persuaded the SEC to allow it to use "mark-to-market" accounting for its energy trading contracts. The rule said that Enron could estimate the total value of a long-term contract the day it was signed and book the entire estimated gain as current revenue.

Here's how it worked in practice. Enron signs a 20-year contract to supply natural gas to a utility company. Enron estimates the contract will generate $50 million in total revenue. Enron books the entire $50 million in *today's* revenue, adjusting only for the estimated cost of delivering the gas.

The problem, of course, is that no one can accurately predict the price of natural gas 20 years into the future. If gas prices fall, the estimate will be wrong. If the utility goes bankrupt, the contract won't be fulfilled. If Enron is overly optimistic in its assumptions, the revenue is imaginary.

Enron was aggressively optimistic in its assumptions. The company booked revenue from contracts that were years from being delivered, based on projections that were essentially guesses. When the actual results fell short — as they inevitably did — Enron had to write down the value of the contracts, creating losses that it then had to paper over with more aggressive accounting.

Mark-to-market accounting made Enron's income statement look spectacular. Revenue grew from $13 billion in 1996 to $101 billion in 2000. But the cash flow statement told a different story.

**Trick 2: Special Purpose Entities (SPEs)**

This is where Enron's fraud moved from aggressive accounting to outright deception.

Enron had a problem. It had taken on enormous debt to finance its trading operations and its international investments. That debt showed up on the balance sheet as liabilities. More debt meant lower credit ratings. Lower credit ratings meant higher borrowing costs. Higher borrowing costs meant less profit.

So Enron created hundreds of "Special Purpose Entities" — partnerships that were technically independent companies but were in fact controlled by Enron. Enron would transfer its debt to these partnerships, removing the debt from its balance sheet. The partnerships would then borrow money themselves (guaranteed by Enron) and use that money to buy assets from Enron or invest in Enron-related projects.

The most infamous of these was a series of partnerships run by Enron's own CFO, Andrew Fastow — named LJM1 and LJM2 (the initials of Fastow's wife and children). These partnerships did billions of dollars in transactions with Enron. They bought Enron's troubled assets (removing losses from Enron's books). They invested in Enron's projects (providing cash to Enron). And they were compensated with Enron stock and fees.

The conflict of interest was extraordinary. Fastow, as CFO, was responsible for protecting Enron's financial health. As the manager of the partnerships, he was responsible for maximizing the partnerships' profits. Those two roles were in direct conflict. Fastow made tens of millions of dollars from the partnerships while Enron — the company he was supposed to be serving — was collapsing.

On the balance sheet, the SPEs allowed Enron to show dramatically lower debt than it actually had. Enron's debt was $38 billion at the time of bankruptcy, but its balance sheet showed only $13 billion. The rest was hidden in the SPEs.

**Trick 3: Related-Party Transactions**

The partnership deals were inherently conflicted because the partnerships were run by Enron's CFO. But beyond the conflict of interest, the transactions themselves were designed to create fake revenue and fake profits.

Here's a simplified example of how it worked. Enron would create a partnership. Enron would sell an asset — say, a power plant in India — to the partnership for $100 million. The partnership would borrow the $100 million from a bank, with Enron's guarantee. Enron would record a $100 million gain on the sale (even though the asset might only be worth $60 million). The gain showed up on the income statement as profit. The debt was off Enron's balance sheet (it was on the partnership's books).

The problem: the asset wasn't really sold. Enron still bore the risk. And the partnership was paying for the asset with borrowed money that Enron had guaranteed. If the asset's value fell — or if the partnership couldn't make its debt payments — Enron was on the hook. The "sale" was a fiction.

### What the Financial Statements Revealed

Here is the critical lesson for investors. All of Enron's tricks created distortions in the financial statements that a careful reader could have spotted.

**The Income Statement Distortion:**

Enron's revenue was growing at an extraordinary rate — from $13 billion in 1996 to $101 billion in 2000. But this growth was not from building a real business. It was from booking the estimated future value of long-term contracts as current revenue. A careful reader would have noticed something strange: Enron's revenue per employee was among the highest of any company in America. In 2000, Enron reported $101 billion in revenue with 21,000 employees — nearly $5 million in revenue per employee. Compare that to Goldman Sachs at $1.2 million per employee, or General Electric at $500,000. Was Enron really five times more productive than Goldman Sachs?

**The Cash Flow Statement Distortion:**

This was the most obvious warning sign, and the one most analysts missed. Enron's net income was growing nicely. But its operating cash flow was negative for several of the years before its collapse. The company was reporting profits while burning cash.

Here are the actual numbers:

| Year | Net Income | Operating Cash Flow |
|------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1998 | $703M | $1.6B (positive — looks fine) |
| 1999 | $893M | $1.2B (positive — looks fine) |
| 2000 | $979M | -$114M (RED FLAG) |

In 2000, Enron reported nearly $1 billion in profit while burning $114 million in cash from operations. This is the classic sign of accounting manipulation — net income without cash. A company that is genuinely profitable collects cash from its customers. A company that is making up its profits does not.

The divergence between net income and operating cash flow was the single most important red flag, and it was hiding in plain sight on every quarterly cash flow statement.

**The Balance Sheet Distortion:**

Enron's balance sheet showed $13 billion in debt. But the footnotes revealed that the company had guaranteed billions more in debt held by its SPEs. A careful reader who went through the footnotes — and few did — would have seen that Enron's total debt exposure was closer to $38 billion.

Additionally, Enron's return on capital was deteriorating. Despite the explosive revenue growth, Enron was generating less and less profit per dollar of capital employed. In 1995, Enron generated a 13.6% return on capital. By 2000, that had fallen to 5.9%. The business was consuming more capital to generate less profit — a sign that the growth was not real.

### The Lesson of Enron

Enron is the most important case study in financial statement analysis because it demonstrates the gap between the story a company tells and the story the numbers tell.

The story Enron told was brilliant: we're revolutionizing energy markets, we're creating a new kind of business, the old rules don't apply to us. Investors and analysts bought the story. They wanted to believe. They looked at Enron's soaring revenue and earnings and saw confirmation of the narrative.

But the numbers told a different story. Operating cash flow was negative. Return on capital was declining. The balance sheet was loaded with hidden debt. The footnotes described related-party transactions that were impossible to understand. The company's own CFO was managing partnerships that did business with the company. Every one of these was a red flag — not conclusive proof of fraud, but a clear signal that something was wrong.

The lesson is simple and permanent: **financial statements are only as honest as the people preparing them.** Accounting rules are guidelines. Incentives matter. When management has extraordinary incentives to report certain numbers — and when the accounting rules give them room to engineer those numbers — the financial statements cannot be taken at face value.

As I have always said: "It takes 20 years to build a reputation and five minutes to ruin it." Enron's reputation was built on a story. The numbers were the first place the story started to crack.

## Case Study 2: Amazon's Deliberate "Losses"

In the first decade of the 2000s, a remarkable debate was happening on Wall Street. Amazon.com was reporting explosive revenue growth — from $2.8 billion in 2000 to $34 billion in 2010 — while reporting near-zero net income. In 2000, Amazon lost $1.4 billion. In 2001, it lost $567 million. In 2002, it lost $149 million. The company didn't report a meaningful GAAP profit until 2003, and even then, the profit was tiny compared to the revenue base.

To the casual observer — or the casual analyst — Amazon looked like a terrible business. Growing revenue but never making money? That's the classic pattern of a business that has no pricing power, no competitive advantage, and no path to profitability.

But the casual observer was wrong. Amazon was one of the best businesses of the twenty-first century. And the truth was visible in the financial statements — if you knew where to look.

### The Income Statement Deception

Amazon's income statement looked awful for years. Here's a simplified version of Amazon's financial results for fiscal 2003 (one of the first "profitable" years):

```
AMAZON.COM, INC.
SIMPLIFIED INCOME STATEMENT, 2003

Revenue                                           $5.3B
Cost of Goods Sold                                $4.0B

Gross Profit                                      $1.3B
Gross Margin                                       25%

Operating Expenses:
  Fulfillment (warehouses, shipping)              $0.6B
  Marketing                                       $0.2B
  Technology & Content                            $0.4B
  General & Administrative                        $0.1B

Total Operating Expenses                          $1.3B

Operating Income (Loss)                           $34M
Other Income (Expense)                            $30M

Net Income                                        $35M
```

Gross margin of 25% is fine — not great, not terrible. But net income of $35 million on $5.3 billion in revenue? That's a net profit margin of 0.7%. The company made seven-tenths of one cent of profit for every dollar of sales. By any normal standard, that looked like a struggling business.

But look at what Amazon was spending money on. Technology & Content: $400 million. That's not just maintaining existing systems. That's building the infrastructure for Amazon Web Services, which would become one of the most profitable businesses in technology history. Fulfillment: $600 million. That's not just shipping books. That's building a global logistics network that would eventually rival FedEx and UPS. The income statement showed these as expenses. But they were investments.

### The Cash Flow Statement Reveals the Truth

Now let's look at the cash flow statement for the same year:

```
AMAZON.COM, INC.
SIMPLIFIED CASH FLOW STATEMENT, 2003

CASH FLOW FROM OPERATING ACTIVITIES
  Net Income                                       $35M
  Depreciation & Amortization                     $76M
  Stock-Based Compensation                        $71M
  Changes in Working Capital                      $90M
  Other                                            $47M

NET CASH FROM OPERATIONS                          $319M

CASH FLOW FROM INVESTING ACTIVITIES
  Capital Expenditures (mostly fulfillment
    centers, technology infrastructure)           ($155M)

NET CASH FROM INVESTING                          ($155M)

CASH FLOW FROM FINANCING ACTIVITIES
  Other financing                                 ($145M)

NET CHANGE IN CASH                                $19M
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Free Cash Flow ($319M - $155M)                   $164M
```

This tells a completely different story. Amazon generated $319 million in cash from operations — nine times its reported net income of $35 million. After investing $155 million in growth, the company had $164 million in free cash flow. This was not a struggling business. It was a business that was deliberately choosing to reinvest its cash flow into future growth.

The divergence between net income ($35M) and operating cash flow ($319M) was enormous — and it was a positive sign, not a negative one, because the divergence was driven by two things:

1. **Non-cash charges:** Depreciation and stock-based compensation were real costs on the income statement but didn't reduce cash. Depreciation reflected investments that had already been made in previous years. Stock-based compensation was issuing shares to employees, not paying cash.

2. **Working capital efficiency:** Amazon's business model — collecting cash from customers almost immediately (credit card payments) while paying suppliers in 30-60 days — created a natural cash float. As revenue grew, this float grew automatically, generating more cash without any additional effort.

Amazon was what I would call an "investment story" disguised as a "loss story." The income statement showed the investment as expense. The cash flow statement showed the truth: the underlying business was generating cash, and management was reinvesting every dollar they could into building a stronger competitive position.

### The Pattern Through Time

What's remarkable is that this pattern continued for more than a decade. Here are the numbers for 2006:

- Net income: $190 million.
- Operating cash flow: $702 million.
- Free cash flow: $398 million.

And 2010:
- Net income: $1.15 billion.
- Operating cash flow: $3.5 billion.
- Free cash flow: $2.5 billion.

In every year, operating cash flow dramatically exceeded net income. Amazon's real earnings were three to four times what the income statement showed. The company was one of the most profitable businesses in the world, masked by an accounting convention that treats growth investments as expenses.

### The Lesson of Amazon

Amazon teaches us the most important distinction in this entire chapter: **the difference between "this company is losing money" and "this company is investing money."**

A company that is losing money because its core business doesn't work — its customers aren't willing to pay enough to cover its costs — is a company that is dying. A company that is reporting low profits because it's pouring every available dollar into growth is a company that might be creating enormous value.

How do you tell the difference? The cash flow statement. If operating cash flow is strong and the company is investing that cash into assets that will generate future returns, the income statement losses are a choice, not a failure. If operating cash flow is also weak — if the company is borrowing money just to stay alive — the losses are the truth.

The second lesson is about how to think about depreciation. On the income statement, depreciation is an expense — it reduces earnings. But depreciation is the recognition of an investment that was already made. When Amazon builds a fulfillment center for $100 million and depreciates it over 20 years, the cash was spent in year one, but the expense is spread over two decades. The income statement shows a $5 million annual expense for that fulfillment center. The cash flow statement flows it through in the year of construction.

The right way to think about this: the investment decisions have already been made. The depreciation is just accounting for them. What matters for the future is whether the assets Amazon is building — fulfillment centers, AWS infrastructure, delivery networks — are generating returns that exceed their cost. Amazon's returns on invested capital, especially in AWS, have been extraordinary.

Jeff Bezos understood something that most CEOs don't: **profits are not the point.** The point is free cash flow per share. If you can grow free cash flow per share by investing today's profits in tomorrow's assets, you should do it, even if it makes your income statement look bad for a decade.

The market eventually figured this out. Amazon's stock price rose more than 100,000% from its IPO through 2024. The investors who read the cash flow statement instead of the income statement were the ones who benefited.

## Case Study 3: Apple's Cash Machine

Amazon was an investment story disguised as a loss story. Apple is something different: a cash machine of almost unprecedented scale.

Let's look at Apple's financial statements for fiscal year 2023 — a year that demonstrates why Apple is one of the greatest businesses in American history.

### The Income Statement: Pricing Power

```
APPLE INC.
SIMPLIFIED INCOME STATEMENT, FISCAL 2023

Revenue                                           $383.3B
Cost of Goods Sold                                $214.1B

GROSS PROFIT                                      $169.2B
GROSS MARGIN                                        44.1%

Operating Expenses:
  R&D                                              $29.9B
  SG&A                                             $25.1B

Total Operating Expenses                           $55.0B

OPERATING INCOME                                   $114.3B
  Other Income                                     $1.3B

PRE-TAX INCOME                                     $115.6B
  Income Tax                                       $17.3B

NET INCOME                                          $98.3B
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
```

Let's sit with those numbers for a moment.

**$383 billion in revenue.** That is more than the GDP of many countries.

**$169 billion in gross profit.** A 44.1% gross margin on hardware and services is extraordinary. Most hardware companies — Dell, HP, Lenovo — operate at gross margins of 15-25 percent. Consumer electronics companies are lucky to hit 30 percent. Apple is operating at a gross margin that looks more like a software company than a hardware company.

How does Apple achieve this? Three things:

1. **Pricing power.** The iPhone is a status symbol and an ecosystem play rolled into one. Customers are willing to pay $1,000+ for a phone because they trust Apple, they're invested in the App Store and iCloud ecosystem, and — let's be honest — the iPhone is genuinely the best phone for a large segment of the market.

2. **Economies of scale.** Apple sells 200+ million iPhones per year. The R&D cost of developing iOS is spread over 1+ billion active devices. The fixed costs of chip design, camera engineering, and software development are enormous, but per-unit, they're negligible.

3. **Services revenue.** Apple's Services segment (App Store, Apple Music, iCloud, AppleCare, Apple Pay, etc.) generated roughly $85 billion in revenue in 2023. Services have very high gross margins — probably 70-75 percent. As services grow as a percentage of Apple's revenue, the overall gross margin will keep rising.

**$114 billion in operating income.** This is not a typo. Apple generated $114 billion in profit from its core operations. The entire Dow Jones Industrial Average, taken together, has a combined operating income of maybe $800-900 billion. Apple alone accounts for more than 10 percent of that.

**$98 billion in net income.** Apple earned more money in 2023 than Coca-Cola, Disney, McDonald's, Nike, Starbucks, and Ford combined. Combined.

### The Balance Sheet: A Fortress

```
APPLE INC.
SIMPLIFIED BALANCE SHEET, SEPTEMBER 30, 2023

ASSETS
  Cash & Marketable Securities                   $172.5B
  Accounts Receivable                              $29.5B
  Inventory                                         $6.3B
  Other Current Assets                             $14.7B
  Property, Plant & Equipment (net)                $43.8B
  Goodwill & Intangibles                           $14.5B
  Other Assets                                     $37.2B

TOTAL ASSETS                                      $318.5B

LIABILITIES
  Accounts Payable                                 $62.6B
  Short-Term Debt                                  $13.3B
  Other Current Liabilities                        $68.4B
  Long-Term Debt                                   $95.3B
  Other Non-Current Liabilities                    $58.7B

TOTAL LIABILITIES                                 $298.3B

SHAREHOLDERS' EQUITY                               $20.2B

LIABILITIES + EQUITY                              $318.5B
```

The most striking thing about Apple's balance sheet: **$172.5 billion in cash and marketable securities.** Apple had more cash than most countries' GDP. It was sitting on a pile of capital larger than the entire market capitalization of 95 percent of public companies.

For years, there was a controversy about this cash pile. Most of it was held overseas, where bringing it back to the United States would have triggered U.S. corporate taxes. Investors argued that Apple should either bring the cash home and pay the tax, or use it more aggressively for acquisitions or share buybacks. Apple eventually began returning enormous amounts of capital to shareholders — we'll see that in the cash flow statement.

The other notable thing is Apple's debt. Apple has $95.3 billion in long-term debt despite having $172 billion in cash. Why would a company with that much cash borrow money? The answer is tax arbitrage. Apple borrowed money in the United States at low interest rates and used it for share buybacks and dividends, while keeping its overseas cash offshore to avoid repatriation taxes. The interest on the debt was also tax-deductible. It was a sensible financial strategy.

The tiny shareholders' equity — $20.2 billion against $383 billion in revenue — reveals something important about Apple's capital allocation. Apple has spent hundreds of billions of dollars buying back its own stock. Every buyback reduces the equity on the balance sheet (treasury stock is subtracted from equity). Apple's equity has gone down over time not because the company is struggling, but because it has been returning capital to shareholders aggressively.

### The Cash Flow Statement: The Machine in Motion

```
APPLE INC.
SIMPLIFIED CASH FLOW STATEMENT, FISCAL 2023

CASH FLOW FROM OPERATING ACTIVITIES
  Net Income                                       $98.3B
  Depreciation & Amortization                      $11.5B
  Stock-Based Compensation                         $10.6B
  Changes in Working Capital & Other               ($6.1B)

NET CASH FROM OPERATIONS                          $118.7B

CASH FLOW FROM INVESTING ACTIVITIES
  Capital Expenditures                              ($11.0B)
  Purchases of Securities                          ($34.6B)
  Other Investing                                   ($0.5B)

NET CASH FROM INVESTING                           ($46.1B)

CASH FLOW FROM FINANCING ACTIVITIES
  Dividends Paid                                   ($15.1B)
  Share Repurchases                                ($73.8B)
  Debt Issuance (Net)                              ($5.5B)
  Other Financing                                   ($2.2B)

NET CASH FROM FINANCING                           ($96.6B)

NET CHANGE IN CASH                                ($24.0B)
  Cash at Beginning of Year                       $196.5B
  Cash at End of Year                             $172.5B
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FREE CASH FLOW ($118.7B - $11.0B)                $107.7B
```

This is the cash flow statement of a genuinely remarkable business.

**$118.7 billion in operating cash flow.** Apple generated nearly $119 billion in cash from its core business in a single year. That is $326 million per day. Every day. Seven days a week, 365 days a year, $326 million in cash arrived from customers buying iPhones, Macs, iPads, services, and accessories.

**$11 billion in capital expenditures.** For most companies, CapEx consumes a large portion of cash from operations. For Apple, CapEx is less than 10 percent of operating cash flow. Apple is an asset-light business — it doesn't own its own factories (Foxconn and other contract manufacturers make its products). It doesn't own vast distribution networks. This is a critical competitive advantage. Apple needs to invest only $1 in long-term assets to generate $10 in operating cash flow. That's the kind of capital efficiency that compounds wealth.

**$107.7 billion in free cash flow.** Apple's free cash flow was higher than its net income — again, the cash flow statement is more honest than the income statement. This is the hallmark of a business with strong working capital management: Apple collects cash from customers immediately (credit cards, Apple Pay) and pays suppliers on net-30 or net-60 terms. The float works in Apple's favor.

**$73.8 billion in share repurchases.** Apple bought back more than $73 billion of its own stock in a single year. That reduced the share count by roughly 3 percent. Over the past decade, Apple has bought back more than $600 billion in stock — the largest repurchase program in history. This has been enormously accretive to remaining shareholders. As Warren Buffett (a major Apple shareholder through Berkshire Hathaway), I can tell you this directly: Apple's buyback program has made each remaining share worth more because there are fewer shares sharing the same earnings.

**$15.1 billion in dividends.** Apple pays a modest but growing dividend. The company returns cash to shareholders through dividends and buybacks combined.

Apple ended the year with $172.5 billion in cash, even after spending $96.6 billion on capital returns ($73.8B buybacks + $15.1B dividends + $2.2B other). The cash pile is so large that Apple could return $100 billion per year and still have cash left over.

### What Makes Apple's Financial Profile Almost Unmatched

Apple's financial statements reveal structural advantages that very few companies in history have achieved:

**1. Pricing power in a commodity market.** The smartphone market is intensely competitive. There are dozens of Android manufacturers, ranging from Samsung to Xiaomi to Google. Yet Apple captures 80-85 percent of the global smartphone profits with only 15-20 percent of unit volume. This is pricing power of an extraordinary order.

**2. Asset-light, capital-efficient model.** Apple's manufacturing is outsourced. Its retail stores are smaller and more profitable per square foot than almost any other retailer. Its R&D, while significant ($30 billion), is a small fraction of revenue (8 percent). Apple generates $10 of operating cash flow for every $1 of CapEx.

**3. Recurring revenue in a product people replace every 3-4 years.** The iPhone upgrade cycle creates a natural annuity. Each iPhone user is likely to buy another iPhone. The App Store, iCloud, Apple Music, and Apple TV+ create recurring subscription revenue on top. The combination is extraordinarily powerful.

**4. Customer captivity with high switching costs.** Once a user is in the Apple ecosystem, leaving is painful. You'd lose your apps, your purchases, your data, your photos, your messages, your integration with other Apple devices. This creates retention rates that are far higher than any competitor.

**5. A capital allocation machine.** Apple's management has been disciplined about returning capital to shareholders. The buyback program has been executed at scale, consistently, without overpaying for acquisitions or making foolish strategic bets. This is rare in corporate America.

### The Lesson from Apple

Apple's financial statements reveal a business with a wide, durable moat. The numbers — 44 percent gross margins, $119 billion in operating cash flow, $108 billion in free cash flow, a fortress balance sheet with $172 billion in cash — are not the result of accounting tricks or one-time events. They are the result of structural competitive advantages that have been built and maintained over two decades.

The lesson is not simply "Apple is a great business." The lesson is that **the financial statements of a great business have a very specific look**:

- High and stable gross margins suggesting pricing power.
- Operating cash flow that consistently exceeds net income.
- Low capital expenditure requirements relative to operating cash flow.
- A strong balance sheet with manageable debt and ample liquidity.
- Growing free cash flow per share over time.

When you see a company with these characteristics, you've found something worth studying. When you see a company that lacks them — low margins, operating cash flow consistently below net income, heavy debt, weak cash generation — you've found something to avoid, regardless of how good the story sounds.

## Pulling It All Together: The Scoreboard of Business

Let me tell you what I've learned in seventy years of reading financial statements. The numbers matter, but they are never the whole story. They are the scoreboard — and you cannot run a business or invest in one without reading the scoreboard. But the scoreboard doesn't tell you how the game is being played. It only tells you who is winning.

The three financial statements give you three different views of the same business:

- The **income statement** tells you whether the business is creating value for its customers — whether its products are worth more to customers than they cost to produce. But it's an opinion, not a fact. It depends on estimates, judgments, and sometimes manipulation.

- The **balance sheet** tells you whether the business is financially strong enough to survive adversity. It shows you the foundation. If the foundation is solid — plenty of cash, manageable debt, positive equity — the business can weather storms.

- The **cash flow statement** tells you the truth. It shows you where the money actually came from and where it actually went. It is the hardest to manipulate and the most honest measure of a business's economic performance.

The connections between them create a complete picture. Net income flows from the income statement into retained earnings on the balance sheet. Cash on the balance sheet equals the ending cash on the cash flow statement. Depreciation touches all three. Working capital changes tie the income statement to the cash flow statement. Once you see these connections, you can't unsee them. Financial statements become a language you can read fluently rather than a code you have to decipher.

The red flags I've described — revenue growing faster than receivables, net income outpacing operating cash flow, ballooning goodwill, related-party transactions, frequent accounting changes, large gaps between GAAP and non-GAAP numbers — are not guarantees of fraud. They are warning signs that tell you to look deeper. Most companies with these warning signs are not frauds. They are simply weaker businesses than they appear. But in either case, the right response is the same: don't invest until you understand what's really happening.

The limitations of financial statements are equally important. They don't capture brand, culture, competitive position, or management quality. They don't tell you what will happen in the future. They can be legally manipulated within the rules. The most successful investors — Charlie Munger, and I hope I can include myself in this category — have succeeded not just by reading the numbers, but by understanding the *business* behind the numbers. We've invested in businesses we understood deeply, with durable competitive advantages and honest, capable management. The financial statements were our starting point, not our conclusion.

---

## The One Thing to Remember

> Financial statements are the language of business. You can't make intelligent decisions about where to put your money until you can read them fluently. But remember: the numbers on the page are not the business itself. They are a representation of the business — a map, not the territory. The best investors learn to read the map, then go see the territory for themselves. And they never forget that the most important asset in any business — the quality and integrity of the people running it — never appears on a balance sheet.

## How to Use This Tomorrow

1. **Pull up the last 10-K or annual report for a company you care about.** Most are available free on the SEC's EDGAR database or on the company's investor relations page. Start with the income statement. Write down the gross margin, operating margin, and net profit margin for the last three years. Are they stable, improving, or declining?

2. **Track the cash flow.** For the same company, compare net income to operating cash flow for each of the last three years. Are they roughly aligned? If operating cash flow is consistently lower than net income, dig into *why* — is it because of changes in working capital (growing receivables, growing inventory) or non-cash charges?

3. **Calculate free cash flow.** Subtract capital expenditures from operating cash flow. Is free cash flow positive? Is it growing? Is it higher or lower than net income? A company that consistently generates more free cash flow than net income is a company that's collecting its earnings in cash — which is a very good sign.

4. **Examine the balance sheet.** Look at the ratio of current assets to current liabilities (the current ratio). Is it above 1.0? Above 1.5? The higher, the safer. Look at the debt-to-equity ratio. Is the company carrying a lot of debt relative to its equity? If so, make sure the operating cash flow is sufficient to cover the interest payments.

5. **Run the Enron test.** If you could only ask one question about a company's financial statements, ask this: "Where is the cash?" Look at operating cash flow. Compare it to net income. If operating cash flow is lower, ask why. If the company can't generate cash from its core operations, nothing else matters.

6. **Look for Buffett's moat.** The best single indicator of a durable competitive advantage is high and stable gross margins. A company that can consistently earn 40 percent or higher gross margins has pricing power. A company whose gross margins are declining is losing that power. Start every analysis with gross margin trends.

**Exercise 1: The Lemonade Stand Grows**

Return to Sally's Lemonade Stand. This year, Sally sold 20,000 cups at $2.50 each (she raised prices). Her cost per cup rose to $0.75 (inflation hit the lemon market). She bought a second lemonade machine for $12,000 (depreciated over 10 years). She hired her cousin too, adding $4,000 to wages. The loan from her parents remains $10,000 at 5 percent. The tax rate is still 25 percent. Construct the full income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow statement. Show all three statements and the connections between them. Compare this year to last year. Is Sally's business more or less profitable on a per-cup basis? Is her cash position stronger or weaker?

**Exercise 2: The Enron Test**

Find the most recent 10-K for any company that has been in the news for accounting concerns, declining stock price, or significant financial restatements (some well-known ones: Nikola, WeWork, Luckin Coffee — but any company works). Read the cash flow statement. Compare net income to operating cash flow for the last three years. Calculate free cash flow. Does the company's cash generation support its reported earnings, or is there a gap? Write a paragraph explaining whether you would invest based on the cash flow evidence alone.

**Exercise 3: The Amazon Analysis**

Pick a high-growth company — ideally one with negative or low net income but strong revenue growth. (Examples from various years: Shopify, Uber, Peloton, Zoom, Snowflake — depending on where they are in their lifecycle.) Read the income statement and cash flow statement side by side. Is the company reinvesting in growth (high CapEx, high R&D, heavy working capital investment) or is it simply failing to generate cash from its operations? How would you tell the difference? Write your analysis.

**Exercise 4: Read the Footnotes**

Find the 10-K for a company that has significant goodwill on its balance sheet (S&P 500 companies with large acquisitions — think Kraft Heinz, AT&T, or any large pharmaceutical company). Go to the footnotes section on goodwill and intangible assets. Find how much of the company's total assets are goodwill. If the company had to write down that goodwill tomorrow — admitting the acquisitions were overpriced — how much would it reduce shareholders' equity? Would the company still have positive equity? This is the exercise that teaches you the difference between accounting value and real value.

**Exercise 5: Build Your Red Flag Checklist**

Based on the seven red flags in this chapter, create a one-page checklist you could use to quickly screen any company's financial statements. Which looks cleanest? What does that tell you about which industry has more "accounting risk"?

## Further Reading

- **The Interpretation of Financial Statements** by Benjamin Graham — This is the book I read when I was nineteen years old, and it remains the best introduction to financial statement analysis ever written. Graham (who was also Warren Buffett's teacher at Columbia) explains the three financial statements with extraordinary clarity. The book was published in 1937, but the fundamentals haven't changed. Start here.

- **Financial Statements: A Step-by-Step Guide to Understanding and Creating Financial Reports** by Thomas Ittelson — If Graham's book feels too dated, this is the modern equivalent. It walks through the three financial statements with simple examples (a lemonade stand, in fact) and shows how they connect. It's the book I'd give to a young person who wants to understand financial statements for the first time.

- **The Essays of Warren Buffett: Lessons for Corporate America** edited by Lawrence Cunningham — This is a collection of my letters to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders, arranged by topic. The sections on accounting, financial reporting, and "moats" are directly relevant to this chapter. If you want to understand how I actually read financial statements, read these essays. They're free online at Berkshire Hathaway's website.

- **The Smartest Guys in the Room** by Bethany McLean and Peter Elkind — The definitive account of the Enron collapse. McLean was the *Fortune* reporter who wrote the first article questioning Enron's financial statements in 2001. Her book reads like a thriller, but it's also a masterclass in what financial statement analysis can and cannot catch. Every investor should read it.

- **Creative Cash Flow Reporting** by Charles Mulford and Eugene Comiskey — An advanced book for those who want to understand how companies manipulate cash flow information. It's technical but invaluable. Most investors focus on income statement manipulation; the authors show that cash flow manipulation is also common — and more dangerous because most investors assume cash flow is honest.
