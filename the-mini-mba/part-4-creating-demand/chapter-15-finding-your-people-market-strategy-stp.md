# Chapter 15: Finding Your People — Market Strategy & STP

In 1984, Nike was in trouble. The company that had defined running culture in the 1970s, that had grown from a Blue Ribbon Sports distributorship into a $920 million athletic shoe company, was watching its market share evaporate. The culprit was Reebok — a British company that had spotted a trend Nike's executives had dismissed.

Aerobics.

The fitness craze of the 1980s was reshaping the athletic footwear industry, and Reebok had ridden it to astonishing success. The Reebok Freestyle, a leather aerobics shoe designed specifically for women, had become the must-have shoe of the fitness movement. Between 1983 and 1985, Reebok's annual revenue exploded from $13 million to $307 million — a 2,260 percent increase in two years. By 1986, Reebok had captured 30 percent of the U.S. athletic footwear market. Nike had fallen to 21 percent.

Phil Knight, Nike's founder and CEO, watched this happen with growing frustration. Nike had the resources, the brand recognition, and the athlete endorsements to compete. But it couldn't win in aerobics, because aerobics wasn't really about athletic performance — it was about fashion, style, and social participation. Nike's products were designed for runners, for basketball players, for serious athletes pushing their physical limits. The Freestyle was designed for women who wanted to look good in a leotard while doing grapevines in a mirrored studio. These were fundamentally different products serving fundamentally different customers.

Knight and his team faced a choice. They could chase Reebok into the aerobics market — designing fashionable shoes for the fitness-casual customer, competing on style and color rather than performance — or they could double down on Nike's existing identity and find a way to win that didn't require beating Reebok at its own game.

They chose option three.

Instead of asking "how do we beat Reebok in aerobics?" they asked a more fundamental question: "who are we actually trying to reach?" The answer changed everything. Nike's true customer was not the aerobics enthusiast. It was not the casual fitness participant. It was the *athlete* — defined not by skill level or competitive ambition, but by mindset. The person who pushes their limits. The person who wakes up at 5 AM to train. The person who believes that greatness is earned, not given.

This insight — that Nike's real market was a mindset rather than a demographic — led to the most famous advertising campaign in history: "Just Do It." Launched in 1988, the campaign didn't feature Reebok-style aerobics enthusiasts in colorful leotards. It featured real athletes — the eighty-year-old runner completing the San Francisco Marathon, the disabled athlete pushing through pain, the unknown basketball player dreaming of greatness. It featured Michael Jordan, whose first signature shoe had launched in 1985 and was already becoming a cultural phenomenon. It featured Bo Jackson, who played both professional football and baseball. It featured athletes who represented the human struggle to achieve, not the fashion of fitness.

The repositioning was not just an advertising campaign. It was a fundamental redefinition of Nike's market. By targeting the athlete mindset rather than the aerobics demographic, Nike escaped the trap of competing on Reebok's terms. It stopped trying to appeal to everyone and started speaking to a specific identity — an identity that millions of people wanted to claim for themselves, even if they never competed in a single organized sport. "If you have a body, you are an athlete," Nike's advertising declared. The message was inclusive in spirit but exclusive in identity: this brand is for people who take their bodies and their limits seriously.

The results were staggering. Between 1988 and 1998, Nike's revenue grew from $877 million to $9.6 billion — an 11-fold increase in a decade. The company's U.S. athletic footwear market share, which had fallen to 21 percent in 1986, climbed back to 43 percent by 1998. "Just Do It" became one of the most recognized slogans in history. And Nike transformed from a running shoe company into the most valuable sports brand on earth.

This is the power of knowing who you are actually serving. Not "everyone." Not "our target demographic." A specific person, with a specific need, at a specific moment. The discipline of finding that person — of dividing the market into groups, choosing which group to serve, and occupying a distinct place in their mind — is called STP: Segmentation, Targeting, and Positioning.

It is the most important marketing framework you will ever learn.

## 1. What Marketing Actually Is

Before we discuss STP, we need to clear something up. Most people — including most executives — think marketing means advertising, promotion, selling, or branding. It is none of those things. Or rather, it is all of those things plus everything else the company does.

Here is my definition, which I have been teaching for sixty years: **marketing is the whole business seen from the customer's perspective.** It is not a department. It is not a set of tactics. It is the fundamental orientation of the company toward the person who pays the bills.

The aim of marketing is to make selling unnecessary. This sounds paradoxical, so let me explain it carefully.

When you understand your customer so well that your product or service fits them perfectly — when it solves a real problem they have, at a price they are willing to pay, delivered where and when they want it — then selling becomes almost redundant. The product sells itself. The customer seeks you out. Your job is not to persuade people to buy something they don't need. Your job is to create something so aligned with their needs that the transaction feels natural, inevitable, even welcome.

This is very different from what most companies do. Most companies start with a product — something they can make, something they are good at, something they have invested in — and then go looking for customers to sell it to. They treat marketing as the department that "markets" whatever the factory produces. This is product-oriented thinking, and it is the root of most marketing failures.

Market-oriented thinking starts with the customer. It begins with a question: what do people actually need, want, and value? Then it works backward to the product, the price, the distribution, and the message. The product becomes the answer to a question the customer is already asking.

STP is the framework that makes market-oriented thinking operational. It forces you to stop treating "the market" as an undifferentiated mass and start making specific choices about who you serve and how.

## 2. Segmentation — Dividing the Market

No product serves everyone equally well. This is not a limitation. It is a fact of human diversity. People have different needs, different budgets, different values, different circumstances. A product that perfectly serves one group will be meaningless to another.

Segmentation is the practice of dividing a heterogeneous market into smaller, homogeneous groups whose members share similar needs, behaviors, or characteristics. The purpose is not to create more work — it is to focus your resources where they will have the most impact. A dollar spent marketing to someone who is a perfect fit for your product is worth ten dollars spent marketing to someone who is not.

There are four major bases for segmentation, and most good segmentation strategies use more than one.

### Demographic Segmentation

Age, gender, income, education, occupation, marital status, family size, ethnicity, religion. Demographics are the most common basis for segmentation because they are easy to measure, easy to find data on, and often correlate with meaningful differences in needs and behavior.

A luxury car company targets high-income professionals aged 45-65. A baby food company targets new parents. A retirement community targets people over 65. These are demographic segments, and they work because people at different life stages and income levels genuinely have different needs.

But demographics have limits. They tell you who someone is, but not what they want. Two 35-year-old men with the same income and education level can have completely different attitudes toward spending, risk, and lifestyle. Demographics are a starting point, not a destination.

### Psychographic Segmentation

Values, attitudes, interests, lifestyle, personality. Psychographics get at the *why* behind the purchase. They divide people not by what they look like on paper, but by how they think and what they care about.

Consider the difference between someone who buys a Toyota Prius and someone who buys a Ford F-150. Demographically, they might be identical — same age, same income, same region. Psychographically, they are different species. The Prius buyer values environmental responsibility, efficiency, and signaling their values to others. The F-150 buyer values capability, independence, and rugged self-reliance. A marketing message that resonates with one will alienate the other.

Psychographic segmentation is harder than demographic segmentation because the data is harder to collect. You cannot look at census data to find people who value environmental responsibility. You need surveys, purchase history analysis, behavioral data, and sometimes good old-fashioned intuition. But when it works, it works powerfully because it reaches people at the level of identity, not just circumstance.

### Behavioral Segmentation

Purchase behavior, usage patterns, brand loyalty, benefits sought, buying occasion. Behavioral segmentation divides people by what they *do* rather than who they *are* — and what they do is usually a better predictor of what they will do next.

Consider how a software company might segment its market:

A company might divide its market into non-users (never tried the product), trial users (tried once), light users (use monthly), heavy users (use daily), and former users (stopped using). Each of these groups needs a completely different marketing approach. Non-users need awareness and education. Trial users need onboarding and support. Light users need engagement and reminders. Heavy users need loyalty programs and advanced features. Former users need win-back campaigns and product improvement communications.

Behavioral segmentation is often the most actionable because behavior reveals intent. Someone who has visited your website three times in the past week is telling you something that no demographic or psychographic profile can match.

### Geographic Segmentation

Country, region, city, climate, urban vs. rural. Geography matters because physical location shapes needs, preferences, and access.

A clothing retailer needs different inventory for Tokyo in July (summer, humid) and Tokyo in January (winter, cold). A fast-food chain needs different menu items for India (where beef is culturally sensitive) and Texas (where beef is celebrated). A home heating company has customers in Minnesota and almost none in Hawaii.

Geography also matters for distribution. Selling to customers in dense urban areas is very different from selling to customers in rural areas. The logistics, the media channels, the competitive landscape — all of these shift with location.

### Choosing the Right Segmentation Base

How do you decide which basis for segmentation to use? The answer depends on your product, your market, and your objective. Here is a simple heuristic:

Use **demographic** segmentation when your product solves different problems for different life stages or income levels. A financial services company segments by age because a 25-year-old needs a different product than a 65-year-old.

Use **psychographic** segmentation when the product is tied to identity, values, or lifestyle. A car company segments by values because a Prius buyer and an F-150 buyer are making statements about who they are.

Use **behavioral** segmentation when you have data on what customers actually do. An e-commerce company segments by purchase frequency because past behavior is the best predictor of future behavior.

Use **geographic** segmentation when location drives needs or access. A clothing retailer segments by climate because winter in Chicago is not the same as winter in Phoenix.

In practice, most effective segmentation strategies use two or three bases together. A common and powerful combination: divide by geography first (which region?), then by behavior (how often do they buy?), then by psychographics (what do they value?). This layered approach produces segments that are both actionable and insightful.

### The Most Common Mistake

The most common mistake in segmentation is not segmenting at all. Startups and new product launches are especially prone to this. The founder believes their product is for "everyone" — a universal solution to a universal problem.

It is not.

If you believe your product is for everyone, you have not thought hard enough about who it is truly meant for. Every product has an ideal customer — the person who needs it most, values it most, and is most likely to buy it. If you cannot describe that person in specific detail, you are not ready to market your product.

The second most common mistake is segmenting too finely. Creating fifty micro-segments with tiny addressable markets is not a strategy — it is a way to waste marketing budget on customized messages that reach nobody. The art of segmentation is finding the balance between segments that are internally homogeneous and segments that are large enough to be commercially viable.

### How to Actually Segment a Market

Segmentation is easier to describe than to do. Here is a practical process that works across industries.

**Step 1: Start with the need.** Before you divide the market by demographics or geography, ask: what job are customers trying to get done? What problem are they trying to solve? The need should be your first segmentation criterion because it is the most fundamental. Everything else is detail.

**Step 2: Identify the variables that predict different needs.** Which characteristics — age, income, location, behavior, values — actually separate people with one need from people with a different need? Not all variables matter. The ones that predict meaningful differences in purchase behavior are the ones you want.

**Step 3: Build the segments.** Group customers who share the same need and the same predictive characteristics. Give each segment a name that captures its essence, not a statistical label. "Budget-conscious families" is a better segment name than "Segment A, ages 25-45, income $40K-$70K."

**Step 4: Profile each segment.** For each segment, describe: size, growth trajectory, profitability, purchase frequency, price sensitivity, distribution preferences, communication channel preferences, and competitive alternatives. The profile tells you whether the segment is worth targeting and how to reach it.

**Step 5: Validate against reality.** Segments that look clean on a spreadsheet often blur in the real world. Test your segments against actual customer behavior. Do customers in Segment A actually behave differently from customers in Segment B? If not, they are not separate segments.

A good segmentation exercise produces three to seven segments. Fewer than three, and you are probably not segmenting enough. More than seven, and you are likely over-segmenting.

## 3. Targeting — Choosing Your Segment

Segmentation reveals the map. Targeting is the choice of which territory to occupy.

Once you have divided the market into groups, you must decide which group (or groups) to serve. This decision is strategic, not tactical. It determines everything that follows: product design, pricing, distribution, communication, even the company's culture and identity.

There are four major targeting strategies, each with its own logic and trade-offs.

### Undifferentiated Targeting (Mass Marketing)

The company treats the entire market as one segment and offers a single product to everyone. Henry Ford's Model T is the classic example: "Any customer can have a car painted any color that he wants, so long as it is black." One product, one price, one message.

The advantage is scale. Producing one product for everyone is cheaper than producing variants for different segments. The marketing message is simpler. The supply chain is more efficient.

The disadvantage is vulnerability. If any competitor identifies a segment that the mass-market product does not serve well, they can enter with a tailored offering and take that segment. This is exactly what General Motors did to Ford in the 1920s, offering different cars for different price points and lifestyles. Ford's single-product strategy could not withstand the assault of segmented competitors.

Undifferentiated targeting makes sense when the market is genuinely homogeneous — when all customers want roughly the same thing. This is rare. Most markets are diverse enough that mass marketing leaves opportunities for competitors.

### Differentiated Targeting (Segmented Marketing)

The company targets two or more segments with different offerings for each. Procter & Gamble does this relentlessly: Tide for premium laundry customers, Gain for fragrance lovers, Era for budget-conscious buyers, Dreft for baby clothes. Each product is tailored to a different segment, and they compete against each other within P&G's own portfolio.

The advantage is reach. By covering multiple segments, the company captures more total market share and diversifies its risk. If one segment shrinks, others may grow.

The disadvantage is complexity and cost. Each segment requires its own product design, its own marketing campaign, its own distribution strategy. The company must maintain multiple brands, multiple supply chains, multiple teams. This is expensive, and it requires organizational sophistication that many companies lack.

Differentiated targeting works when the segments are large enough to justify the investment and when the company has the resources to manage the complexity. It is the dominant strategy for large consumer goods companies, but it is usually not appropriate for startups or small businesses.

### Concentrated Targeting (Niche Marketing)

The company goes after a single segment — a niche — and designs everything around serving that group better than anyone else. The Ferrari customer does not care about the Ford F-150. The F-150 customer does not care about the Ferrari. Each company serves one segment with laser focus.

The advantage is depth. A concentrated targeter can know its customers better, serve them more precisely, and build a stronger position in its chosen segment than any generalist could. The niche also creates a defensive moat: larger competitors may not find the niche worth entering, and customers may be unwilling to switch because the niche player understands them so well.

The disadvantage is dependency. If the niche shrinks — if tastes change, if technology makes the niche obsolete, if a larger competitor finally decides to enter — the concentrated targeter has nowhere to go. The classic example is the buggy whip manufacturer: the best buggy whip in the world was worthless after the automobile arrived.

Concentrated targeting is often the right strategy for startups and small businesses. Instead of trying to compete with established players across the entire market, find a segment they are ignoring and serve it brilliantly. Then grow from there.

### Micromarketing (Local or Individual Marketing)

The company tailors offerings to the needs of specific individuals or local groups. This is the most extreme form of targeting, made possible by modern data and digital technology. Amazon shows you recommendations based on your specific purchase history. Netflix customizes the thumbnails and recommendations you see. A local restaurant sends different offers to customers who visit weekly versus customers who visit once a year.

The advantage is relevance. When the product, price, and message are tailored to a specific person, the response rate is dramatically higher. The disadvantage is cost and complexity. Doing marketing at the individual level requires sophisticated data infrastructure, analytics, and automated execution systems.

A startup with limited capital should almost always start with concentrated targeting — find a niche, dominate it, then expand. ### Evaluating Segment Attractiveness

Not all segments are worth targeting. Even if you can identify a group with distinct needs, you must evaluate whether serving them makes commercial sense. Five criteria determine segment attractiveness:

**Measurable.** Can you identify and quantify the segment? If you cannot measure its size, you cannot evaluate whether it is worth serving.

**Substantial.** Is the segment large enough to be profitable? A segment of five people is not a market, no matter how well you serve them.

**Accessible.** Can you reach the segment through existing distribution and communication channels? A segment of vegan marathon runners is identifiable, but if you have no way to reach them with your message or deliver your product to them, they are not a viable target.

**Differentiable.** Does the segment respond differently to marketing stimuli than other segments? If your product appeals equally to men and women, splitting by gender is not a meaningful segmentation.

**Actionable.** Can your organization actually develop an effective program to serve this segment? A segment may be measurable, substantial, accessible, and differentiable — but if your company lacks the resources, capabilities, or strategic focus to serve it, you should pass.

## 4. Positioning — Occupying the Mind

Segmentation tells you who is out there. Targeting tells you who to pursue. Positioning tells you what to say — and more importantly, what to *be* — in the minds of your chosen customers.

Positioning is the act of designing the company's offering and image to occupy a distinctive place in the target market's mind. It is not about what you do to the product. It is about what you do to the *perception*.

The classic formulation comes from Al Ries and Jack Trout, who wrote the seminal book *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* in 1981. Their central insight: the human mind is a defense mechanism. It protects itself from the overwhelming volume of information in the environment by accepting only what matches its existing knowledge and experience. To succeed in marketing, you must position your product in the mental slot that the prospect already has for your category.

This is not about creating something new and different. It is about manipulating what is already in the mind. *Retrieving* existing connections, not making new ones.

### The Positioning Statement

A good positioning statement is a single sentence that captures the essence of your position. The standard formula:

> **For [target customer], [brand] is the [category] that [point of difference], because [reason to believe].**

Let's look at real examples:

**Volvo:** For safety-conscious families, Volvo is the automobile that offers the highest safety standards in the industry, because of our history of safety innovation including the three-point seatbelt (which we gave away to the industry freely because it was too important to patent).

**Dollar Shave Club:** For practical men who are tired of overpaying for razor blades, Dollar Shave Club is the razor subscription service that delivers high-quality blades for a few dollars a month, because we cut out retail markups and sell directly to you.

**Airbnb:** For travelers who want to experience a destination like a local, Airbnb is the accommodation platform that connects you with unique homes and experiences, because our hosts share their actual homes and local knowledge.

Notice what positioning statements do not contain: features. They do not say "Volvo has side airbags and anti-lock brakes." They say "Volvo is the safest." They do not describe the product. They describe the position. The features are evidence for the position, not the position itself.

### Positioning Maps

A positioning map (or perceptual map) is a two-dimensional chart that plots brands based on how customers perceive them on key dimensions. It reveals the competitive landscape visually and shows where there are gaps — positions that no brand currently occupies.

The judgment about whether the opportunity is real requires additional analysis: is there demand for an economy-performance car? Can it be built profitably at that price point? The positioning map is a starting point for strategic thinking, not a conclusion.

### Repositioning

Sometimes, a company's current position is not working. The market has changed. Competition has intensified. The company has outgrown its original identity. Repositioning is the act of changing the position — deliberately shifting how the target market perceives the brand.

Repositioning is one of the hardest things in marketing because it requires changing perceptions that have been built up over years or decades. It is easier to create a new position from scratch than to change an existing one. But when it works, it can be transformative.

Nike's "Just Do It" campaign was a repositioning. The company moved from "running shoe for serious runners" to "athletic brand for anyone with an athlete's mindset." This was not a new product — it was a new position. The shoes were the same. The customer was different.

Repositioning can also fail spectacularly. When JCPenney tried to reposition from "discount department store with coupons and sales" to "fair and square everyday pricing," customers rejected it. The repositioning violated what they believed about the brand. The lesson: repositioning must be grounded in something the customer can believe. If your position is too far from your current reality, you are not repositioning. You are just confusing your customers.

## 5. The 4Ps (And 7Ps) — The Marketing Mix

Segmentation, targeting, and positioning are the strategy. The marketing mix is the execution. Once you know who you are serving and what position you want to occupy in their minds, you need a set of tactical tools to deliver on that position.

The 4Ps framework — Product, Price, Place, Promotion — was developed by E. Jerome McCarthy in 1960 and popularized by Philip Kotler. It remains the standard framework for the marketing mix, though it has been extended to 7Ps for service businesses.

### Product

The product is the tangible or intangible offering that satisfies a customer need. It includes the core product (what the customer is actually buying), the actual product (features, design, quality, packaging, brand name), and the augmented product (installation, warranty, after-sale service).

Every positioning decision has implications for the product. If you are positioning as a luxury brand, the product must have premium materials, finer craftsmanship, and greater attention to detail. If you are positioning as a value brand, the product must be functional, reliable, and priced appropriately. The product is the physical manifestation of the position.

### Price

Price is the amount the customer pays. It is the only P that generates revenue — the other three generate costs. Pricing is the most powerful lever in the marketing mix because a small change in price has a large effect on profitability.

Price communicates position. A high price signals quality, exclusivity, and status. A low price signals affordability, value, and accessibility. The price must be consistent with the position. A luxury watch that costs $50 is not a luxury watch; it is a watch that cannot deliver on its positioning promise. A budget airline that charges premium prices is not a budget airline.

The relationship between price and positioning is bidirectional. Price supports the position, and the position justifies the price.

### Place

Place (also called distribution) is how the product reaches the customer. It includes the channels (retail, wholesale, direct, online), the logistics (warehousing, transportation, inventory management), and the coverage (intensive, selective, exclusive distribution).

Distribution is often the most difficult element of the marketing mix to change because it involves physical infrastructure and long-term relationships. But it is also one of the most important for positioning. A luxury brand sold through Walmart is no longer a luxury brand. A mass-market product available only in boutique stores cannot achieve the volume it needs.

Consider how Tesla changed the place variable in the automotive industry by selling directly to customers rather than through franchised dealerships. This was not a minor operational choice — it was integral to Tesla's position as a disruptive, innovative brand that did not play by the old rules.

### Promotion

Promotion encompasses all the activities that communicate the product's benefits and position to the target market. Advertising, public relations, sales promotion, direct marketing, personal selling, digital marketing, content marketing — all of these fall under promotion.

The key to effective promotion is consistency. Every communication should reinforce the same position. The message, tone, imagery, and channels should all reflect the same understanding of who the customer is and what the brand stands for. A cheap-looking advertisement undermines a premium position. A humorless, corporate tone undermines a fun, accessible position.

### The 7Ps: Extending for Services

In 1981, Bernard Booms and Mary Bitner extended the 4Ps to 7Ps, adding People, Process, and Physical Evidence. These three additional elements are critical for service businesses, where the customer experience is the product.

**People.** In a service business, the employees *are* the product, or at least an inseparable part of it. The friendliness of the hotel staff, the expertise of the consultant, the empathy of the healthcare provider — these are not add-ons to the service. They are the service. Hiring, training, and motivating the right people is a core marketing function, not just an HR function.

**Process.** The procedures, mechanisms, and flow of activities by which the service is delivered. The check-in process at a hotel, the checkout process at a grocery store, the claims process at an insurance company — these are marketing. A frustrating process damages the position. A smooth, pleasant process reinforces it.

**Physical Evidence.** The environment in which the service is delivered and any tangible cues that support the service's position. The cleanliness of the restaurant, the design of the waiting room, the quality of the printed materials. Physical evidence is particularly important when the product itself is intangible. You cannot touch a hotel stay, but you can touch the sheets.

### Internal Consistency

The most important thing about the marketing mix is internal consistency. All the elements must work together to reinforce the same position. This is exactly the same principle as the activity system in strategy (Chapter 1): each element makes the others more powerful, and inconsistency weakens the whole system.

Consider a hotel positioning itself as "luxury," but staffing its front desk with undertrained, low-paid employees (People), using cheap check-in kiosks instead of personal greeting (Process), and having worn carpet in the lobby (Physical Evidence). The inconsistency destroys the position. The customer experiences the reality, not the promise.

When the marketing mix is internally consistent, the position becomes believable and durable. When it is inconsistent, the customer receives mixed signals and trusts none of them.

## 6. The Marketing Environment

No marketing strategy operates in a vacuum. Every business operates within a larger environment of forces it cannot control but must respond to. Understanding those forces is the difference between a strategy that works because it is aligned with reality and one that fails because it ignored the world.

### PESTEL Analysis for Marketing

The PESTEL framework (Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal), which we discussed in Chapter 5, applies directly to marketing. Each dimension can create opportunities or threats for a market strategy.

**Political forces.** Trade policy, government stability, political movements. A company marketing products in a politically unstable region may need to segment based on political risk tolerance. A company whose brand takes a political stance — like Nike's support of Colin Kaepernick — must understand how that position affects different segments.

**Economic forces.** Inflation, interest rates, income distribution, unemployment. During a recession, price-sensitive segments grow and premium segments shrink. A company targeting luxury customers may need to reconsider its position when the economy contracts.

**Social forces.** Demographics, cultural norms, values shifts. The rise of health consciousness has reshaped food marketing. The increase in working women has reshaped household product marketing. The aging of the population in developed countries has created enormous opportunities in senior-focused products and services.

**Technological forces.** The internet, mobile, social media, AI, data analytics. Technology has changed every element of the marketing mix. Distribution can now be direct-to-consumer, bypassing traditional retail entirely. Promotion can be personalized and targeted at the individual level. Product development can be informed by real-time customer data.

**Environmental forces.** Climate change, sustainability, resource scarcity. More and more consumers are segmenting themselves based on environmental values. Companies that ignore this trend risk losing segments that care about sustainability. Companies that embrace it can build positioning around environmental responsibility.

**Legal forces.** Regulations, consumer protection laws, data privacy rules. GDPR in Europe, CCPA in California — these laws fundamentally change what companies can do with customer data, which affects segmentation, targeting, and personalization.

### Competitor Analysis

Marketing is not just about the customer. It is about the customer's alternatives. A complete market strategy must account for what competitors are doing, where they are positioned, and where they are vulnerable.

The most basic tool for competitor analysis is a simple mapping: who are your direct competitors (same product, same customer), your indirect competitors (different product, same need), and your potential competitors (could enter the market)? For each competitor, assess their positioning, their strengths, their weaknesses, and their likely response if you enter their space.

The crucial insight: **competition defines the position as much as the customer does.** Your position is not absolute — it is relative. You are not just "safe." You are "the safest among car brands." You are not just "fun." You are "more fun than the alternative." Understanding the competitive landscape tells you what positions are available, which are occupied, and where the gaps are.

A practical framework for competitor analysis in marketing begins with a simple grid:

| Competitor | Positioning | Target Segment | Key Strength | Key Weakness |
|------------|-------------|----------------|--------------|--------------|
| You | ? | ? | ? | ? |
| Competitor A | ? | ? | ? | ? |
| Competitor B | ? | ? | ? | ? |

Fill this out honestly. The most important column is "Key Weakness" — both your own and your competitors'. Your weakness tells you what you cannot be. Your competitor's weakness tells you where they are vulnerable. A marketing strategy that attacks a competitor's genuine weakness — while avoiding your own — is almost always a winning strategy.

### Product-Oriented vs. Market-Oriented Companies

I began this chapter with the distinction between product-oriented thinking and market-oriented thinking. Let me close this section by making it explicit, because I have spent my career arguing that the market-oriented company will always, over the long term, outperform the product-oriented company.

A product-oriented company asks: "What can we make?" Then it tries to find customers for what it makes. Its marketing department exists to sell whatever the factory produces. Innovation is driven by internal capabilities, not external needs.

A market-oriented company asks: "What do customers need?" Then it works backward to design the product, the price, the distribution, and the message. Its marketing function is not a department of salespeople — it is the strategist, the researcher, the voice of the customer inside the company. Innovation is driven by customer insight, not internal capability.

The difference shows up in every decision. The product-oriented company develops a new feature because the engineers think it is cool. The market-oriented company develops a new feature because customers have been asking for it. The product-oriented company chooses distribution channels based on what is easiest for the company. The market-oriented company chooses channels based on where the customer wants to buy.

The product-oriented company is wrong more often. The market-oriented company fails less because it starts with the answer to the hardest question: is there demand?

## 7. Limitations of STP

Segmentation, targeting, and positioning is a powerful framework. It has structured how I think about markets for decades, and I have seen it transform companies that apply it rigorously. But it is not a perfect framework. It has limitations, and ignoring those limitations leads to mistakes.

### Markets Don't Always Divide Neatly

Segmentation assumes that markets can be divided into discrete groups with clear boundaries. In reality, customer needs exist on a spectrum. The line between "budget-conscious" and "value-seeking" is fuzzy. The boundary between "luxury" and "premium" depends on who you ask.

Over-segmentation is a real risk. You can slice a market so finely that no segment is commercially viable, or you can create segments that exist in your spreadsheet but not in reality.

The solution is not to abandon segmentation, but to be pragmatic about it. Segments are analytical constructs, not natural kinds. They are useful to the extent that they help you make better decisions. If a segment is not driving action, it is not a good segment.

### Customers Can Be in Multiple Segments

A person can be a budget-conscious shopper for one purchase and a premium shopper for another. The same person who buys generic groceries might splurge on a luxury vacation. The same person who is price-sensitive about razor blades might be brand-loyal about sneakers.

STP assumes a certain consistency of customer behavior within a category. But people contain multitudes. Their purchasing behavior is influenced by context, mood, social pressure, and dozens of other factors that segmentation cannot capture.

This is why behavioral segmentation often outperforms demographic or psychographic segmentation. What people *do* is a better predictor of what they will do next than what they *are*.

### Positioning Can Backfire

A position that is too narrow can limit growth. If Volvo is "the safest car," what happens when the entire automotive industry raises safety standards to near-equivalence? Volvo's position becomes less distinctive. If a brand is "the affordable luxury option," what happens when the economy improves and customers want full luxury?

Repositioning is difficult and costly. Companies can become trapped in positions that were once valuable but are now constraints. The very clarity that made the position powerful becomes a cage.

The solution is to think of positioning not as a permanent identity but as an evolving proposition. Your position should be broad enough to allow growth and adaptable enough to respond to market changes. It should be anchored in something durable about your company — your capabilities, your values, your approach — rather than a narrow product or feature.

### The Framework Can Become Static

STP is a snapshot. It captures a moment in time. But markets are dynamic. Customer needs change. New competitors emerge. Technology creates new segments and destroys old ones.

The danger is that a company does segmentation once, chooses a target, establishes a position, and then treats these decisions as permanent. Five years later, the world has changed but the marketing strategy has not.

The solution is to treat STP as an ongoing process, not a one-time exercise. Segments should be reviewed annually. The competitive position should be reassessed regularly. The marketing mix should evolve with the customer. The framework is a tool for thinking, not a monument to be preserved.

### The Ethical Dimension of STP

There is a less discussed but critically important limitation of STP: the ethical implications of segmentation and targeting.

Segmentation identifies groups of people who share certain characteristics. Targeting decides which groups to pursue. But what happens when the basis of segmentation is inherently problematic? Targeting children, the elderly, or financially vulnerable people with products they do not need and cannot afford is a well-documented marketing practice that the STP framework alone cannot prevent. Predatory lending, questionable health supplements, and addictive gaming products often reach their customers through sophisticated segmentation and targeting strategies.

The question is not whether STP works — it clearly does. The question is whether marketing to a vulnerable segment, just because they are reachable and profitable, is the right thing to do. STP does not answer that question. It is a tool, not a moral compass. The marketer must supply the ethics.

Another ethical concern: segmentation can reinforce stereotypes. If you target a product at "stay-at-home moms" or "urban youth" based on demographic assumptions, you may be perpetuating narrow and potentially harmful views of those groups. Modern marketing is increasingly aware of this risk, and many companies are moving toward more inclusive segmentation strategies that recognize the diversity within any demographic group.

STP is a powerful framework. Use it wisely.

## Case 1: Nike's "Just Do It" Repositioning

We opened this chapter with the Nike story. Now let us examine it systematically, using the STP framework to understand what Nike actually did and how the repositioning worked.

### The Situation

In 1984, Nike was the dominant brand in running shoes but was losing to Reebok in the fast-growing aerobics category. The conventional response would have been to develop an aerobics shoe, market it to women participating in aerobics, and compete head-to-head with Reebok for the aerobics customer.

Nike tried this. The company launched an aerobics shoe called the Nike Air Conditioner in 1985. It was a competent product — technically superior to the Reebok Freestyle in several ways. But it failed to gain traction because Nike's brand identity worked against it. The Nike brand meant serious athletic performance. Aerobics customers did not see themselves as serious athletes. They saw themselves as fitness participants, and Nike felt too intense, too competitive, too *male* for their identity.

Nike's executives faced a strategic choice: change the product or change the customer.

### The Diagnosis

The Nike management team, led by marketing director Jim Riswold and advertising agency Wieden+Kennedy, reached a diagnosis that was both simpler and more profound than the conventional view.

The problem was not Reebok. The problem was that Nike had defined its customer too narrowly. Nike thought it was targeting "serious runners." But the company's core competence — its real asset — was not running shoes. It was the ability to inspire athletic achievement. The company had a powerful brand that could speak to anyone who cared about pushing their limits, which was a much larger market than the running shoe market.

The insight: Nike was not in the shoe business. It was in the *human potential* business.

This distinction is worth pausing over, because it is the most important strategic insight in marketing. A shoe company competes against other shoe companies on features, price, and distribution. A human potential company competes against anything that helps people achieve — coaching, self-help books, fitness programs, motivation. The frame of competition expands dramatically when you define your market by the customer's need rather than by your product. And when the frame expands, the possibilities for growth expand with it.

### The New Segmentation

Nike's original segmentation was based on sport: runners, basketball players, tennis players, and so on. This was a functional segmentation — useful for product development but limiting for brand building.

The new segmentation was psychographic: people with an athlete's mindset. This segment cut across sports, across genders, across ages, across skill levels. It included the professional athlete (Michael Jordan), the weekend warrior, the first-time marathoner, the elderly jogger, the child playing pickup basketball. What united them was not the sport they played but the attitude they brought.

This was a brilliant segmentation move because it was simultaneously inclusive and exclusive. It was inclusive because anyone could qualify — "if you have a body, you are an athlete." It was exclusive because it required the customer to identify with a specific set of values: determination, effort, self-improvement, grit. People who did not care about pushing their limits were not Nike's customer.

### The New Target

Nike's target became: anyone, of any age or gender or sport, who takes their physical limits seriously and wants to achieve something through effort.

This target was enormous — much larger than the running shoe market — but it was also specific. Not "everyone." Not "people who exercise." People with a specific psychological orientation toward achievement. This target gave Nike permission to expand from running shoes into basketball, tennis, golf, soccer, and eventually into every sport and fitness category imaginable.

### The New Position

Nike's position shifted from "best running shoes" to "brand for athletes." The positioning statement: "For athletes of all levels, Nike is the athletic brand that stands for achievement and self-actualization, because we understand the athlete's mindset and create products and messages that inspire greatness."

The "Just Do It" tagline captured this position perfectly. Three words that said: stop thinking, stop hesitating, stop making excuses. Go do the thing you are capable of doing. It was not about the shoe. It was about the person wearing the shoe.

### The Marketing Mix

**Product.** Nike expanded its product line from running shoes into basketball (Air Jordan), cross-training (the Air Trainer), and eventually into every sport category. The product strategy was to create performance-enhancing footwear and apparel for real athletes. The Air cushioning technology became a product platform that spanned categories, reinforcing the position with visible innovation.

**Price.** Nike maintained premium pricing. This was consistent with the position: serious athletes do not buy cheap equipment. Premium pricing also funded the enormous marketing investments the strategy required, including Michael Jordan's endorsement contract, which cost $500,000 in 1984 (plus a royalty structure that eventually made Jordan hundreds of millions).

**Place.** Nike continued to sell through sporting goods stores and athletic footwear retailers — not department stores, not discount chains. The distribution reinforced the athletic positioning. A Nike shoe could not be bought in a store that also sold cheap sneakers.

**Promotion.** This is where the repositioning came to life. The "Just Do It" campaign, created by Wieden+Kennedy, used the following elements:

- Real athletes (not models) in emotionally powerful situations
- Minimal product focus — the ads were about the athlete, not the shoe
- Black-and-white cinematography that felt serious and timeless
- Slogans that captured the athlete's inner voice ("There is no finish line")
- Celebrity endorsements from athletes who embodied the mindset (Michael Jordan, Bo Jackson, Andre Agassi)

The first "Just Do It" ad, directed by David Fincher, featured 80-year-old Walt Stack running across the Golden Gate Bridge. No product shot. No price. Just an old man running, with the words "Just Do It" on screen. It was pure positioning.

The campaign included dozens of ads, each targeting a different dimension of the athlete mindset. One of the most famous, "Failure," featured Michael Jordan — the greatest basketball player alive — talking about all the shots he had missed in his career. *"I've missed more than 9,000 shots in my career. I've lost almost 300 games. Twenty-six times I've been trusted to take the game-winning shot and missed. I've failed over and over and over again in my life. And that is why I succeed."* The ad contained no product imagery. No price. No call to action. It was a statement about what it means to be an athlete — and by extension, what it means to be a Nike customer.

Another ad featured Bo Jackson, the two-sport professional athlete, in a campaign called "Bo Knows." The spots showed Bo playing baseball, football, basketball, cycling, tennis — even playing guitar. The message: Bo Jackson is an athlete, not just a football player or a baseball player. Nike's shoes were for athletes, not just runners.

A third iconic spot, "There Is No Finish Line," showed a runner on a lonely road. The voiceover: *"There is no finish line. There is no finish line. There is no finish line."* The message: athletic achievement is not a destination. It is a lifelong pursuit. And Nike is the brand that understands that.

### The Result

Between 1988 and 1998, Nike's revenue grew from $877 million to $9.6 billion. The company's U.S. athletic footwear market share recovered from 21 percent to 43 percent. The brand went from being the top running shoe brand to the most valuable sports brand in the world. "Just Do It" became part of the cultural vocabulary.

The repositioning worked because it was rooted in a genuine insight about the customer — not a clever advertising slogan. Nike understood that its real customer was not the person who ran marathons but the person who *wanted* to become the kind of person who runs marathons. The product was merely the tool. The real value was the identity the brand offered.

### Lessons from Nike

**The market is not a product category; it is a customer need.** Nike stopped defining its market by what it made (running shoes) and started defining it by who it served (athletes). This simple shift opened enormous growth possibilities.

**Positioning is not about the product; it is about the customer's identity.** The most powerful positions appeal to who the customer wants to be, not just what they want to buy. Nike sold the identity of an athlete, and the shoes were evidence of that identity.

**A great position survives competitive attacks.** Reebok could not copy Nike's position because Nike's position was not a product feature — it was a relationship with the customer's self-concept. Reebok could make aerobics shoes, but it could not make people feel like Nike made them feel.

## Case 2: Dollar Shave Club

In March 2012, a thirty-one-year-old entrepreneur named Michael Dubin sat in a small production studio in Los Angeles and made a video that would change the razor industry forever. The video was two minutes long. It cost $4,500 to produce. Dubin was the only actor.

The opening line, delivered directly to camera with deadpan confidence: *"Do you like spending $20 on a razor? Because that's f***ing bananas."*

The video went on to explain that Dollar Shave Club offered high-quality razor blades delivered to your door for a few dollars a month. It was funny, irreverent, and brutally honest about the absurdity of paying $30 for a package of four razor cartridges. It ended with Dubin delivering the line that would become the company's tagline: *"Our blades are f***ing great."*

Within 48 hours of the video's release, Dollar Shave Club received 12,000 orders. The website crashed. Dubin and his co-founder Mark Levine scrambled to fulfill orders from a small warehouse. Within three months, they had 200,000 subscribers. By 2015, they had 3 million subscribers and were generating $200 million in annual revenue. In 2016, Unilever acquired Dollar Shave Club for $1 billion in cash.

To understand how a $4,500 YouTube video could disrupt Gillette's 70-year dominance of the razor industry, we must look at the STP strategy behind it.

### The Market Before Dollar Shave Club

In 2010, the U.S. razor blade market was dominated by a single company: Gillette. A Procter & Gamble brand since 2005, Gillette held approximately 70 percent of the market. Its strategy was a model of premium branding: Gillette invested heavily in research and development to produce blades with more and better features (three blades, then five, then a vibrating handle, then a heated razor). It supported these products with massive advertising campaigns featuring the world's most recognizable athletes (Derek Jeter, Tiger Woods, Roger Federer). And it charged premium prices — about $30 for a package of four replacement cartridges.

The system was elegant. Gillette gave away the razor handle at a low price or even for free. The profit came from the replacement cartridges, which had extremely high margins and which customers needed to buy repeatedly. This was the classic razor-and-blades business model (which itself gave the model its name).

But the model had a structural vulnerability: it relied on customers not thinking too hard about what they were paying. Gillette's pricing for replacement blades was disconnected from the cost of manufacturing them. A package of cartridges that cost perhaps $1 to produce was sold for $30. The margin was sustained by brand loyalty, inertia, and the complete absence of meaningful competition at Gillette's level.

### The Segmentation Insight

Dubin and Levine identified a segment that Gillette was actively ignoring: price-sensitive, practical men who resented paying $30 for razor blades but had no good alternative.

This segment was large. Most men in America were buying Gillette or Schick razors, not because they loved them, but because they had no convenient alternative. They were not enthusiasts about shaving. They were people who needed to shave and wanted the cheapest adequate solution.

But this segment was also invisible to Gillette because Gillette's strategy was built on the opposite assumption: that men wanted better shaving experiences and would pay for innovation. Gillette was competing against itself — trying to get customers to upgrade from the Mach3 to the Fusion to the Fusion ProGlide. The budget segment was not just unserved; it was *unseen*. Gillette's entire strategic orientation was toward the premium end of the market.

### The Targeting Strategy

Dollar Shave Club chose concentrated targeting. It went after one segment — practical, price-conscious men — and designed everything around serving them.

This segment had several characteristics that made it attractive:

- **Substantial:** Millions of men bought razors regularly.
- **Underserved:** Gillette and Schick competed on features and price escalation, not on affordability.
- **Reachable:** These men were online, used social media, and were receptive to direct-to-consumer brands.
- **Price-sensitive:** The core insight was that the segment wanted "good enough" quality at a fraction of the premium price.
- **Convenience-seeking:** The subscription model addressed the annoyance of running out of blades and having to buy them at a store.

### The Positioning

Dollar Shave Club's position was: "For practical men who are tired of overpaying for razors, Dollar Shave Club is the razor subscription that delivers great blades for a few dollars a month."

The position was built on three pillars:

**Irreverence.** The tone was anti-establishment, funny, and honest. The positioning weaponized humor against Gillette's seriousness. Gillette was the establishment; Dollar Shave Club was the rebel.

**Honesty.** Dubin's video admitted openly what Gillette's advertising never would: razor blades are absurdly overpriced. The honesty was itself a positioning device. "We tell you the truth. They don't."

**Simplicity.** No vibrating handles, no five-blade systems, no lubricating strips. Just a good blade at a fair price. The positioning implicitly argued that Gillette's features were unnecessary — marketing gimmicks designed to justify premium prices.

### The Marketing Mix

**Product.** The product was a high-quality four-blade cartridge that was functionally equivalent to Gillette's offerings. The key insight: the customer did not need the most advanced blade. They needed a blade that worked well enough. Dollar Shave Club sourced the blades from a Korean manufacturer that had decades of experience making razor cartridges for private-label brands.

**Price.** The pricing was the centerpiece of the strategy. The basic plan was $1 per month (plus shipping and handling). The mid-tier was $3 per month. The top tier was $6 per month. Compare this to the $30 that a package of four Gillette cartridges cost. The price communicated the position more clearly than any marketing copy could.

**Place.** Direct-to-consumer, subscription only. This was critical for several reasons. It eliminated retail markups, allowing the low price. It created recurring revenue, making unit economics predictable. It built a direct relationship with the customer, providing data and enabling personalized communication. And it avoided the shelf-space battle with Gillette, which would have been unwinnable in traditional retail.

**Promotion.** The $4,500 YouTube video was the entire marketing launch. Dubin wrote the script himself. He directed it himself. He acted in it himself. The video was shared millions of times across social media, covered by news outlets, and watched by tens of millions of people. The total marketing spend for the launch was $4,500. Compare that to the hundreds of millions Gillette spent annually on advertising.

The video worked because it was perfectly aligned with the position. It was irreverent, funny, and honest. It did not look like corporate advertising. It looked like a guy telling you the truth about an industry that had been overcharging you for decades.

### The Result

Dollar Shave Club grew to 3 million subscribers and $200 million in annual revenue within four years. It captured about 5 percent of the U.S. razor market — enough to force Gillette to respond.

Gillette's response is worth examining because it reveals how market leaders react to disruptive targeting. In 2017, Gillette launched Gillette on Demand — a subscription service that delivered blades to customers' doors, exactly like Dollar Shave Club. It also cut prices on its premium cartridge refills by up to 20 percent. This was an admission that Dollar Shave Club's segmentation insight had been correct: there was a large segment of price-sensitive men that Gillette had been ignoring, and that segment was now worth fighting for.

But Gillette faced a structural problem that Dollar Shave Club did not. Every Dollar Shave Club subscriber who saved money was a validation of the brand. Every Gillette subscriber who saved money was a contradiction of it — Gillette's entire brand identity was built around premium quality, innovation, and performance. Offering a budget subscription undermined the premium positioning that had sustained Gillette's margins for decades. Gillette could not win on price without losing its identity.

In 2016, Unilever acquired Dollar Shave Club for $1 billion in cash. The founders walked away with life-changing wealth. But the more significant outcome was the demonstration that a $4,500 video and a clear segmentation strategy could disrupt a seventy-year-old monopoly.

### Lessons from Dollar Shave Club

**The market leader's strength is also its weakness.** Gillette's premium strategy was enormously profitable, but it created an opening at the bottom of the market that a disciplined competitor could exploit. The very things that made Gillette successful — high prices, premium features, massive advertising — made it impossible for Gillette to compete on price without destroying its own business model.

**You do not need a better product. You need a different customer.** Dollar Shave Club's product was not better than Gillette's. It was simply good enough — at a radically lower price, delivered in a radically more convenient way. The company won not by beating Gillette at its own game but by finding a segment that Gillette was not serving.

**Distribution innovation can be more powerful than product innovation.** The subscription model was not a new idea, but applying it to razors — a category that was purchased on autopilot in grocery stores — was genuinely novel. Dollar Shave Club did not invent a better blade. It invented a better way to buy blades.

**The best marketing is built into the business model.** The $4,500 video was brilliant, but it worked because the business model made the positioning believable. A cheap-looking product at a high price would have been laughed at. An expensive product at a cheap price would have been unsustainable. The alignment between product, price, distribution, and promotion made the marketing authentic. It was not just a good ad. It was a true statement about the value the company offered.

## Case 3: Airbnb's Market Segmentation

In October 2007, two design school graduates named Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia were struggling to pay rent in their San Francisco apartment. A major industrial design conference was coming to town, and all the hotels were sold out. They saw an opportunity: they inflated three air mattresses, listed them as "air bed and breakfast" on a simple website, and charged $80 per night.

Three guests showed up. They slept on the air mattresses, ate a Pop-Tart breakfast, and left. Chesky and Gebbia made enough money to cover their rent. More importantly, they discovered something that would build a $100 billion company: there were people who wanted to host strangers in their homes, and there were travelers who preferred staying with a local over staying in a hotel.

Airbnb's trajectory from three air mattresses to a global hospitality platform is a case study in how segmentation evolves as a company grows. The company did not have a single segmentation strategy from the beginning. It discovered its segments through iteration, observation, and learning.

### The Early Days: One Segment

In the beginning, Airbnb's customer was simple: budget travelers who could not find or afford a hotel. The value proposition was straightforward: cheaper accommodation than a hotel, in a real neighborhood, with a local host.

The early segmentation was primarily geographic and behavioral. The customers were people attending events where hotel capacity was tight — conferences, festivals, sporting events. The hosts were people who had spare space and wanted extra income. The match was practical, not emotional.

This first segment was small but viable. Airbnb grew slowly, processing a few thousand bookings per month in its first year. The company survived on credit card debt and cereal boxes (they sold Obama O's and Cap'n McCain's during the 2008 election to raise cash).

### The Insight That Changed Everything

In 2009, Chesky and Gebbia took a step that would reveal a much larger market. They flew to New York City, the company's largest market at the time, and personally visited every host they could. They took professional photographs of the listings. They talked to hosts about why they hosted.

What they discovered changed their understanding of their market. The hosts were not just looking for extra money. They were looking for *connection*. They enjoyed meeting people from around the world. They liked showing off their city. They took pride in being hosts.

And the guests were not just looking for cheap accommodation. They wanted to *live like a local*. They wanted to stay in a real neighborhood, not a tourist district. They wanted to interact with a real person who could tell them where to eat, what to see, how to experience the city authentically.

This insight led to a psychographic redefinition of Airbnb's customer. The target was no longer "budget traveler." It was "traveler who values authentic local experiences."

This was a profound segmentation insight because it changed the nature of competition. If Airbnb's competitor was "cheap hotels," the company was competing on price — a difficult game. But if Airbnb's competitor was "cookie-cutter tourist experiences," the company was offering something genuinely different. The cheap price was a feature, but the authentic experience was the product.

### The Segmentation Evolution

As Airbnb grew, its segmentation strategy evolved through several phases:

**Phase 1: Budget travelers (2007-2009).** The original segment. People who could not afford or find hotels. Functional need: cheap accommodation.

**Phase 2: Authentic experience seekers (2009-2012).** People who wanted to travel differently. Psychographic need: local, authentic, personal experiences.

**Phase 3: Mainstream travelers (2012-2016).** As trust in the platform grew, Airbnb began attracting travelers who were not just looking for something different but were looking for something that was *as good as* a hotel. This required the company to professionalize the host experience — implementing standards, insurance, and customer support.

**Phase 4: All travelers (2016-present).** Airbnb expanded into business travel (Airbnb for Work), luxury (Airbnb Luxe), and experiences (Airbnb Experiences). The segmentation broadened to include segments that would have seemed impossible in the early days: business travelers who needed reliable Wi-Fi and workspaces, families who needed cribs and child-friendly homes, luxury travelers who wanted villas with private pools and concierge services.

### The Trust and Quality Challenge

One of the most difficult obstacles Airbnb faced in expanding beyond its original segment was the trust problem. The early adopters — budget travelers seeking authentic experiences — were willing to accept a certain amount of uncertainty. They understood that staying in a stranger's home was not the same as staying in a hotel, and they accepted the trade-offs.

But mainstream travelers and business travelers had different expectations. They wanted reliability: guaranteed clean sheets, working Wi-Fi, a lock on the bedroom door, a host who would not cancel at the last minute. They wanted consistency: knowing that an Airbnb "4.5-star rating" meant roughly the same thing in Paris, Tokyo, and Chicago.

Airbnb addressed this through a series of investments that were, in effect, moves toward product standardization without losing the brand's authentic identity:

- **Professional photography.** In 2010, Airbnb launched a program to send professional photographers to capture listings. This improved the quality of listings across the platform and reduced the gap between expectation and reality — one of the most common sources of customer dissatisfaction.
- **Host guarantees.** Airbnb introduced a $1 million host guarantee to protect hosts against property damage, addressing the fear that kept many potential hosts from listing their homes.
- **Verified ID.** Airbnb required users to verify their identities, building trust between strangers who were about to sleep in each other's homes.
- **Reviews and ratings.** The two-way review system — both guests and hosts review each other — created a self-policing mechanism that maintained quality without requiring Airbnb to centrally inspect every property.

These investments did not make Airbnb like a hotel. They made Airbnb *trustworthy enough* for mainstream travelers while maintaining the distinctive value of local, authentic experiences. The company had found a way to safely serve multiple segments without homogenizing its product.

### The Positioning Challenge

Airbnb's greatest positioning challenge has been reconciling its growing number of segments with its core identity.

The original position was: "For travelers who want to live like a local, Airbnb is the accommodation platform that connects you with unique homes and hospitable hosts."

This position was powerful because it was distinctive. No hotel chain could offer what Airbnb offered — staying in someone's actual home, getting personal recommendations from a local, experiencing a city from the inside.

But as Airbnb expanded into business travel and luxury, it needed to add new promises: safety, reliability, consistency. These promises were in tension with the original position. A standardized luxury experience is, by definition, not "unique." A business traveler who needs reliable Wi-Fi and a desk is not looking to "live like a local."

Airbnb's response has been to layer segments rather than replace them. The core position — belonging anywhere — remains the umbrella. Under that umbrella, different segments get different marketing messages and product features:

- **Airbnb for Work:** "Business travel that feels like travel, not a trip."
- **Airbnb Luxe:** "The world's most beautiful homes, with white-glove service."
- **Airbnb Experiences:** "Not just where you go, but what you do there."

Each sub-position is consistent with the overarching brand, but each speaks to a different segment with different priorities.

### The Lessons

Airbnb's segmentation journey teaches several important lessons about market strategy:

**Segmentation is not a one-time exercise.** Airbnb's target customer evolved dramatically over its first decade. The company did not abandon its original customer — it added new segments as it learned more about who its product could serve and as it built the capabilities to serve them.

**The best segmentation insights come from customer interaction.** Chesky and Gebbia did not discover the "live like a local" insight through market research reports. They discovered it by talking to hosts and guests in person. The richest segmentation insights are ethnographic — they come from deep observation, not from demographic data.

**A growing company can serve multiple segments if it maintains a consistent brand identity.** Airbnb's segments are diverse, but they are united by an overarching position: "belong anywhere." This umbrella positioning allows the company to serve budget travelers, luxury travelers, business travelers, and experience seekers without confusing its brand.

**Segmentation can create new markets.** Airbnb did not enter an existing market and take share from incumbents. It created a new category — peer-to-peer home rental — that had not existed before. The segmentation insight was not about dividing an existing market but about discovering that a market could exist where none was recognized.

## The One Thing to Remember

> Marketing is not about selling what you make. It is about making what will sell — and to know what will sell, you must know who you are selling to. Segmentation, targeting, and positioning are the tools that force you to answer that question with precision and honesty. Without them, you are not doing marketing. You are guessing.

## How to Use This Tomorrow

1. **Write your positioning statement.** Use the formula: "For [target customer], [brand] is the [category] that [point of difference], because [reason to believe]." If you cannot complete this sentence in a way that feels specific and honest, you do not have a clear position. Keep working until the sentence is tight enough to fit on a business card.

2. **Map your current segments.** List the different groups of customers you currently serve (or plan to serve). For each group, write down: demographic profile, psychographic profile, behavioral pattern, and the specific need they are hiring your product to fill. If two segments look identical on this analysis, they are not separate segments.

3. **Evaluate your targeting strategy.** Are you trying to serve everyone? If so, you are likely serving no one well. Which segment would you serve if you had to pick just one? What would happen if you focused all your marketing resources on that segment for the next six months?

4. **Check your marketing mix for consistency.** List your current Product, Price, Place, and Promotion. Ask: do all four elements reinforce the same position? Or do they send mixed signals? Find the weakest link — the element that is most inconsistent with your position — and fix it first.

5. **Do the "segment you are ignoring" exercise.** Think about your market leader or biggest competitor. What segment are they not serving well? Is there a group of customers who are underserved, overcharged, or ignored? That is your opportunity. Dollar Shave Club found it in razors. Airbnb found it in hotels. Find it in your industry.

6. **Run the "if we disappeared" test.** Imagine your company disappeared tomorrow. Which customers would miss you most? Which would barely notice? The customers who would miss you most are your true segment — the ones for whom your positioning is most valuable. The ones who would not notice are either not your customers or evidence that your positioning is not distinctive enough.

7. **Build a competitor positioning grid.** Draw a simple grid with your brand and your top three competitors along the top. Down the side, list the five most important dimensions of your market (price, quality, convenience, service, selection). For each brand, rate them high/medium/low on each dimension. The pattern reveals where the market is crowded, where it is empty, and where your brand sits relative to competitors. This exercise takes thirty minutes and produces insights that most companies never bother to generate.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1: Positioning Statement Workshop.**

Choose a product or brand you know well — it could be your own company's product, a competitor's, or a famous brand. Write a positioning statement for it using the formula. Then write a positioning statement for a direct competitor. Compare them. Are they genuinely different, or do they say the same thing in different words? If they are not different, what does that tell you about the competitive landscape?

**Exercise 2: Perceptual Map.**

Pick an industry you are interested in (automotive, smartphones, coffee, airlines, hotels, or any other). Identify the two most important dimensions customers use to evaluate brands in that industry. Draw a 2x2 matrix and plot at least six brands on it. Where are the clusters? Where are the gaps? Is there a gap that represents a genuine opportunity, or is it a gap because nobody wants that position? Write a paragraph explaining the most interesting finding from your map.

**Exercise 3: The Segment That Should Not Exist.**

Identify a product or service that should not work according to conventional market logic — a niche product that serves a very small segment in a world of mass-market alternatives. (Examples: mechanical watches in the age of smartwatches, vinyl records in the age of streaming, premium fountain pens.) Analyze why this segment exists. What need does it serve that the mass-market alternative does not? What would happen if the segment disappeared?

**Exercise 4: The Four Ps Audit.**

Take a product you use regularly. For each of the 4Ps (Product, Price, Place, Promotion), describe the current execution. Then ask: are all four consistent with the same position? If you were to change one P — say, dramatically increase the price or switch to direct-to-consumer distribution — what would have to change about the other three Ps to maintain consistency? This exercise reveals how deeply the 4Ps are connected.

**Exercise 5: The STP Audit of a Failed Product.**

Find a product or company that failed in the market — not because the product was bad, but because nobody bought it. (Segway, Google Glass, New Coke, or any other well-known flop.) Analyze the failure through the STP lens: Who did they think their customer was? Did they segment correctly, or did they assume "everyone" would want it? Did they target a segment that was too small, too hard to reach, or unwilling to pay? Was their positioning clear and distinctive, or was it confused? Write a one-page analysis identifying the STP mistake that doomed the product.

**Exercise 6: Segment a Market from Scratch.**

Pick a broad market (for example: "people who need to eat lunch in a city"). Segment it using at least two different bases (demographic, psychographic, behavioral, or geographic). Identify at least five distinct segments. For each segment, estimate: size, growth trajectory, and the key need that a lunch product would need to serve. Then pick one segment and design a minimal viable product, price, place, and promotion for it. What makes this segment worth serving?

## Further Reading

- **Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind** by Al Ries and Jack Trout — The original and still the best book on positioning. Published in 1981, its insights about how the human mind processes marketing messages are as relevant today as they were forty years ago. The writing is sharp, the examples are vivid, and the core concept — that positioning is about occupying a mental slot, not about product features — is one of the most important ideas in marketing.

- **Marketing Management** by Philip Kotler — The definitive textbook on marketing, now in its sixteenth edition. Kotler has been the preeminent authority on marketing for half a century, and this book is the most comprehensive treatment of the subject available. It is dense, academic, and thorough — the opposite of a light read, but the book you turn to when you need depth on any marketing topic.

- **The 22 Immutable Laws of Marketing** by Al Ries and Jack Trout — A concise, provocative book that distills marketing strategy into twenty-two rules. Some of the rules are debatable, but all of them are thought-provoking. The core message: marketing is a battle of perceptions, not products, and the laws of marketing are more like the laws of gravity than the laws of physics — they describe how the world works, whether you like it or not.

- **Zero to One** by Peter Thiel — Not strictly a marketing book, but Thiel's chapter on "Monopoly" contains one of the best discussions of the relationship between market definition and business strategy. Thiel argues that every successful company starts by dominating a small market and expands from there — which is essentially a concentrated targeting strategy. His question "what important truth do very few people agree with you on?" is a segmentation question in disguise.

- **Obviously Awesome** by April Dunford — A modern, practical guide to positioning specifically for technology companies and startups. Dunford's framework for "positioning in ten steps" is the most actionable positioning methodology I have seen in recent years. If you work in a tech company and need to position a product that does not fit neatly into existing categories, start here.
