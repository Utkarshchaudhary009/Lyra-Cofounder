# Chapter 25: The Invisible Force — Organizational Culture

*"Culture is not a lever you pull. It's a garden you tend. And most leaders are terrible gardeners."*

---

In 2009, a streaming company that had just survived a near-death experience posted a 127-slide PowerPoint presentation to SlideShare. It was titled "Netflix Culture: Freedom & Responsibility." The company had nearly collapsed in 2008 — a poorly timed price increase had sent subscribers fleeing, and the financial crisis was making matters worse. The CEO, Reed Hastings, later admitted that he had spent months lying awake at night thinking about what kind of company Netflix needed to become. The old culture — built for a DVD-by-mail startup — was not going to carry them through the streaming transition. Something fundamental needed to shift. The slide deck was his answer.

It went viral. Five million views. Sheryl Sandberg called it "the most important document ever to come out of Silicon Valley." Companies around the world tried to copy it. Consultants built entire practices around it. And yet — most companies that tried to adopt the Netflix culture failed. They adopted the artifacts (no vacation policy, no expense policy) without understanding the basic assumptions that made those policies work. They wanted the freedom without the responsibility. They wanted the trust without the ruthless performance standards. They wanted the surface without the depth. And they got exactly what they asked for — nothing.

This is the fundamental problem with how most leaders approach culture. They see the visible surface — the ping pong tables, the open floor plans, the mission statements on the wall — and they think that's culture. It's not. Culture is the invisible, unconscious, taken-for-granted set of beliefs that determine how people actually behave when no one is watching. It's the shared learning of a group. And it is the most powerful — and most misunderstood — force in organizational life.

I have spent over sixty years studying organizational culture. I have consulted with companies from Digital Equipment Corporation to General Motors, from startups to governments. And I have watched generation after generation of leaders make the same mistake: they try to change culture by changing the visible artifacts, without ever touching the invisible assumptions that drive behavior. They print new values on posters. They redesign the office. They launch a "culture initiative." And nothing changes. The posters go up, the assumptions stay the same.

This chapter is about understanding what culture actually is — not what the marketing department says it is, but what it really is: the pattern of shared basic assumptions that a group has learned as it solved its problems of external adaptation and internal integration. It is about why culture matters more than strategy. And it is about why most efforts to change culture fail — and what it actually takes to succeed.

I want to be clear from the start: culture is not a management lever. You cannot pull it and expect something to happen. Culture is the outcome of a thousand small decisions, reinforced over years. It is not something you *have*. It is something you *are*. And the first step to understanding it is to stop looking at the visible surface and start digging into the invisible depths.

## Core Concepts

### 1. Schein's Three Levels of Culture

When I walk into an organization to study its culture, I do not start by reading the mission statement. I do not look at the website. I do not count the ping pong tables. I start by observing what happens in the hallway conversations, in the way people react when a mistake is made, in the silence that falls when a particular topic is raised. Because culture exists at three levels — and only the first level is visible.

**Level 1: Artifacts.** These are the visible, tangible, audible manifestations of culture. The office layout. The dress code. The rituals and ceremonies. The language people use. The stories they tell. The published values on the wall. The way people address each other (first names? titles?). The parking spots (who gets the best one?). The architecture.

Artifacts are easy to observe but extraordinarily hard to interpret. I can walk into an office and see that everyone is wearing jeans and T-shirts. I can see the open floor plan, the beanbag chairs, the game room. But what does that mean? Does it mean the company values creativity and informality? Does it mean they want to attract young talent? Does it mean they're trying to signal "we're not like those stuffy corporations"? Or does it mean they simply never thought about it? Artifacts without context are meaningless. The same open floor plan at a tech startup and a newspaper newsroom mean completely different things.

This is the trap most leaders fall into. They see the artifacts of a successful company — Google's free food, Netflix's no vacation policy, Zappos' culture book — and they try to copy the artifacts. But artifacts are just the visible tip of an invisible iceberg. Copy the artifacts without understanding what lies beneath, and you get the surface without the substance.

**Level 2: Espoused Values.** These are the stated beliefs, norms, and rules of behavior. The mission statement. The "core values" poster in the lobby. The code of conduct. What the organization *says* it believes.

Every company has espoused values. Most of them sound the same: integrity, innovation, customer focus, teamwork, respect. But there is a crucial distinction that most people miss: there is a difference between *espoused* values and *enacted* values. Espoused values are what you say you believe. Enacted values are what you actually reward and punish. And the gap between them is where culture reveals itself.

I worked with a company that had "innovation" as one of its core values. It was on the wall, in the annual report, in every CEO speech. But when I watched how the company actually operated, I saw something different. People who proposed new ideas were criticized. Risks were punished. Failure was a career-ender. The company rewarded predictability, not innovation. The espoused value was "innovation." The enacted value was "don't make mistakes."

The gap between espoused and enacted values is not necessarily hypocrisy. Often, it's simply that the company has never been forced to confront the contradiction. The espoused values were written in a strategic planning offsite by a group of executives who genuinely believed in them. But those values never got integrated into the actual operating principles of the organization — the hiring criteria, the promotion decisions, the budget allocations, the performance reviews. They remained aspirational, not operational.

**Level 3: Basic Underlying Assumptions.** This is the deepest level of culture — the unconscious, taken-for-granted beliefs that actually drive behavior. These are not written down. They are not discussed. They are so deeply embedded that people don't even realize they hold them. They are the "way we do things around here" that everyone knows but no one articulates.

Basic assumptions answer fundamental questions: What is the nature of reality? Is the world predictable or chaotic? What is the nature of human nature? Are people basically good, basically lazy, or something else? What is the nature of human relationships? Is competition natural? Is collaboration?

At a practical level, basic assumptions show up in how organizations answer questions like: What happens when someone makes a mistake? Is it a learning opportunity or a black mark? What happens when someone disagrees with the boss? Is that healthy debate or insubordination? What determines who gets promoted? Merit? Politics? Tenure?

I once worked with a manufacturing company that had a deep, unspoken basic assumption: "The people on the factory floor cannot be trusted to think." This assumption was never stated. It wasn't written anywhere. But it showed up in every policy, every procedure, every management decision. There were detailed manuals for every task. Supervisors watched workers constantly. Any deviation from procedure was punished. When I asked why, managers said things like "that's how we maintain quality" or "you can't let them make their own decisions." But the underlying assumption was clear: workers are not capable of judgment.

The problem with basic assumptions is that they are extraordinarily difficult to surface and even harder to change. You cannot simply announce a new assumption. You cannot put "trust your employees" on a poster. The old assumption is embedded in every system, every process, every managerial habit. Changing it requires years of consistent, reinforced behavior — and even then, you may only scratch the surface.

The three levels of culture explain why most culture change efforts fail. Leaders try to change level 1 (artifacts) or level 2 (espoused values) without ever addressing level 3 (basic assumptions). They put new values on the wall. They redesign the office. They launch a new mission statement. But the basic assumptions remain unchanged. So nothing really changes.

**How to Assess Your Culture at Each Level.** If you want to understand your own organization's culture, you need a systematic approach. Do not trust what people tell you in formal settings. Watch what they do.

At the artifact level, ask: What does the physical space say about who matters? Whose photos are on the wall? Who gets the corner office — or is there no corner office at all? How do people dress? Is there a uniform or a range? What rituals do you observe — the morning standup, the Friday beer tap, the annual retreat? What stories do people tell about the company's history? The stories people choose to retell — about the founder, about a heroic customer save, about a disastrous failure — reveal what the culture values.

At the espoused values level, ask: What does the company say it believes? Read the mission statement critically. Read the annual report. Listen to the CEO's speeches. But then ask: are these values actually lived? Find the gap between what is said and what is done. That gap is the most important diagnostic tool you have.

At the basic assumptions level, ask: What do people really believe about why this organization succeeds? Ask people at every level: "What do you need to believe to be successful here?" The answers will surprise you. They will be honest in private in a way they are not in public. They will tell you things like: "You need to be visible to the boss." "You need to never make the same mistake twice." "You need to be willing to stay late." "You need to protect your turf." "You need to never disagree with a senior person in a meeting." These unspoken rules are the real culture.

The gap between what people say is the culture and what people actually experience is the most important measure of organizational health. A small gap means the culture is coherent — the espoused values match the enacted behavior. A large gap means the culture is confused or hypocritical — and that confusion is itself a cultural feature. Organizations with large gaps between espoused and enacted values tend to have low trust, high cynicism, and a "say one thing, do another" atmosphere that erodes everything.

### 2. Where Culture Comes From

Culture does not appear by accident. It emerges from three primary sources: the founder's imprint, the organization's response to critical incidents, and the accumulated learning from successes and failures.

**The Founder's Imprint.** Every organization's culture starts with its founder. The founder's beliefs, values, assumptions, and personality become the seed from which the culture grows. This is because founders have a unique power: they get to choose the initial team, define the mission, set the rules, and establish the norms. And people who join a startup are self-selecting — they are attracted to the founder's vision and values. Over time, the founder's way of seeing the world becomes the organization's way of seeing the world.

I have studied this process for decades, and I am continually struck by how directly the founder's personality shapes the organization's culture — and how long that imprint lasts. I worked with a company founded by an engineer who believed that everything could be optimized, that every problem had a rational solution, and that emotions were irrelevant to business decisions. Twenty years after he left, the company still operated as if feelings did not matter. Decisions were made by spreadsheets. Human concerns were secondary. The founder had been gone for two decades, but his assumptions were still running the company.

This imprinting process happens through several mechanisms. First, founders hire people like themselves — not necessarily in background, but in values and assumptions. They select for fit. Second, founders model behavior. The first employees watch the founder and learn what matters. Third, founders set the reward systems. They decide who gets promoted and why. Fourth, founders make the early critical decisions, and those decisions set precedents that become embedded in the organization's memory.

But here is the danger: the founder's assumptions are shaped by the context in which the company was founded. A founder who started a company during a recession will have different assumptions about risk and growth than a founder who started during a boom. A founder who came from a dysfunctional family will have different assumptions about trust and control than a founder who came from a supportive one. The founder's personal history — their experiences, traumas, triumphs, and failures — becomes embedded in the organizational DNA.

Thomas Watson Sr. built IBM around his personal beliefs about professionalism, service, and respect. His son Thomas Watson Jr. later codified those beliefs into IBM's "Basic Beliefs" — the first corporate values statement of its kind. Those beliefs shaped IBM for generations. Walt Disney's perfectionism and belief in "the Disney magic" became embedded in every aspect of the company. Even today, decades after his death, the Disney culture reflects his assumptions about quality, creativity, and customer experience.

Jeff Bezos's "Day 1" philosophy — the determination to maintain the urgency and hunger of a startup, even as Amazon grew into a trillion-dollar corporation — is a direct expression of his basic assumptions about the world: that complacency is the greatest threat, that customer focus is the only durable advantage, that the internet changes everything. Those assumptions became Amazon's culture.

Mark Zuckerberg's "Move Fast and Break Things" was not a slogan. It was a fundamental assumption about the nature of software and the world: that speed matters more than perfection, that it is better to launch imperfectly than to delay, that the market rewards action. That assumption shaped Facebook's culture for its first decade — until it ran into problems that required a different approach.

The founder's imprint is powerful, but it is also a source of fragility. What works in the startup phase — the founder's intense personal involvement, the lack of formal processes, the assumption that everyone is aligned with the founder's vision — can become dysfunctional as the organization scales. The founder's strengths become the organization's limitations. This is why so many founders fail as CEOs: the culture they created for a 50-person company breaks down at 5,000 people. The very intensity that made the early culture so powerful becomes a bottleneck as the organization grows. The founder's assumption that "I need to be involved in every decision" that worked for a startup becomes paralyzing for a corporation. The founder's "I trust my gut" that worked in the early days becomes dangerous when decisions involve billions of dollars.

This is why professional CEOs are often brought in to replace founders — not because the founder is not brilliant, but because the culture needs to evolve beyond the founder's assumptions. The best founders understand this. They recognize that their imprint, while valuable, is not eternal. They hire professional managers who can evolve the culture beyond the founder's original assumptions while preserving the core values that made the company great.

**Critical Incidents.** The second source of culture is the way the organization handles its defining moments. Every organization faces crises — a product failure, a lawsuit, a competitor's attack, a market crash. How the organization responds to these crises teaches everyone what the organization truly values. Not what it says it values. Not what it aspires to value. What it actually values, revealed through action under pressure.

Think of critical incidents as the moments when culture is forged in fire. Before the crisis, the culture is a collection of untested assumptions. After the crisis, those assumptions are either validated and deepened or challenged and revised. A crisis is an opportunity for cultural learning, whether the organization realizes it or not.

When Johnson & Johnson faced the Tylenol poisonings in 1982, they recalled all Tylenol capsules from the market — 31 million bottles, at a cost of $100 million. The decision was made without hesitation, based on the company's credo, which stated that the company's first responsibility is to the customers who use its products. That response taught generations of J&J employees that the credo was real, that the company would put safety above profit. It became a defining cultural moment.

When BP faced the Deepwater Horizon oil spill in 2010, their initial response was to downplay the severity, blame contractors, and resist paying claims. The CEO famously said "I'd like my life back." That response taught BP employees volumes about what the company actually valued — and not in a good way. The culture that emerged from that crisis was one of defensiveness and blame-shifting.

Critical incidents reveal culture because they force organizations to make choices under pressure. In normal times, anyone can claim to value safety or integrity or customer focus. It's when those values conflict with something else — profit, convenience, reputation — that the real culture shows itself. A crisis does not create a culture. It exposes it.

**Learning from Success and Failure.** Culture also emerges from what the organization learns works and doesn't work. When a company tries something and succeeds, that behavior gets reinforced. When it tries something and fails, that behavior gets discouraged. Over time, these accumulated lessons become embedded in the organization's assumptions about what leads to success.

Consider a simple example: a startup launches a product quickly with minimal testing. It's buggy, but the speed allows them to beat a competitor to market. The product succeeds. What does the organization learn? It learns that speed matters more than perfection. This becomes an assumption. Years later, when the company is serving millions of customers and a bug could cause real damage, that assumption persists — even though the context has completely changed.

This is why successful organizations often struggle to change their culture. The very assumptions that made them successful in one environment can become traps in a new environment. The assumption that "we know our customers" works until the customers change. The assumption that "our products are the best" works until a competitor innovates. The assumption that "our way is the right way" works until the world shifts.

The learning from success is particularly dangerous because it feels so right. When you succeed, you naturally attribute that success to what you did. You don't question your assumptions because your assumptions just led to success. This is why culture is hardest to change when the organization is most successful — and why the most dangerous time for any organization is when everything is going well.

I have watched this pattern destroy company after company. They achieve great success. They develop a strong belief in "our way of doing things." They stop listening to outside voices because they believe they have the answers. And then the world changes, and they cannot change with it because their success has made them arrogant and blind.

The pattern is so consistent that I have developed a diagnostic question for every organization I work with: "When was the last time your organization learned something that fundamentally changed how you operate?" If the answer is "I can't remember," the culture is likely stuck. Learning must be continuous — not just incremental improvement, but periodic, fundamental reassessment of assumptions. The organizations that survive over the long term are the ones that can question their own success.

### 3. The Culture-Strategy Connection

Peter Drucker is often quoted as saying, "Culture eats strategy for breakfast." It is one of the most repeated phrases in business, and like many frequently quoted phrases, it is usually misunderstood.

What Drucker meant is not that strategy doesn't matter. Strategy matters enormously. What he meant is that a brilliant strategy executed poorly — because the culture won't support it — is worthless. And a decent strategy executed brilliantly — because the culture enables it — can be transformational.

Consider the case of IBM in the early 1990s. The company had a clear strategy: pivot from mainframes to the emerging world of distributed computing and services. The strategy was sound. The market demanded it. The financial resources were there. But the culture was still the mainframe culture — hierarchical, risk-averse, internally focused. The strategy failed until Lou Gerstner changed the culture.

Or consider Nokia. By 2007, Nokia's strategy was actually fairly clear. They knew smartphones were coming. They had the technology. They had the resources. They even had a working touchscreen prototype before the iPhone launched. But the culture — an engineering culture that prioritized hardware over software, a Finnish consensus culture that moved slowly, a success culture that couldn't believe anyone could beat them — made execution impossible.

The relationship between culture and strategy works in both directions. A strong culture can enable strategic execution by providing alignment, speed, and shared purpose. But a strong culture can also become a barrier to strategic change, because the assumptions that made the old strategy work are embedded in every system and every person's mind.

I call this the "cultural DNA" problem. Once an organization has learned a particular way of solving problems, that learning becomes encoded in the culture — in the structure, the processes, the assumptions, the reward systems. And when the environment changes and a new strategy is required, the cultural DNA resists. It has been optimized for a different environment. It doesn't recognize the new threats.

This is why so many successful companies fail when disruption hits. It's not that they're stupid. It's not that they didn't see it coming. It's that their culture — which made them successful — makes it nearly impossible for them to respond. The culture that created their success is the same culture that prevents their transformation.

Consider the newspaper industry. The leaders of major newspapers knew digital was coming. They saw the internet's potential years before it destroyed their business. But their culture — built around print deadlines, physical distribution, the authority of the printed page, the assumption that "we are in the newspaper business" rather than "we are in the information business" — prevented them from responding effectively. They couldn't change because their identity was tied to the old model.

The New York Times is a fascinating exception. The Times realized that its culture needed to change from a print-first to a digital-first organization. This required changing deeply held assumptions about what "journalism" meant — from a daily product delivered on paper to a continuous product delivered everywhere. The shift required the newspaper to question its most basic assumptions about its identity. It took years. It was painful. But the Times succeeded where most of its peers failed because the leadership understood that culture change had to come before strategy change. They had to unlearn the old culture before they could learn the new one.

So when leaders ask me, "How do we change our culture to execute our strategy?" I tell them: you cannot change culture directly. You change culture by changing what you do. You change the systems, the processes, the reward structures, the decision rights. You change who you hire and who you promote. And if you do those things consistently over a long enough period, the culture will eventually shift to support the new strategy.

But you must be honest about what this requires. It requires sustained attention over years. It requires making uncomfortable decisions. It requires letting go of people who embody the old culture, even if they are high performers. And it requires the leader to model the new culture every single day, because everyone is watching.

### 4. Strong vs. Weak Cultures

Not all cultures are equally powerful. Some organizations have cultures that are clear, widely shared, and deeply held. These are strong cultures. Others have cultures that are fragmented, unclear, and shallow. These are weak cultures.

**Strong Cultures.** A strong culture means that there is high agreement among members about what the culture is, and the values are held with intensity. Everyone knows "how we do things around here," and they believe in it.

The benefits of a strong culture are significant. Alignment is higher — people don't need to be told what to do in most situations because they share the same assumptions. Efficiency is higher — decisions are faster because there is less conflict about what matters. Identity is stronger — people feel part of something and are motivated to contribute.

Strong cultures also reduce the need for formal controls. When everyone shares the same values and assumptions, you don't need extensive policies, procedures, and supervision. The culture does the controlling. This is the "freedom and responsibility" model that Netflix talks about — the culture is so clear and strong that you can give people enormous freedom because they will use it in alignment with the organization's goals.

But strong cultures have significant risks as well. The same alignment that makes decisions faster also makes them more prone to groupthink. The same intensity that creates motivation also creates resistance to dissenting views. The same identity that binds people together also blinds them to threats from outside.

Strong cultures can become insular. They can develop an assumption that "our way is the best way" and become hostile to outside ideas. They can become arrogant. And when the environment changes, a strong culture is very hard to change because the assumptions are so deeply held.

I have seen strong cultures destroy companies. The culture that made Digital Equipment Corporation a dominant force in minicomputers made it impossible for them to see the PC revolution coming. The culture that made Kodak the leader in film made them incapable of embracing digital photography — even though they invented it. The same assumptions that created their success created their blindness.

**Weak Cultures.** A weak culture is one where there is low agreement about values and assumptions, or the values are held with low intensity. People don't share a clear understanding of "how we do things around here." Different parts of the organization operate with different assumptions.

The benefits of a weak culture are often overlooked. Weak cultures can be more flexible because there is no dominant set of assumptions enforcing conformity. They can be more open to outside ideas because there is no strong identity to protect. They can be more adaptable because there is less cultural resistance to change.

But the costs are also significant. Decision-making is slow because there is no shared framework for prioritizing. Coordination is difficult because different units operate with different assumptions. There is confusion about what matters, and people waste energy navigating competing expectations.

Weak cultures often lead to excessive formal controls because the informal control of culture is absent. When you can't trust that people share your assumptions, you need more rules, more processes, more supervision. And that creates its own costs — slower decisions, less innovation, lower morale.

**The Balance.** The healthiest organizations, in my observation, have cultures that are strong in their core values but leave room for diversity in how those values are expressed. They have a clear identity and shared assumptions about what matters most, but they are also open to challenge and adaptation. They are strong enough to provide alignment but flexible enough to allow evolution.

This is harder than it sounds. Strong cultures naturally resist challenge because they believe in themselves. Flexible cultures naturally resist clear identity because they value diversity. The organization that can hold both — that can be clear about its core while adaptive in its expression — has achieved something genuinely difficult.

I have seen this balance in a few exceptional organizations. They have what I call "strong core, open edges" — a clear, non-negotiable set of values and assumptions at the center, combined with a genuine openness to new ideas and diverse approaches at the periphery. The core provides identity and alignment. The edges provide learning and adaptation. The tension between them is not a problem to be solved — it is a dynamic to be managed. Organizations that manage this tension well survive. Organizations that resolve it in favor of either pure strength (rigidity) or pure openness (chaos) do not.

**When Strong Cultures Become Dysfunctional.** It is important to recognize the warning signs of a strong culture becoming dysfunctional. The first sign is when cultural values are used to dismiss outside perspectives. If you hear "that's not how we do things here" more than you hear "that's interesting, tell me more," the culture is becoming insular.

The second sign is when cultural values are used to avoid hard conversations. If the culture values "teamwork" and that means no one ever gives critical feedback, the culture has become a trap. Healthy cultures use their values to enable hard conversations, not avoid them.

The third sign is when cultural values become a substitute for thinking. If people stop asking "what is the right decision?" and instead ask "what does our culture say?" the culture has become a cage. Values should guide thinking, not replace it.

The fourth sign is when deviation from cultural norms is punished more severely than failure to achieve results. If being "not a culture fit" is a worse offense than missing targets, the culture has become a cult. Healthy cultures hold people accountable for both results and values. Unhealthy cultures prioritize conformity over performance.

Every leader should periodically ask: is our culture serving us, or are we serving our culture? Is it enabling performance, or is it constraining it? Is it open to learning, or is it closed to challenge? The answers to these questions determine whether the culture is an asset or a liability.

### 5. Subcultures

One of the most common mistakes leaders make is to talk about "the culture" as if every organization has just one. No organization has a single, monolithic culture. Every organization is a collection of subcultures — different groups with different assumptions, values, and ways of working. The leader who says "we need to create one culture" is not aiming for cultural strength. They are aiming for cultural tyranny.

The presence of multiple subcultures is not a sign of weakness. It is a sign of complexity. Different functions require different cultures. An R&D lab that values creativity and exploration will look different from a manufacturing plant that values consistency and precision. A sales team that values persistence and relationship-building will look different from a legal department that values accuracy and caution. The health of an organization is not determined by the uniformity of its culture — it is determined by the coherence of its subcultures.

**The Major Fault Lines.** Subcultures form along predictable lines. The most common is functional: engineering has a different culture than sales, which has a different culture than finance, which has a different culture than marketing. Engineers value precision, elegance, and truth. Salespeople value relationships, persistence, and closing. Finance values accuracy, control, and efficiency. Each group develops its own language, its own assumptions, its own norms. And when these subcultures collide — which they do, constantly — the result is misunderstanding, conflict, and sometimes paralysis.

I have sat in countless meetings where engineering and sales could not agree on a product roadmap. Engineering wanted to build the perfect product. Sales wanted to sell what customers were asking for now. Both groups were acting in perfect alignment with their subcultural values. But without a shared overarching culture that could mediate between them, the conflict was unproductive. The organization needed a higher-level shared assumption — something like "we ship imperfect products quickly and improve them based on customer feedback" — that could bridge the gap between engineering's perfectionism and sales's urgency.

Another fault line is hierarchical: executives see the world differently than middle managers, who see it differently than frontline workers. Each level has different information, different incentives, and different assumptions about what matters. The C-suite talks about strategy and shareholder value; middle managers talk about resources and deadlines; frontline workers talk about customers and daily hassles.

Geography creates subcultures too: the Tokyo office is different from the New York office, which is different from the Bangalore office. National culture interacts with organizational culture.

And time creates subcultures: the "old guard" who grew up with the company has different assumptions than the new hires. There is often tension between the people who built the organization and the people who are trying to transform it.

**The Challenge of Subcultures.** Subcultures can work together or against each other. When they work together, they create a healthy tension that improves decision-making. Engineering challenges sales on unrealistic promises. Finance challenges marketing on wasteful spending. That friction, when managed well, produces better outcomes.

When subcultures work against each other, they create silos, conflict, and paralysis. Engineering won't talk to sales. Marketing blames finance for not understanding. The old guard resists every change the new hires propose. The organization becomes a collection of warring tribes, each protecting its own territory.

The art of managing subcultures is not to eliminate them — that is neither possible nor desirable. The art is to create enough shared culture across the organization that the subcultures can communicate and collaborate, while still allowing each subculture the autonomy it needs to do its work effectively.

**How to Think About Subcultures.** I tell leaders to think of their organization as a society, not a machine. A society has many groups with different values and practices, but there is an overarching culture that holds them together. The same should be true of an organization.

The key is to be clear about which values and assumptions are mandatory across the organization and which can vary by group. At the highest level, things like integrity, respect, and a commitment to the mission should be universal. But below that, there should be room for difference. Engineering doesn't need to operate like sales. The Tokyo office doesn't need to operate like New York.

The most dangerous assumption is that "our culture" must be uniform. When you try to impose a single culture on diverse groups, you create resistance and resentment. You crush the very diversity that makes the organization strong. The goal is not cultural uniformity — it's cultural coherence. A common framework that allows for difference within agreed boundaries.

**How Subcultures Interact.** Subcultures interact in three basic patterns. The first is integration: subcultures work together harmoniously, each contributing its unique perspective while sharing overarching values. This is the ideal state, but it requires constant management and communication.

The second is differentiation: subcultures operate in parallel, each autonomous in its own domain. Engineering builds products. Sales sells them. Marketing markets them. They do not need deep integration. But this works only when there is enough shared understanding to prevent the subcultures from working at cross-purposes.

The third is fragmentation: subcultures are in active conflict. Engineering blames sales for promising what cannot be built. Sales blames marketing for not generating enough leads. Marketing blames engineering for building products that are hard to market. The organization is not a team — it is a collection of warring tribes, each protecting its own interests at the expense of the whole.

The art of managing subcultures is recognizing which pattern you are in and acting accordingly. Integration requires investment in shared experiences and communication. Differentiation requires clear boundaries and handoff protocols. Fragmentation requires intervention — sometimes leadership change, sometimes restructuring, sometimes letting go of people who cannot work across boundaries.

### 6. Culture Change

I have spent some of the most difficult years of my career watching organizations try to change their culture. And most of them fail. Not because they lack resources or intelligence or commitment. Because they misunderstand what culture change actually requires.

Let me be very clear: culture change is the hardest thing an organization can attempt. It is harder than a new strategy. Harder than a merger. Harder than a restructuring. Because culture change requires changing the basic assumptions that people hold at an unconscious level. And people do not give up their assumptions easily.

I developed a model for culture change that has three stages: unfreezing, changing, and refreezing. It sounds simple. It is not. I have watched brilliant CEOs fail at culture change because they thought it was a project with a timeline, like a product launch or a restructuring. Culture change is not a project. It is a fundamental relearning process that touches every aspect of the organization.

The model is derived from Kurt Lewin's work on change theory, which I have adapted and refined through decades of consulting. Lewin understood something that most leaders still do not: that change is not about adding something new. It is about unfreezing the old, so that the new can take hold. You cannot paint a new culture on top of an old one. You must first create the space for the new culture to grow.

**Stage 1: Unfreeze.** The first step is to create sufficient motivation to change. People don't change their deeply held assumptions unless they have to. As long as the current culture feels comfortable and seems to be working, there is no motivation to change.

Unfreezing happens when three conditions are met. First, there must be enough disconfirming data — evidence that the current way of doing things is not working. Second, the disconfirming data must be connected to the organization's goals and values — people must feel that the failure is consequential. Third, there must be enough psychological safety — people must believe that change is possible and that they will survive the process.

Most culture change failures happen at this stage. Leaders try to skip the unfreezing. They announce a new culture without first creating the motivation for change. And people rightly respond: "Why? Things are fine. What's wrong with how we do things?" Without a crisis — or a compelling case that a crisis is coming — culture change is impossible.

The most effective culture change leaders are often the ones who deliberately surface the crisis. They make the case that the current culture is not just suboptimal — it's dangerous. They create the discomfort that makes change necessary. This is uncomfortable leadership. It means highlighting failures and problems that people would rather ignore. But it is necessary.

Let me give you a concrete example of unfreezing from my own experience. I worked with a technology company that had a deeply embedded culture of hierarchy and deference to authority. Decisions were made at the top and cascaded down. No one questioned senior leaders. The culture had developed because the founder was a brilliant visionary who had been right about everything for a decade. But the company had grown to 5,000 people, and the founder could no longer make every decision. The culture of deference was now a liability — important issues were not being raised because no one wanted to disagree with a senior person.

The CEO recognized the problem but could not change the culture by simply announcing a new value. The unfreezing came when a junior engineer raised a serious product quality issue in a meeting, and a senior vice president dismissed it. The product failed in the market, costing the company millions. The CEO used that failure to create a cultural crisis. He held a company-wide meeting where he took responsibility for creating a culture where people were afraid to speak up. He made the case that the culture of deference was not just uncomfortable — it was dangerous. He shared the financial cost. He shared the personal cost. He created the discomfort that made change possible.

This is uncomfortable leadership. It means highlighting failures and problems that people would rather ignore. But it is necessary.

**Stage 2: Change.** Once people are unfrozen, they are ready to learn new assumptions and new behaviors. This is the stage where the actual culture change work happens — and it is the stage that requires the most sustained attention.

Change works through three mechanisms: (1) identifying with a new role model, (2) learning from experience, and (3) systematic reinforcement of new behaviors.

The most powerful mechanism is role modeling. When a leader consistently demonstrates new behaviors, people watch, learn, and begin to adopt those behaviors themselves. This is why culture change always starts at the top. If the CEO doesn't model the new culture, no one will believe it's real.

Learning from experience means giving people opportunities to practice new behaviors and see that they work. If you want a culture of innovation, give people innovation projects and let them succeed (and fail) in safe ways. The experience of success with a new behavior is more powerful than any training program.

Systematic reinforcement means aligning every organizational system with the new culture. Reward systems must reward the new behaviors. Promotion decisions must elevate people who embody the new culture. Hiring criteria must select for the new values. If the old systems remain in place, they will pull people back to the old culture.

**Stage 3: Refreeze.** The final stage is stabilizing the new culture — embedding the new assumptions so they become as taken-for-granted as the old ones were.

Refreezing happens through institutionalization. The new behaviors become encoded in policies, procedures, structures, and rituals. They become the "way we do things around here." New people are socialized into the new culture. Old artifacts are replaced.

It's important to note that "refreeze" doesn't mean the culture becomes permanent. It means the new culture becomes stable enough to serve as a platform for further evolution. A culture that is constantly changing is not a culture at all — it's chaos. But a culture that never changes is a trap.

**Why Culture Change Fails.** Most culture change fails because leaders try to change artifacts and espoused values without touching basic assumptions. They put up new posters. They write new mission statements. They launch a "values initiative." And nothing changes, because the basic assumptions remain the same.

Culture change fails when leaders don't invest the time. Real culture change takes three to ten years, depending on the size of the organization and the depth of the change. That's not a project timeline. That's a generational shift.

Culture change fails when leaders don't model the change. You cannot delegate culture change to HR. You cannot announce it in a town hall and expect it to happen. The leader must live the new culture every day, in every decision, because everyone is watching for signs that the change is not real.

Culture change fails when leaders don't change the systems. You can talk about a culture of innovation all you want, but if your budgeting process punishes failure, your reward system pays for predictability, and your promotion system elevates cautious executives, the culture will not change. The systems will defeat the vision.

And culture change fails when leaders give up. Culture change is exhausting. There are setbacks. There are moments when the old culture reasserts itself. There are people who resist. The difference between success and failure is often simply that the successful leaders kept going when the unsuccessful leaders stopped.

I have seen the pattern many times. A new CEO announces a culture change initiative. There is enthusiasm for six months. Then the quarterly results come in, and they are not as good as expected. The board starts asking questions. The CEO loses focus. The culture change initiative gets deprioritized. The old culture creeps back. Two years later, the CEO is frustrated that "nothing changed." But the truth is that the change was never given the sustained attention it required.

The most successful culture change I have witnessed took seven years from initiation to stabilization. It was led by a CEO who made culture the number one priority for almost a decade. Every quarter, he reviewed cultural metrics alongside financial metrics. Every town hall, he talked about the cultural journey. Every time there was a setback — and there were many — he used it as a teaching moment rather than an excuse to abandon the effort. He understood that culture change is not a sprint or even a marathon. It is a permanent shift in how the organization operates, and it requires permanent attention.

The hardest truth about culture change is this: you will never be done. Culture requires ongoing maintenance, ongoing reinforcement, ongoing evolution. The moment you stop paying attention, the culture will begin to drift. It will not drift toward perfection. It will drift toward entropy — toward the path of least resistance, toward the informal power structures, toward the way things have always been done. A healthy culture is not a destination. It is a continuous practice.

## Case Studies

### Case 1: The Netflix Culture Deck — Freedom and Responsibility

In 2009, Reed Hastings published a slide deck that would become the most famous statement of organizational culture ever written. It was called "Netflix Culture: Freedom & Responsibility," and it was remarkably honest about the nature of the employment relationship. The deck did not pull punches. It did not use euphemisms. It told employees exactly what the deal was: we are not a family, adequate performance is not enough, and you are responsible for your own success here.

The context matters. In 2009, Netflix was in the middle of a difficult transition from DVD-by-mail to streaming. The company had nearly collapsed in 2008. Hastings realized that the old culture — built for a logistics company that shipped red envelopes — was not going to work for a technology company that streamed video. He needed a culture that could attract and retain the kind of talent that could build a streaming platform. He needed engineers who could handle ambiguity and complexity. He needed a culture that could move fast and make good decisions without layers of approval. The culture deck was his answer to that challenge.

The deck began with a bold statement: "We're a team, not a family." This was a deliberate provocation. Many companies describe themselves as families. Families, Hastings argued, are based on unconditional love. You don't fire family members when they underperform. But teams are different. Teams are assembled to achieve a specific goal. If a team member is not performing at the level required, they should be replaced — generously, respectfully, but decisively.

This assumption — "we are a team, not a family" — is the deepest layer of the Netflix culture. It shapes everything else. If you accept that assumption, the rest of the culture makes sense. If you reject it, the rest seems harsh or even cruel.

**The Key Principles.** The culture deck articulated several principles that became famous in their own right.

"Freedom and Responsibility." Netflix had no vacation policy. No formal tracking of vacation days. The assumption was simple: adults should be able to manage their own time. If you get your work done and don't abuse the system, you can take as much vacation as you want. The same applied to expenses: no formal expense policy, just "act in Netflix's best interest."

"Context, not Control." The traditional management model is control: set rules, monitor compliance, punish violations. Netflix proposed an alternative: give people context — information about strategy, goals, competitive dynamics — and then trust them to make good decisions. Instead of requiring approvals, provide information and let people decide.

"Adequate performance gets a generous severance package." This was the most controversial principle. If an employee was doing a "solid" job but not an exceptional one, Netflix would give them a generous severance — typically four months of pay — and let them go. The logic: one adequate performer on a team of all-stars drags everyone down. The organization should be composed entirely of A-players.

"Highly Aligned, Loosely Coupled." Teams should be tightly aligned on goals and strategy but loosely coupled in how they execute. They should coordinate when necessary but otherwise operate autonomously.

But the culture deck was not just a set of policies. It was a system of mutually reinforcing beliefs. "Freedom and Responsibility" was not a standalone idea. It was connected to "Context, not Control." It was connected to "Highly Aligned, Loosely Coupled." It was connected to "Adequate Performance Gets a Generous Severance." Each policy depended on the others. You could not have freedom without the performance bar. You could not have context without alignment. You could not have loose coupling without trust.

This is what made the Netflix culture so hard to copy. Companies that copied the no-vacation policy without the performance standards ended up with people taking advantage of the system. Companies that copied the generous severance without the culture of candor ended up with lawsuits and resentment. The policies were not independent. They were a system.

**Why It Worked.** The Netflix culture worked because it was internally consistent. Every policy reinforced every other policy. The no-vacation policy only worked because Netflix was clear about performance expectations and because people who abused trust were quickly removed. The "context, not control" approach only worked because the culture was clear about what mattered and because people who couldn't handle ambiguity didn't last.

The culture was also remarkably honest. Most companies say "people are our most important asset" and then treat them like costs. Netflix said "adequate performance gets a generous severance" — a statement that was brutally honest about the employment deal. You're here as long as you're exceptional. When you're not, you'll be treated well, but you'll be asked to leave.

This honesty, paradoxically, created more trust, not less. Employees knew exactly where they stood. They knew what was expected. They knew the consequences. There were no surprises. And there was no pretense that the company would take care of them regardless of performance.

**The Criticism.** But the Netflix model has been criticized extensively. Critics argue that it creates a culture of fear — not the overt fear of a toxic environment, but a subtler anxiety. When everyone knows that "adequate" is not enough, when the bar is constantly rising, when there is no safety net, people can become exhausted. The constant pressure to be exceptional can be dehumanizing.

There is also the question of whether the model works for all types of work. Netflix's model may work for knowledge workers in a high-demand field like technology, where talent is scarce and the market rewards excellence. But would it work for a hospital? A school? A manufacturer? The assumptions that drive Netflix's culture are not universal.

And there is the problem of what happens to the people who are "generously severanced." However generous the package — and four months' pay is genuinely generous — being told you're not exceptional is painful. The model treats people as resources to be optimized rather than as human beings with worth beyond their current output.

**The Lesson.** The Netflix culture deck teaches us that a great culture is not necessarily a comfortable culture. Netflix's culture is demanding, honest, and unapologetically focused on performance. It works because the people who thrive there share the assumptions that drive it — that exceptional performance matters, that feedback is a gift, that teams are not families.

But it also teaches us that culture cannot be copied. Many companies tried to adopt Netflix's policies — no vacation, generous severance — without adopting the underlying assumptions. They failed because they dismantled the artifacts without understanding the beliefs that made them work. You cannot have no vacation policy if your culture doesn't trust people to manage their own time. You cannot have generous severance if your culture doesn't value candor.

The culture deck is not a template. It is a case study in cultural coherence — one set of assumptions, consistently applied.

### Case 2: Amazon's Leadership Principles

If Netflix's culture is built on a single, coherent set of assumptions about freedom and responsibility, Amazon's culture is built on 16 written principles that govern every aspect of the business. These are the Leadership Principles, and they are not aspirational statements on a wall. They are the operating system of the company. They govern hiring. They govern firing. They govern promotions. They govern meetings. They govern strategy. They govern day-to-day decision-making. There is no aspect of Amazon that the Leadership Principles do not touch.

The origin of these principles is instructive. Jeff Bezos wrote the original set in the late 1990s, when Amazon was still a struggling online bookstore. He was frustrated by the difficulty of scaling culture as the company grew. Early employees had absorbed his assumptions through direct exposure — they worked with him, they watched him make decisions, they learned his values through observation. But as Amazon grew from dozens to hundreds to thousands of employees, that direct transmission became impossible. Bezos needed a way to encode his assumptions so that every new hire could learn them without spending years working directly with him.

The Leadership Principles are, in essence, a codification of Jeff Bezos's basic assumptions about business. They are what Bezos believed about customers, about competition, about decision-making, about standards, about hiring. And because they were written down and operationalized, they became self-perpetuating. Each new hire is selected for alignment with the principles. Each new hire is socialized into them. Each new hire is evaluated against them. The principles are not just values — they are a replicating system.

**The Principles.** The 16 principles cover a wide range: Customer Obsession, Ownership, Invent and Simplify, Are Right, A Lot, Learn and Be Curious, Hire and Develop the Best, Insist on the Highest Standards, Think Big, Bias for Action, Frugality, Earn Trust, Dive Deep, Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit, Deliver Results.

Each principle has a detailed description. "Customer Obsession" is not just "focus on the customer." It is: "Leaders start with the customer and work backwards. They work vigorously to earn and keep customer trust. Although leaders pay attention to competitors, they obsess over customers."

"Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit" is: "Leaders are obligated to respectfully challenge decisions when they disagree, even when doing so is uncomfortable or exhausting. Leaders have conviction and are tenacious. They do not compromise for the sake of social cohesion. Once a decision is determined, they commit wholly."

"Bias for Action" is: "Speed matters in business. Many decisions and actions are reversible and do not need extensive study. We value calculated risk taking."

**Operationalization.** What makes Amazon's principles different is how deeply they are embedded in the company's operations. This is not a list of nice-sounding values. It is a system of evaluation and decision-making.

Every hiring decision includes interviewers who are "bar raisers" — people whose specific job is to ensure that every new hire meets the culture bar. These bar raisers are not part of the hiring team; they are independent evaluators who report to HR. They can veto any hire, even if the hiring manager disagrees.

Every job interview includes "leadership principle" questions. Candidates are asked to describe specific situations where they demonstrated each principle. "Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager." "Tell me about a time you took a calculated risk." "Tell me about a time you insisted on the highest standards despite pressure to compromise."

Every employee's performance review includes assessment against the leadership principles. Promotion decisions are based on them. The principles are discussed in meetings. They are referenced in decisions. They are the language of the company.

Meetings themselves reflect the culture. Amazon meetings famously start with silent reading of a six-page memo — no PowerPoint allowed. The assumption is that PowerPoint encourages superficial thinking while writing a narrative forces depth. The first 30 minutes of a meeting are silent reading, followed by discussion. This is a cultural artifact that embodies a deep assumption: that deep thinking is more important than polished presentation.

**The Strength of Strong Principles.** The Amazon system shows the power of strong, operationalized cultural principles. Decisions are faster because there is a shared framework. Alignment is higher because everyone uses the same language. The culture becomes self-reinforcing because every new hire is selected and socialized into the same assumptions.

"Disagree and Commit" is a particularly powerful principle. It solves a fundamental problem in organizations: the tension between debate and action. Many organizations either have endless debate (analysis paralysis) or premature consensus (groupthink). "Disagree and Commit" says: debate fiercely, but once a decision is made, commit fully. Jeff Bezos once wrote: "If you're a senior vice president and you disagree with a decision, you owe me a direct email explaining why. But once I make the decision, you execute as if it were your own."

Principle 2.

This principle prevents two common pathologies: the person who agrees in the meeting but sabotages afterwards, and the person who stays silent in the meeting but complains later. It creates both the space for dissent and the expectation of commitment.

**The Dark Side.** But the Amazon culture has a dark side, and it is important to examine it honestly. I include this not to criticize Amazon — I include it because every strong culture has a dark side, and pretending otherwise is dangerous. If you are building a strong culture, you must be aware of what you are creating, including its costs.

The intensity of the principles can create a brutal environment. Employees describe "purposeful Darwinism" — a culture where people are constantly evaluated and ranked, where the bottom performers are systematically weeded out. The stack ranking system (which Amazon has officially eliminated but many say persists in practice) creates internal competition that can undermine collaboration. When everyone knows that someone will be ranked at the bottom and eventually removed, the incentive to help colleagues is diminished. The culture of "ownership" can become a culture of blame — when everything is your responsibility, failures become personal rather than systemic.

The stack ranking system (which Amazon has officially eliminated but many say persists in practice) creates internal competition that can undermine collaboration.

The principles can be weaponized. Employees learn to use the principles to argue against anything. Someone who opposes a proposal can frame their opposition in terms of "Customer Obsession" or "Frugality" or "Insist on the Highest Standards." The principles become a rhetorical tool rather than a genuine guide.

The culture of high standards can become perfectionism that prevents action. The "Bias for Action" principle is supposed to counteract this, but in practice, the fear of being wrong can overwhelm the willingness to act.

And the culture can be emotionally punishing. Multiple investigative articles have documented stories of employees crying at their desks, of people with serious illnesses afraid to take time off, of a system that demands everything and gives little in return. Amazon's turnover rate is high, and the company has been criticized for its treatment of both white-collar and blue-collar workers.

**The Lesson.** Amazon's culture teaches us that strong cultural principles, deeply operationalized, create powerful alignment — but they also create powerful pressure. The same system that produces extraordinary results at scale can also produce extraordinary human cost.

The lesson is not that Amazon's culture is good or bad. The lesson is that culture is a system of trade-offs. You cannot have the alignment of strong principles without the pressure of strong accountability. You cannot have the speed of "Disagree and Commit" without the discomfort of constant disagreement. You cannot have the excellence of "Insist on the Highest Standards" without the anxiety of never feeling good enough.

The question is not whether a culture is "good" or "bad." The question is: what are you willing to tolerate? Amazon's culture is willing to tolerate high turnover, intense pressure, and the emotional cost of constant evaluation in exchange for extraordinary performance. That is a choice. And it is a choice that every organization makes, whether it knows it or not.

### Case 3: Uber's Toxic Culture Under Travis Kalanick

In 2017, a former Uber engineer named Susan Fowler published a blog post titled "Reflecting on one very, very strange year at Uber." It described a culture of systemic harassment, sexism, and dysfunction. The company had publicly celebrated its "hustle" culture. Now that culture was being exposed for what it really was.

The story of Uber's toxic culture is the story of how assumptions that drive success in one phase can drive destruction in the next. Travis Kalanick built Uber into a $70 billion company by being relentless, aggressive, and willing to break rules. Those same qualities nearly destroyed the company.

**The Cultural Norms.** Under Kalanick, Uber developed a set of cultural norms that were explicit, celebrated, and dangerous.

"Always Be Hustling." This was the defining norm. Growth at all costs. Move faster than anyone. Break things. In Uber's early years, this norm was a competitive advantage. The company entered new cities without regulatory approval. It launched in defiance of taxi commissions. It grew at a pace that seemed impossible. But "Always Be Hustling" also meant that ethical boundaries were constantly pushed. It meant that employees were expected to sacrifice everything for growth — their health, their relationships, their integrity. It meant that anything that slowed down — including legal compliance, safety concerns, and human decency — was seen as weakness.

Kalanick's "Always Be Hustling" did not just encourage hard work. It encouraged rule-breaking. Uber's growth was built on a foundation of regulatory defiance, and that defiance became a cultural value. The company prided itself on being willing to do what competitors would not. That pride, initially a source of strength, became a source of blindness. The company could not see that what made it successful in its startup phase — breaking rules, moving fast, ignoring critics — would become toxic as it scaled into a global corporation with thousands of employees and millions of users.

"Toe-stepping." Kalanick explicitly encouraged competition as war. Uber's early playbook was built on the assumption that the taxi industry was corrupt and needed to be destroyed. This created a siege mentality: the world is against us, and we must fight. That mentality justified aggressive tactics: targeting competitors (the "Hell" program to poach Lyft drivers), misleading regulators (the "Greyball" tool to identify and deceive enforcement officials), and treating competitors as enemies rather than market participants.

"Principled Confrontation." The company valued aggressive debate. People were encouraged to challenge each other, to push back, to be direct. In theory, this is a healthy cultural norm — the kind of "creative abrasion" that produces better decisions. In practice, at Uber, it became an excuse for bullying. Disagreement became personal attacks. Directness became cruelty.

**The Consequences.** The results of this culture were predictable, though they seemed to surprise the company when they arrived. What is remarkable about Uber's story is not that the culture became toxic — it is that everyone in the company could see it happening and no one could stop it. The culture was so strong, the assumptions so deeply embedded, that the organization could not self-correct. It took an external crisis — the Fowler blog post, the video, the investigations — to force change that should have been initiated internally years earlier.

The harassment claims. Susan Fowler's blog post described a system where harassment was reported and ignored, where HR was seen as protecting high-performing harassers, where women who spoke up were told their performance was the problem. Her post triggered an avalanche of similar stories from current and former employees.

The leaked video. In 2017, dashcam footage emerged of Kalanick arguing with an Uber driver about fare reductions. The video showed the CEO of a $70 billion company screaming at a driver: "Some people don't like to take responsibility for their own shit. They blame everything in their life on somebody else." The video became a symbol of everything wrong with Uber's culture — arrogance, disrespect, and a complete failure to understand how the culture looked from the outside.

The Greyball investigation. The Department of Justice opened an investigation into Uber's use of "Greyball" — a tool that identified and evaded regulators. The company had used technology to break the law. The assumption that "growth at all costs" justified anything had led directly to criminal behavior.

The talent exodus. Top executives left. Engineers who had joined Uber for the challenge left for companies with better cultures. The company that had once been the most desirable destination in Silicon Valley became a place people were embarrassed to work.

The regulatory backlash. Cities around the world pushed back against Uber's tactics. London revoked Uber's license. New York imposed caps. The regulatory strategy that had worked when Uber was a startup — ask forgiveness, not permission — stopped working when Uber was a global giant.

**The Outcome.** In June 2017, Travis Kalanick was forced out of the company he founded — pushed out by the very board that had previously celebrated his relentless growth. Uber's valuation dropped by roughly 30%. The company that had once been valued at $70 billion was suddenly worth $48 billion. The brand that had been synonymous with innovation became synonymous with scandal.

The new CEO, Dara Khosrowshahi, took over with a mandate to fundamentally change the culture. His approach is a case study in how to begin the unfreezing process. He did not try to change everything at once. He focused on the most critical changes. He replaced the cultural values — "Always Be Hustling" became "We Do the Right Thing." He brought in new leadership from outside the company. He committed to transparency and accountability. He acknowledged the damage and apologized — genuinely, specifically, without defensiveness.

But Khosrowshahi also understood something that many culture change leaders miss: you cannot change culture by changing values alone. You have to change the systems. He changed the hiring criteria. He changed the performance review process. He changed the promotion standards. He invested in diversity and inclusion programs. He created reporting mechanisms for harassment and discrimination. He put HR in a position of power rather than a service function.

And yet, culture change at Uber has been slow and painful. The old assumptions are deeply embedded. Many of the people who built the old culture still work there. The pressure to return to the high-growth, high-aggression model is constant, especially when competitors like Lyft are gaining market share. And the scars of the old culture — the lost trust, the damaged reputation, the legal exposure — will take years to heal, if they ever fully do.

The Uber story is ongoing. As of this writing, the company is still navigating the tension between its old and new selves. The question is not whether Khosrowshahi can change the culture — it is whether the culture can change fast enough to save the company from the consequences of its own past.

**The Lesson.** Uber's story teaches us that culture is not a "nice to have." It is a risk management tool. Toxic culture destroys value. It creates legal exposure. It drives away talent. It invites regulatory scrutiny. And the bill always comes due.

Kalanick was a brilliant entrepreneur who built a revolutionary product and a world-changing company. But his cultural assumptions — that growth justifies anything, that competition is war, that aggression is strength — created a system that eventually consumed him. The same man who built Uber also built the culture that destroyed his leadership.

The tragedy of Uber is that the cultural assumptions that made the company successful in its early years — speed, aggression, rule-breaking — were exactly the assumptions that became toxic as the company scaled. What works in a startup with 50 people does not work in a global corporation with 20,000 people. The assumptions that bring you success at one stage become the source of failure at the next.

The lesson for every leader is this: pay attention to the culture you are creating, because it will outlast you. The assumptions you embed in the organization will continue to drive behavior long after you are gone. If those assumptions are toxic, the toxicity will compound. And one day, the bill will come due.

## How to Use This Tomorrow

Culture is not a project. It is a practice. Here is how to start practicing today.

**First, diagnose your culture honestly.** Do not read the mission statement. Do not look at the wall. Walk the halls and observe. What happens when someone makes a mistake? Do people surface the mistake and learn from it, or do they hide it and blame someone else? What happens when someone disagrees with the boss? Is it welcomed as healthy debate, or is it punished as insubordination? Who gets promoted, and why? Look at the last five promotions and ask: what did these people actually do to earn them? What behaviors are actually rewarded — not in the performance review form, but in who gets the bonus, the promotion, the corner office? The gap between what you say you value and what you actually reward is where your real culture lives.

**Second, look for the basic assumptions.** Ask yourself: what are the taken-for-granted beliefs in this organization that no one ever questions? Ask your colleagues — in private, with the promise of anonymity: "What do you have to believe to succeed here?" The answer — what people actually believe they need to believe — is the real culture. Also ask: "What can never be said here?" The topics that cannot be raised, the questions that cannot be asked, the truths that cannot be spoken — those are the most powerful indicators of basic assumptions. A culture that cannot discuss its own failings is a culture that cannot change.

**Third, identify the subcultures.** Who are the different groups in your organization? What are their different assumptions? Where do they align? Where do they conflict? Do not try to eliminate the differences. That is a fool's errand. Try to create a common framework that allows difference within boundaries. The goal is not one culture. The goal is a culture that can hold multiple subcultures in productive tension.

**Fourth, if you want to change the culture, start with the systems.** Do not write a new mission statement. That is level 1. Go to level 3. Change the hiring criteria. Change the promotion standards. Change the reward systems. Change who gets power. Change the meeting structures. Change the decision-making processes. Culture is embedded in systems, and systems are the most powerful lever for culture change. Change the systems, and the culture will follow — slowly, reluctantly, but it will follow.

**Fifth, model the culture you want.** People are watching you all the time. They are watching what you pay attention to. They are watching how you react to bad news. They are watching who you promote. They are watching what you tolerate. Your behavior is the most powerful cultural signal in the organization. If you want a culture of learning, you must model learning — admit when you are wrong, ask questions, seek feedback. If you want a culture of candor, you must model candor — give direct feedback, encourage dissent, listen to criticism. If you want a culture of respect, you must model respect — even when it's hard, especially when it's hard, most of all when no one is watching.

**Sixth, be patient.** Culture change takes years, not months. It takes longer than most leaders have the patience for. It takes longer than most boards allow. But the only alternative is to let the culture drift — and that is not an alternative at all. Culture will develop whether you manage it or not. The only question is whether it will develop in a direction that serves the organization.

I have one more piece of advice for leaders, and it is the most important. If you take only one thing from this chapter, take this: pay attention to the culture you are creating, because it will outlast you. The assumptions you embed today will drive behavior for years after you are gone. Every decision you make — who you hire, who you promote, what you reward, what you tolerate, how you react to bad news, what you laugh at, what you get angry about — is a cultural signal. You are building culture whether you know it or not. The only question is whether you are building it intentionally or accidentally.

And finally, remember: culture is not a lever you pull. It is a garden you tend. You cannot force the plants to grow. You can only create the conditions for growth — the right soil, the right water, the right light — and then wait. And if you are patient, if you are consistent, if you tend the garden every day, something will grow. It may not be what you expected. It may take longer than you wanted. But it will be real.

Most leaders are terrible gardeners. They want to yell at the plants to grow faster. They want to pull them up to check the roots. They want to redesign the garden every season. But a good gardener knows that the work is slow, invisible, and cumulative. You prepare the soil. You plant the seeds. You water. You wait. And one day, you have a garden.

That is culture. And it is the most important thing you will ever build.

I have spent over sixty years studying organizational culture. I have seen it destroy companies and save them. I have watched brilliant leaders fail because they could not understand the culture they had created. I have watched ordinary leaders achieve extraordinary things because they understood how to tend the cultural garden.

If I have one message for you, it is this: do not take culture for granted. Do not assume it will take care of itself. Do not think that because you are not managing it, it does not exist. Culture is always operating, always shaping behavior, always determining what is possible and what is not. The only question is whether you will shape it intentionally or let it shape you accidentally.

You are building culture with every decision you make. Every person you hire. Every person you promote. Every behavior you tolerate. Every conversation you have. Every email you send. Every meeting you run. Every reaction you have to good news and bad. You are building culture constantly, whether you know it or not.

So know it. Pay attention. Be intentional. And remember: you are not a culture architect. You are a gardener. Your job is not to design the perfect culture and install it. Your job is to create the conditions for a healthy culture to grow — and then to tend it, patiently, persistently, every single day.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Culture exists at three levels:** artifacts (visible but hard to interpret), espoused values (what people say they believe), and basic underlying assumptions (what people actually believe). The deepest level — basic assumptions — is the most influential and the hardest to change.

2. **Culture comes from founders, critical incidents, and accumulated learning.** The founder's assumptions become the seed. How the organization handles crises reveals its true values. What gets rewarded becomes embedded in the culture.

3. **Culture eats strategy for breakfast — but it can also eat the company.** A strong culture enables execution, but it can also create blindness, insularity, and resistance to necessary change.

4. **Strong cultures have clear trade-offs.** Alignment and efficiency come at the cost of potential groupthink and rigidity. Weak cultures have costs too: confusion, inconsistency, and excessive formal controls.

5. **Subcultures are not a problem; they are reality.** Every organization has multiple cultures — by function, level, geography, and tenure. The art is creating coherence without uniformity.

6. **Culture change requires unfreezing, changing, and refreezing.** Most change efforts fail because leaders skip the unfreezing (creating motivation to change) or try to change artifacts without touching assumptions.

7. **Toxic cultures destroy value.** Uber is not an exception; it is a warning. The assumptions that drive success in one phase can become the source of destruction in the next.

8. **You cannot copy culture.** The Netflix deck and Amazon's principles are not templates. They are expressions of specific assumptions in specific contexts. Copy the artifacts without understanding the assumptions, and you get the surface without the substance.

9. **The leader is the most powerful cultural signal.** Your behavior — what you pay attention to, how you react, who you promote — communicates more about culture than any mission statement ever could.

10. **Culture is a garden, not a lever.** You tend it patiently, consistently, over years. You cannot force it. You can only create the conditions. And that is both the hardest and most important work a leader does.
