# Chapter 27: The Only Constant — Leading Change

In 1993, IBM was not a company in trouble. It was a company already presumed dead.

The numbers were staggering. IBM lost $8.1 billion that year — at the time, the largest single-year corporate loss in American history. The company had been the undisputed king of computing for decades, the archetype of the modern corporation, the stock that widows and orphans held because it was the safest bet on earth. Its mainframe business, once a license to print money, was being gutted by cheaper client-server systems from competitors like Sun Microsystems and Compaq. Its PC business, which IBM had pioneered, was being crushed by clones running Microsoft Windows. Its legendary salesforce, once the most feared in the industry, had become a bloated bureaucracy that couldn't close a deal without a committee of approvals. The company had cut 60,000 jobs in 1992, 45,000 more in 1993, and it still wasn't enough. The conventional wisdom on Wall Street was that IBM needed to be broken up — split into independent companies for hardware, software, and services — to unlock any value at all.

The board responded by doing something that seemed insane. They hired Lou Gerstner — a man who had spent his entire career running food companies. RJR Nabisco. American Express. McKinsey. The man had never written a line of code, never designed a computer, never managed a technology product. He ran a cookie company.

The business press was merciless. *Fortune* called Gerstner "the cookie man." *The Wall Street Journal* questioned whether any outsider could possibly understand IBM's culture, let alone change it. Gerstner himself later admitted that when the headhunter first called, he assumed they were offering him a board seat, not the CEO job. "I was not looking for another CEO position," he wrote. "I especially was not looking for a troubled company in a troubled industry."

But Gerstner took the job, and what followed was one of the most remarkable corporate turnarounds in history. He did not break up IBM. He did not sell off the pieces. Instead, he kept the company together, changed its culture from the inside out, and within three years had returned IBM to profitability. By 1997, IBM was generating $7 billion in net income. The company that had been written off as a dinosaur was, once again, the most valuable technology company in the world.

Gerstner's turnaround is often taught as a strategy story, or a leadership story, or a culture story. It is all of those things. But at its deepest level, it is a change story — a masterclass in what it actually takes to transform a large, complex, deeply entrenched organization. Everything Gerstner did, from his first week on the job to his final year as CEO, maps directly onto the principles that decades of research have shown distinguish successful change efforts from failed ones.

And most change efforts fail. The data is consistent and sobering: roughly 70 percent of all major organizational change initiatives do not achieve their objectives. This is not because the strategies are wrong, or the leaders are incompetent, or the employees are resistant. It is because the people leading the change do not understand how change actually happens. They treat it as a rational process — announce the new direction, provide the incentives, expect compliance — when in reality, change is an emotional, psychological, and deeply human process. Leaders who do not understand this are not leading change. They are issuing commands and hoping for the best.

This chapter will give you a framework for leading change that actually works. It is grounded in three decades of research on why change succeeds and why it fails. You will learn the 8-step model that has been used by thousands of organizations around the world. You will understand why people resist change even when it is in their own interest. You will see what it takes to create genuine urgency, build a coalition that can drive transformation, and anchor new behaviors in the culture so they survive beyond the current leader. And you will study three of the most instructive change stories in business history — IBM's reinvention, Nokia's collapse, and Lego's near-death and rebirth.

If you take only one thing from this chapter, let it be this: change does not fail at the strategy level. It fails at the human level. If you want to lead change, you must learn to lead people.

## Why Change Is Hard

Let us start with the most fundamental question: why is change so difficult? Not just in organizations, but in human beings. The answer lies partly in our biology, partly in our psychology, and partly in the way organizations are structured.

### The Brain Science

When a perceived threat enters your awareness, your brain's amygdala — the ancient, almond-shaped cluster of nuclei that processes emotional reactions — activates within milliseconds. This is the same circuitry that made your ancestors freeze when they heard a rustle in the grass. It is designed for survival, not for reasoned decision-making. And modern neuroscience has shown that the brain processes change — even positive change — through the same threat-detection pathways.

Here is the practical implication: when you tell an employee that the company is restructuring, or that their role is changing, or that the strategy is shifting, their brain does not process that information as a neutral data point. It processes it as a potential threat. The amygdala hijacks the prefrontal cortex — the seat of rational thought — and the person's capacity for clear analysis, creative problem-solving, and open-minded engagement drops significantly. They are not being difficult. They are being human.

This is why change communication that relies entirely on logic — spreadsheets, PowerPoint decks, strategic rationales — so often fails to produce the intended response. You are speaking to the prefrontal cortex of people whose amygdala is screaming at them to be careful. You cannot argue someone out of a threat response. You have to address the emotion first.

### The Certainty Premium

Beyond the biological response, there is a psychological preference that makes change particularly hard: human beings consistently prefer the certainty of a bad situation to the uncertainty of a new one.

This is not a personality flaw. It is a deeply rational response to how our brains assess risk. When you know the current situation — even if it is objectively bad — you know what to expect. You know how to navigate it. You know the unwritten rules, the informal hierarchies, the ways to protect yourself. Change replaces that known territory with an unknown one, and the unknown could always be worse. Your brain, which evolved to prioritize survival above all else, therefore treats the unknown as a threat by default.

Organizational researchers have documented this phenomenon across industries and cultures. Employees who know their company is failing will still resist restructuring because at least they understand the current failure. Middle managers who know their department is inefficient will still resist process changes because the old inefficiencies are predictable. Executives who know their strategy is obsolete will still resist pivoting because the new strategy carries career risk. The certainty premium — the psychological value people place on the known, even when the known is bad — is one of the most powerful forces working against any change effort.

### Organizational Inertia

Individual psychology is only half the problem. The other half is that organizations are designed to resist change. Every element of a well-run company — its systems, its processes, its culture, its incentives, its norms — is optimized for stability. That is the point. Organizations exist to coordinate human activity toward predictable outcomes, and that requires creating stable patterns of behavior that persist over time. The very features that make an organization effective in stable times are the features that make it resistant to change.

Consider the typical annual planning process. Budgets are set. Targets are assigned. Incentives are calibrated. Compensation plans are locked in. All of these mechanisms assume that the future will look roughly like the present. When a leader announces a major change three months into the fiscal year, they are asking the organization to override every one of those mechanisms. The sales team is still being paid to sell the old product. The manufacturing team is still being measured on the old efficiency metrics. The R&D team is still funded through the old project allocation. The systems, processes, and incentives are all pulling in the old direction, and the leader is wondering why people aren't moving in the new one.

Culture adds another layer of inertia. Culture is not what an organization says in its mission statement. It is what people actually do, what they reward, what they punish, what they celebrate, what they ridicule. Culture is the accumulated set of unwritten rules that have evolved over years or decades of shared experience. And culture is extraordinarily durable. A CEO can change the strategy, the structure, the systems, and the incentives, and find that six months later, people are still behaving exactly as they did before, because the culture — the deep, unspoken assumptions about what really matters — has not changed at all.

Gerstner saw this clearly at IBM. "The last thing a company with a powerful culture needs is a vision statement," he famously said. "What it needs is a willingness to change its culture — and that is the hardest thing of all." He was right. Culture is not something you can change by announcement. It changes because a critical mass of people start behaving differently, and the new behaviors produce better results, and those results reinforce the new behaviors, gradually shifting the unwritten rules over time. It takes enormous patience, consistency, and pressure.

### The Status Quo Bias

Finally, there is a cognitive bias that operates at the individual level: the status quo bias. First formalized by economists William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser in 1988, this bias describes the tendency of human beings to prefer that things stay the same, even when objectively superior alternatives are available. In one of their experiments, they showed that people who were given a hypothetical inheritance of shares in a company were far more likely to keep those shares than to sell and reinvest the proceeds, even though the rational financial choice would be to diversify. The mere fact of already holding the shares made them feel more valuable than any alternative.

In organizations, the status quo bias manifests constantly. The company has always done performance reviews this way, so why change? The software has always been deployed on this schedule, so why switch? The strategy has always been based on this assumption, so why question it? Each of these questions has a surface-level logic — the existing system works, it is familiar, it has survived the test of time. But the underlying driver is not logic at all. It is the psychological weight of the status quo, which makes any departure from it feel riskier than staying put.

Now, here is the critical point. None of these forces — the amygdala response, the certainty premium, organizational inertia, culture, the status quo bias — is a sign that people are stupid, lazy, or stubborn. They are signs that people are human, and that organizations are human systems. A leader who tries to drive change by ignoring these forces will fail. A leader who tries to overcome them through sheer force of will will burn out. A leader who understands them — and designs a change approach that works with human nature rather than against it — has a chance.

## The Emotional Curve of Change

One of the most useful frameworks for understanding the human side of change comes not from business research but from grief counseling. In 1969, psychiatrist Elisabeth Kübler-Ross published *On Death and Dying*, in which she described the five stages of grief that people experience when confronting their own mortality: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance. What Kübler-Ross and subsequent researchers discovered is that this same emotional trajectory applies to any significant loss — including the loss of the familiar that change always entails.

When people are told that their organization is going to change in significant ways, they do not simply accept the news and move on. They grieve the loss of the old way of doing things. And they move through the Kübler-Ross stages, albeit in a slightly modified form that applies to organizational change.

### Stage 1: Denial

"No, this can't be happening. They've announced restructurings before and nothing ever changes. This will blow over."

Denial is the brain's way of protecting itself from information that is too threatening to process all at once. It is not a conscious choice. It is a psychological defense mechanism that buys time for the slower, more analytical parts of the brain to catch up.

In organizational change, denial often manifests as dismissal. People point out that previous change initiatives failed. They minimize the significance of the new direction. They go about their work as if nothing has happened, waiting for the storm to pass. Leaders interpret this as resistance, but it is usually not. It is the first stage of processing.

### Stage 2: Anger

"This is stupid. This will never work. The people running this company don't know what they're doing. I've been doing my job perfectly fine for fifteen years."

Anger surfaces when the reality of the change begins to penetrate denial. People start to understand that this is real, and they are frightened, and fear often comes out as anger. They are angry at the leaders who made the decision, angry at the circumstances that made it necessary, angry at themselves for not seeing it coming, angry that their comfortable world is being upended.

The mistake many leaders make at this stage is to get defensive. They try to explain why the change is necessary, to argue against the anger, to prove the angry employees wrong. That approach backfires, because anger is not an argument. It is an emotion. It needs to be acknowledged, not refuted. A leader who can say, "I understand that you're angry, and I would be too in your position," has done more to advance the change than a leader who hands out a spreadsheet showing why the anger is misplaced.

### Stage 3: Bargaining

"Maybe there's a compromise. What if we keep the old system but make some adjustments? What if I just keep doing what I'm doing but in the new department?"

Bargaining is the last attempt to hold onto the familiar. People try to negotiate their way out of the change, hoping that if they can just find the right middle ground, they won't have to fully let go of what they are losing.

The danger at this stage is that leaders, wanting to be reasonable and to reduce conflict, get drawn into compromises that undermine the change. They allow exceptions. They permit people to keep old processes alongside new ones. They create hybrid arrangements that satisfy no one and dilute the transformation. Bargaining must be met with clarity: the change is happening, and while there is room for input on implementation, there is no room for negotiation on direction.

### Stage 4: Depression

"What's the point? I've given this company my best years and now they're throwing it all away. Nothing I do matters."

Depression in the organizational change context is not clinical depression — it is situational sadness and demotivation. People realize that the change is real, that they cannot bargain their way out of it, and that the old normal is genuinely gone. They feel a sense of loss. Performance often dips at this stage. Engagement drops. Some people disengage entirely.

This is the most dangerous point in the emotional curve for change leaders, because it is also the point where they are most likely to lose faith themselves. When everyone is discouraged, it is tempting to conclude that the change was a mistake. But depression is a stage, not a destination. If leaders can hold steady, acknowledge the loss, and keep moving forward, the curve turns upward.

### Stage 5: Acceptance

"Okay. This is happening. I might as well figure out how to make it work."

Acceptance is not enthusiasm. It is not even agreement. It is simply the recognition that the old normal is not coming back, and that the only productive path forward is to engage with the new reality. Acceptance is a quiet stage. People stop arguing and start adapting.

The mistake leaders make at this stage is to assume that acceptance means full buy-in. It does not. It means people have stopped fighting the change. That is progress, but it is not the end of the journey. Acceptance opens the door to the next two stages, which are where real transformation happens.

### Stage 6: Exploration

"Well, what if we tried this? What if we took the new direction and made it even better? What if I found a new role in the new structure that I actually enjoy?"

Exploration is the first genuinely positive stage. People begin to experiment with the new reality, testing possibilities, looking for opportunities. Energy returns. Creativity increases. People who were angry two months ago are now proposing ideas.

This stage needs to be actively cultivated. Leaders should create space for experimentation, reward initiative, and celebrate the people who are finding new ways to contribute. Exploration is fragile — it can be crushed by criticism or excessive control.

### Stage 7: Commitment

"This is actually better than what we had before. I can't believe we did it the old way for so long."

Commitment is the final stage. People have not just accepted the change — they have embraced it. The new way has become the normal way. The old way looks strange and outdated. People are bought in, energized, and productive.

The journey from denial to commitment takes time. It cannot be skipped or rushed. Different people move through the stages at different speeds. A leader who understands the emotional curve does not expect everyone to be at the same place at the same time. They meet people where they are, not where they wish they were.

### What Leaders Get Wrong About the Emotional Curve

The most common mistake leaders make is treating change as a rational process. They assume that if they explain the logic clearly enough, people will get on board. They present the data. They show the analysis. They lay out the business case. And then they are frustrated when people resist.

Here is the truth that every leader must internalize: **people do not resist change. They resist the loss that change brings.**

Every change involves a loss of something — a familiar process, a comfortable routine, a position of influence, a sense of competence, a network of relationships, an identity. The rational benefits of the change may far outweigh those losses, but that does not make the losses any less real. And when leaders ignore the losses — when they say "this change is good for everyone, so there's no reason to be upset" — they are effectively telling people that their feelings do not matter. That creates resentment, which becomes resistance.

The leaders who are most effective at change are the ones who acknowledge the loss explicitly. They say: "I know this is hard. I know you built this system, and it was a good system for its time. I know you are proud of what you accomplished. And I am asking you to let some of that go, because the world has changed and we need to change with it." That kind of honesty does not make the change easier, but it makes it possible. It gives people permission to feel what they feel, which is the first step toward moving through those feelings and getting to the other side.

## Kotter's 8-Step Change Model

I have spent more than three decades studying why change efforts succeed and why they fail. My research has spanned hundreds of organizations across industries and countries, from manufacturing companies trying to implement quality programs to healthcare systems attempting to improve patient safety to technology companies navigating disruptive shifts in their markets.

What I found, consistently, is that successful change efforts pass through a series of phases. They require a reasonable amount of time. Skipping steps creates only the illusion of speed — and inevitably leads to failure. Critical mistakes in any phase can have a devastating impact on the momentum that is necessary for transformation.

The 8-step model that emerged from this research is not a theoretical exercise. It is a practical framework that has been used by thousands of organizations worldwide. Each step addresses a specific barrier to change that the research identified. Each step builds on the one before it. And each step is grounded in the fundamental insight that change is a human process before it is a strategic one.

Before we go into each step, a word about how to use this model. It is not a checklist. It is a sequence. You cannot build urgency in step 1 and then ignore it for the rest of the process — you must maintain it. You cannot form a vision in step 3 and assume everyone understands it — you must communicate it constantly. The steps are not a linear path you follow once and are done with. They are a set of interconnected activities that require ongoing attention and reinforcement throughout the entire change effort.

### Step 1: Create Urgency

The single biggest mistake in virtually every failed change effort is the same: the organization did not feel enough urgency. Not some urgency. Not a mild sense that change might be necessary. Enough urgency to overcome the enormous forces of inertia described earlier in this chapter.

Urgency is the emotional fuel that powers a change effort. Without it, people find a thousand reasons to delay, to debate, to study the issue further, to form a committee, to wait for more data. With genuine urgency, people find a way to move despite the obstacles.

The key word is "genuine." False urgency — manufactured crisis, dramatic announcements that are not backed by reality — does not work for long. People can sense when urgency is real and when it is performance. If you have to exaggerate the threat to create urgency, you have already lost credibility.

Creating genuine urgency requires showing people the reality of the situation — both the threats and the opportunities — in a way that they can feel, not just understand intellectually. Numbers alone rarely work. People need stories, examples, direct exposure to the problems or opportunities. The most effective urgency-building techniques include:

- Bringing in customers who have defected to competitors to speak directly to employees
- Sharing brutally honest assessments of the company's competitive position, including the worst-case scenarios
- Creating experiences that allow people to see the gap between current performance and what is possible
- Identifying and highlighting early warning signs that others are ignoring

Gerstner did this at IBM by being completely candid about the company's situation. He did not sugarcoat. He did not blame external factors. He told employees: "The company is losing money. The industry is changing. The strategies that made us successful are no longer working. If we do not change, we will die." That kind of directness was jarring to an IBM culture that prided itself on politeness and consensus. But it was exactly what the organization needed to hear.

At the same time, Gerstner created urgency through action. He made immediate, visible changes that signaled the old ways were over. He eliminated the company's long-standing practice of flying first-class and staying in luxury hotels — not because the cost mattered (it was a rounding error compared to the $8 billion loss), but because it sent a signal that the culture of entitlement was ending. He canceled the annual management meeting at a resort in Virginia. He cut the company's charitable contributions. Each action was a statement: the old IBM is dead, and the new one is not going to be comfortable for anyone.

The biggest mistake leaders make at this stage is underestimating how much urgency is needed. They assume that if they are feeling the urgency, everyone else must be feeling it too. They are almost always wrong. The CEO who lies awake at night worrying about the competition is surrounded by executives who are comfortable, middle managers who are going through the motions, and frontline employees who have seen five previous "urgent" initiatives come and go without consequence. You cannot create too much urgency. The more you create, the better your chances.

### Step 2: Build a Guiding Coalition

No single person, no matter how brilliant or charismatic, can lead a major change effort alone. The complexity is too great. The resistance is too entrenched. The political forces are too powerful. You need a coalition of people who share the urgency, believe in the direction, and have the power and credibility to make change happen.

A guiding coalition is not the same as the senior leadership team. Senior leaders are included by virtue of their positions, but the coalition must be broader. It should include:

- **Position power**: enough key players on board that the coalition cannot be blocked or ignored
- **Expertise**: people whose knowledge and judgment are respected, regardless of their formal titles
- **Credibility**: people who have the trust of the organization and can carry the message to different constituencies
- **Diversity**: people from different functions, levels, geographies, and perspectives, so the coalition represents the whole organization

The coalition does not have to be large. It needs to be effective. A group of 10 to 20 genuinely committed, well-positioned people is worth more than a hundred people who have been assigned to a task force because their boss told them to join.

The most important quality in a guiding coalition is trust. Change efforts create uncertainty, conflict, and political pressure. If the members of the coalition do not trust each other, they will fracture under that pressure. They will protect their own turf. They will form sub-coalitions that fight each other. The change will die in internal politics.

Building that trust takes time and intention. The coalition needs to meet regularly, discuss the issues openly, and develop a shared understanding of the problem and the solution. It needs to work through disagreements in private so it can present a united front in public. And it needs a leader who can hold the group together through the inevitable challenges.

Gerstner's coalition at IBM was unusual because he was an outsider. He did not have the existing relationships and loyalties that an insider would have. But he built his coalition deliberately, keeping some executives from the old regime while bringing in new people, and making it clear that the criteria for inclusion were performance and commitment to change, not seniority or politics. He created a group of people who shared his diagnosis and his urgency.

The coalition's first task is to develop the vision and strategy — which is step 3. But its ongoing task is to drive, support, and protect the change effort throughout the entire process. The coalition is the engine of transformation. If the engine is not strong enough, the car will not move.

### Step 3: Form a Strategic Vision

Urgency without direction is panic. A coalition without a shared vision is a group of people meeting regularly with no clear purpose. Vision is what transforms energy into action.

A good change vision serves several purposes. It clarifies the direction. It simplifies the complex reality of the transformation into a message that people can understand and remember. It motivates action by creating a picture of a future that is genuinely better than the present. And it coordinates the efforts of thousands of people, each of whom needs to make hundreds of decisions, by giving them a framework for choosing what to do.

A vision is not a strategy document. It is not a five-year plan. It is not a set of financial targets. It is a concise, compelling picture of what the organization will look like after the change is successful. The best change visions can be communicated in five minutes and understood in one.

The characteristics of an effective change vision:

- **Imaginable**: it conveys a clear picture of what the future will look like
- **Desirable**: it appeals to the long-term interests of stakeholders — employees, customers, shareholders
- **Feasible**: it consists of realistic, achievable goals
- **Focused**: it is clear enough to guide decision-making
- **Flexible**: it allows for individual initiative and changing circumstances
- **Communicable**: it can be explained quickly and easily

Gerstner's vision for IBM was deceptively simple: "One IBM." The company was fragmented into dozens of independent fiefdoms, each protecting its own products and territory. Gerstner's vision was to bring them together as a single company that could serve customers with integrated solutions. It was not a complicated vision. It was not a visionary vision in the sense of imagining something entirely new. It was a vision of integration, of putting the pieces back together, of leveraging IBM's greatest strategic asset — its ability to serve large enterprises with end-to-end solutions — that the fragmentation had destroyed.

The vision was compelling because it addressed the real problem. Customers were frustrated with IBM's incoherence — having to deal with different divisions that could not or would not coordinate with each other. The vision of "One IBM" promised a better experience for customers and a more effective company for employees. It was also feasible — IBM had the pieces; it just needed to put them together.

The vision guided everything that followed. When the company invested in Global Services, that was "One IBM." When it standardized systems and processes across divisions, that was "One IBM." When it changed compensation to reward collaboration, that was "One IBM." A good vision is not a slogan. It is an organizing principle that makes thousands of individual decisions consistent with each other.

### Step 4: Enlist Volunteers

A vision, no matter how brilliant, does nothing sitting in a PowerPoint deck. It must be communicated — relentlessly, consistently, through every channel available — until it becomes part of the organization's shared understanding.

The research on successful change efforts is unequivocal on this point: leaders dramatically underestimate how much communication is needed. They think they have communicated the vision because they mentioned it in a town hall, wrote an email, and included it in the quarterly report. They have communicated it once or twice. The organization needs to hear it ten, twenty, fifty times before it actually sinks in.

There are several reasons for this. People are busy with their day jobs and not paying close attention to leadership communications. The vision is competing with a hundred other messages. Each person interprets the vision through their own lens and may not understand what it means for their work. And the organizational grapevine — the informal network of gossip, rumor, and speculation — is always running, frequently undermining the official message.

Effective change leaders use every communication channel available: town halls, emails, newsletters, videos, one-on-one conversations, small group meetings, informal gatherings. They embed the vision in every presentation, every decision, every public statement. They repeat the vision in simple, memorable language until they are tired of hearing it themselves — and then they keep going.

But communication is not just about volume. It is also about walking the talk. If a leader says the vision is about collaboration but rewards individual performance, the message is contradicted. If a leader says the change is urgent but continues to approve business-as-usual projects, the message is undermined. Every action a leader takes is a communication. The most powerful form of communication is consistent, visible behavior that aligns with the vision.

Gerstner understood this deeply. He did not just talk about "One IBM." He demonstrated it. When he made decisions that favored the corporate interest over a particular division's interest, everyone saw it. When he broke up the company's powerful geographical fiefdoms and reorganized around global business units, that was a demonstration of the vision. When he publicly chastised executives who protected their own turf at the expense of the whole, that was a communication. His actions were the message.

The goal of all this communication is not just understanding. It is buy-in — and beyond buy-in, enlistment. You want people to not just accept the vision but to actively help make it happen. You want them to volunteer for the change, to bring their own ideas and energy to the transformation, to recruit others. This only happens when the vision resonates emotionally, when people believe the leader is genuine, and when they see that others are getting on board.

### Step 5: Enable Action

Communication creates willingness. But willingness is not enough. People also need the ability to act on the vision. If the systems, structures, processes, and incentives of the organization block the new behaviors, the change will stall regardless of how committed people are.

Enabling action means removing barriers. The most common barriers include:

- **Structural barriers**: the organizational chart that puts people in silos when the vision requires cross-functional collaboration
- **Process barriers**: approval processes that were designed for the old way and prevent the new way
- **Skill barriers**: people who lack the capabilities needed to work in the new environment
- **System barriers**: information systems, performance management systems, compensation systems that reward old behaviors and punish new ones
- **Supervisory barriers**: managers who actively or passively resist the change, either because they do not believe in it or because it threatens their power

The most important of these — and the most frequently ignored — is the supervisory barrier. Middle managers can kill any change effort. They are the ones who interpret the vision for their teams, who decide which initiatives to prioritize, who control resources and assignments. If middle managers are not on board, or if they are actively undermined by the change, the vision will never reach the front line.

Removing barriers requires the guiding coalition to exercise its power. The coalition must be willing to confront people who block progress, change systems that are misaligned, and shift resources to support the new direction. This is uncomfortable. It creates conflict. It is absolutely necessary.

Gerstner's approach to barrier removal was surgical and ruthless. He identified the "fiefdoms" — the powerful country managers who controlled IBM's regional operations — and broke their power. He changed the compensation system to reward global teamwork rather than local profit. He invested heavily in IBM Global Services, creating a new organization that cut across the old divisions. He personally intervened to remove executives who could not or would not make the transition. Enabling action was not a gentle process. It was a war.

### Step 6: Generate Short-Term Wins

Change is hard. It takes time. It asks people to make sacrifices now for benefits that are somewhere in the future. That is a difficult proposition to sustain — especially when the early stages of change are full of disruption and uncertainty and there is not yet evidence that the new direction is working.

This is why short-term wins are essential. People need to see progress. They need evidence that the change is producing results. They need something to celebrate.

A short-term win is not just any positive outcome. It is a visible, unambiguous, and clearly relevant result that happened because of the change effort. It is something that people can point to and say, "This is working."

The characteristics of an effective short-term win:

- **Visible**: people throughout the organization can see it, not just the team involved
- **Unambiguous**: there is no debate about whether it is a positive result
- **Clearly connected to the change**: people can see the causal link between the new behaviors and the outcome
- **Achievable quickly**: it can be delivered within months, not years

Short-term wins serve multiple purposes. They provide evidence that the change is worth the effort. They reward and energize the people who have been working hard on the transformation. They undermine the skeptics and cynics who predicted failure. They build momentum for the next phase of the effort. And they give the guiding coalition the credibility to push for more aggressive changes.

The danger at this stage is declaring victory too early. Short-term wins are not the end of the change. They are fuel for the journey. Leaders who relax after a few good quarters, who start celebrating before the new behaviors are truly embedded, who declare success and move on to other priorities — these leaders see their change efforts die. The short-term win is a tool, not a destination.

Gerstner generated short-term wins at IBM by focusing on what he called "market-driven initiatives" — specific, targeted efforts to improve customer satisfaction and win new business. Each win was visible. Each win was clearly connected to the new approach. Each win built confidence that the transformation was working. And each win gave Gerstner the credibility to push for deeper, more difficult changes.

### Step 7: Sustain Acceleration

The most dangerous moment in a change effort is the moment just after the first big success. The organization is tired. The natural temptation is to coast. The skeptics are quiet for now. The celebration feels earned.

This is precisely the moment when leaders must push harder. Change is not a single event. It is a cascade of events that build on each other. The first wave of changes creates the conditions for the second wave. The second wave creates the conditions for the third. Each success makes the next success more possible — but only if the organization continues to press forward.

Sustaining acceleration requires:

- **Using the credibility from early wins to tackle bigger, harder changes**: the systems, structures, and cultures that were too entrenched to address early in the process can now be confronted
- **Bringing in new people to drive the change**: the original coalition may need to expand, bringing fresh energy and perspectives
- **Keeping urgency high**: the early success should not reduce urgency — it should increase it, by demonstrating what is possible
- **Removing additional barriers**: each new wave of change will reveal new barriers that need to be addressed
- **Avoiding premature celebration**: the change is not done until it is embedded in the culture

Gerstner's IBM transformation followed this pattern. The early wins were about stopping the bleeding — cutting costs, stabilizing the business, restoring customer confidence. The next wave was about restructuring — eliminating the geographic fiefdoms, creating the global business units, building Global Services. The next wave was about culture — changing the norms, the behaviors, the values that had made IBM resistant to change. Each wave built on the previous one. Each wave was harder than the one before. And Gerstner never let up.

### Step 8: Institute Change

The final step is the one most change efforts skip, and it is the one that determines whether the change will last.

Anchoring change in the culture means making the new behaviors the default. It means transforming the change from something that has to be actively managed into something that is simply how the organization operates. This happens when the new behaviors produce better results — and people see the connection — and when the new behaviors are reinforced by every element of the organizational system.

The most important tool for institutionalizing change is the compensation and promotion system. People pay attention to who gets rewarded and who gets ahead. If the people who embrace the new behaviors are the ones who get promoted, the message is clear. If people who cling to the old ways continue to advance, the message is equally clear — and it will kill the change.

Other tools for institutionalization:

- **Storytelling**: sharing stories of how the new approach produced positive outcomes, until those stories become part of the organizational narrative
- **Succession planning**: ensuring that new leaders come from the change effort, carrying the values and behaviors forward
- **Performance management**: updating all performance criteria to reflect the new behaviors
- **Onboarding**: introducing new employees to the culture through training and orientation that reflects the new way
- **Symbolic actions**: continuing to use rituals, ceremonies, and symbols that reinforce the new culture

The most important measure of institutionalization is simple: what happens when the leader who drove the change leaves? If the change survives the leader's departure, it is anchored. If it reverts, it was not. Gerstner ensured his changes survived by creating a succession process that brought in Sam Palmisano, who had been a key member of the transformation team and who continued and deepened the changes. He also made sure that the new culture was embedded in IBM's performance management, its leadership development, and its decision-making processes.

When Gerstner left IBM in 2002, the company was a different place than the one he had inherited in 1993. It was not just profitable — it was culturally transformed. The arrogance was gone. The internal focus was replaced by customer focus. The fiefdoms were gone, replaced by collaboration. And the company continued to thrive long after its change leader departed. That is the ultimate sign that change has been institutionalized.

## The Fire Below the Platform

Throughout this chapter, we have discussed urgency. But there is a particular flavor of urgency that deserves its own treatment because it is simultaneously the most powerful change motivator and the most dangerous to manufacture. It is called the burning platform.

The term comes from a story — possibly apocryphal, but powerful — about a worker on an oil platform in the North Sea. In 1988, the Piper Alpha platform exploded. One worker, interviewed later, said that when he realized the platform was on fire and about to collapse, he faced a choice: stay on the platform and die, or jump 150 feet into the freezing, oil-covered water. He jumped. When asked why he made that choice, he said: "Better probable death than certain death."

The burning platform is the moment when the status quo becomes so clearly and immediately untenable that the fear of changing is outweighed by the fear of not changing. It is the event that shatters the certainty premium. It makes people willing to let go of the familiar because the familiar has become unbearable.

Great change leaders know how to use the burning platform — or, more precisely, they know how to reveal it. The platform is often already burning. The organization is losing market share, or losing money, or losing talent, or losing relevance. The problem is that people do not see the flames. They are in denial. The leader's job is to make the fire visible.

But the burning platform is a dangerous tool if used dishonestly. If you manufacture a crisis that does not exist, people will eventually realize they have been manipulated. You lose credibility. And the next time you warn of danger, no one will believe you. The "cry wolf" syndrome is a real risk. If your organization is not actually facing an existential threat, do not pretend it is. Use other urgency-building techniques — focus on opportunities, competitive pressures, customer needs — but do not fabricate a platform that is not burning.

The real question, then, is not whether you have a burning platform. It is whether people see it. Most failed change efforts are not failing because the platform is not burning. They are failing because the leader is the only one who can feel the heat. The people who need to move are standing on the platform, feeling perfectly comfortable, because they do not know what the leader knows, or they do not believe it, or they have been told too many times before that the platform was burning and it turned out to be false alarm.

Show them the fire. Do not tell them about it — show them. Bring in the customers who left. Share the financial data that scares you. Walk them through the competitive analysis. Let them feel what you feel. Until they feel it, nothing else you do will matter.

## The Role of Middle Managers

There is a persistent myth in change management that middle managers are the enemy of change. They are portrayed as the bureaucratic layer that resists every initiative, protects their turf, and passively-aggressively blocks transformation. I have found this to be not just wrong but dangerously wrong.

Middle managers are not obstacles to change. They are the amplifiers of change — or, if they are excluded, the amplifiers of resistance.

Think about what middle managers actually do. They are the bridge between the strategic direction set by senior leadership and the operational reality of the front line. They translate the vision into specific actions. They allocate resources. They coach and develop their teams. They interpret the daily flow of information and decide what gets communicated up and what gets communicated down. In a change effort, they are the people who have to make the new direction work in practice — navigating the conflicts between the old demands and the new ones, figuring out how to implement the vision in their specific context, keeping their teams motivated through uncertainty.

The problem is that many change initiatives bypass middle management entirely. Senior leaders announce the change directly to the front line, expecting buy-in and action. Middle managers are informed after the fact, or not informed at all. They are told to implement a change they had no role in shaping, with goals they did not help define, using resources they did not help allocate, in timelines they did not help set.

The result is not resistance. It is disempowerment. Middle managers who feel excluded from the change process lose ownership. They become passive observers, waiting to see whether the initiative will succeed or fail before committing. Some will actively resist, not because they are opposed to change, but because they are opposed to being treated as irrelevant.

The solution is straightforward: include middle managers in the guiding coalition. Not as tokens, but as genuine participants whose perspectives are valued and whose contributions matter. Give them a role in shaping how the change gets implemented in their areas. Empower them to make decisions within the framework of the vision. Recognize that they are caught between the pressure from above and the reality from below, and support them in navigating that tension.

When middle managers are included, they become the most powerful force for change in the organization. They understand the operational reality in ways that senior leaders do not. They have relationships of trust with their teams. They can translate the abstract vision into concrete actions that make sense for their specific context. They can identify barriers that executives would never see. They can generate ideas for implementation that make the change more effective and less disruptive.

When middle managers are excluded, they become the most powerful force against change. Not because they are malicious. Because they are human.

## Change Fatigue

There is another force that kills change efforts — not resistance, but exhaustion. Change fatigue.

Consider the typical large organization. It has a digital transformation initiative. A culture transformation. A restructuring. A new performance management system. A new ERP implementation. A diversity and inclusion initiative. A sustainability program. A cost reduction drive. A innovation push. Each of these is, on its own, a reasonable priority. But collectively, they add up to a level of change that exceeds what human beings can absorb.

The human capacity for change is not infinite. It is a finite resource, like attention or energy or willpower. When an organization launches a dozen major change initiatives simultaneously, it does not get twelve times the results. It gets fragmentation, confusion, and burnout. People cannot prioritize twelve things at once, so they prioritize nothing. They retreat into the familiar. They conserve their energy for the change that will actually affect them — the one they cannot ignore — and disengage from the rest.

The research on change saturation is consistent: there is a clear negative correlation between the number of concurrent change initiatives and the success rate of any individual one. Each additional initiative reduces the probability of success for all of them.

The solution is not to avoid change. The solution is to manage the change portfolio with the same discipline that a financial manager brings to a financial portfolio. Not all changes are equal. Some are critical. Some are important but not critical. Some are nice to have. Some can wait. The job of the change leader is to assess the full portfolio, prioritize ruthlessly, sequence carefully, and communicate the rationale for those choices.

This means saying no. It means telling other leaders that their initiative, however worthy, will have to wait. It means absorbing the political consequences of disappointing powerful stakeholders. It is not easy. It is essential.

It also means recognizing that different parts of the organization may have different saturation points. A sales team that is already handling a product launch, a CRM implementation, and a territory reorganization cannot absorb another change. A manufacturing team that has just implemented a new quality system needs time to stabilize before the next initiative. A leader who understands change saturation adjusts the pace and scope of change to match the capacity of each part of the organization.

The most important insight about change fatigue is this: the damage is often invisible. People do not tell their leaders that they are overloaded with change. They stop complaining, stop suggesting, stop engaging. They quietly do the minimum. The leader looks at the metrics and sees that the change is not taking hold, and assumes the problem is resistance or capability or communication. The real problem is exhaustion.

## The Aftermath

There is one more stage of change that is almost universally overlooked. It comes after the new systems are in place, after the new behaviors are established, after the vision has been realized. It is the aftermath.

Every change involves loss. Something ends. Something that people built, invested in, identified with, and took pride in is being left behind. The emotional curve of change acknowledges this, but even when people reach acceptance and commitment, there is still residual grief that needs to be processed. If it is not processed, it lingers, creating cynicism and disconnection that will infect the next change effort.

The aftermath requires three things from leaders:

First, **acknowledge what was lost.** Do not pretend that the old way was bad in every respect. It was not. It was a reasonable response to the circumstances of its time. The people who built it were not stupid or wrong. They were doing their best with what they knew. Acknowledging this — genuinely, not as a rhetorical gesture — honors the contribution of everyone who made the old system work. It makes it easier for them to let go.

Second, **celebrate what was achieved.** The change effort demanded enormous effort from people. They worked hard, took risks, made sacrifices. They deserve recognition. Celebrating the achievement is not just about making people feel good — it is about reinforcing the connection between the change and positive outcomes, which helps anchor the new behaviors in the culture.

Third, **stabilize the new normal.** The period immediately after a major change is fragile. People are still adjusting. The new processes are not yet smooth. Old habits reassert themselves. Leaders need to provide reassurance, support, and patience during this period. They need to resist the temptation to launch the next change before the current one has truly settled.

The aftermath also includes an often-forgotten step: grieving. Change leaders rarely acknowledge that people need to grieve. But they do. The old way of doing things, the old relationships, the old identity — these losses are real. Creating space for grief, whether through ritual, conversation, or simply time, allows people to process the loss and move forward.

## Case Study 1: IBM's Reinvention Under Lou Gerstner

### The Diagnosis

When Gerstner walked into IBM's headquarters in Armonk, New York, on April 1, 1993, he knew the company was in trouble. What he discovered in his first weeks was worse than he expected — not because the financial situation was more dire than reported, but because the cultural and organizational problems were deeper than anyone had understood.

His first insight came when he asked for a summary of IBM's strategy. The document he received was a 40-page compendium of initiatives, programs, and aspirations. It listed dozens of priorities but offered no coherent direction. The company was simultaneously trying to defend its mainframe business, grow its PC business, expand into software, develop its services capabilities, compete in every geography with every product, and satisfy every customer with every solution. It was not a strategy. It was an encyclopedia of wishful thinking.

The deeper problem, Gerstner realized, was not strategic in the conventional sense. IBM's strategy was not failing because of bad analysis. Its strategy was failing because the company's culture made coherent strategy impossible. The culture was built around a set of assumptions that had become fatal:

- **Arrogance**: IBM had dominated computing for so long that it genuinely believed it was smarter than its customers and competitors. The company knew the "IBM way" was the right way, and anyone who disagreed was wrong. This arrogance made it impossible for IBM to learn from the market.

- **Internal focus**: Decisions were made based on internal politics rather than customer needs. Executives spent more time maneuvering for position within the company than understanding what customers actually wanted. The fiefdoms — the country managers, the product divisions, the functional groups — were so powerful that the company could not coordinate its own actions.

- **Bureaucracy**: Every decision required endless approvals. Every initiative went through layers of committees. The company moved with the speed and agility of a glacier. Gerstner later estimated that IBM had approximately 130 separate committees, many of which existed only to review the work of other committees.

- **Entitlement**: IBM employees had lived so long at the top of the business world that they had come to expect success as their birthright. The culture was comfortable, polite, and resistant to discomfort. No one was ever fired for failing. No one was ever held accountable for results.

Gerstner's diagnosis was clear: IBM's problem was not its products, its technology, or its market position. Its problem was its culture. The strategy that had made IBM dominant in the 1960s and 1970s — own the entire stack, control the customer, protect the mainframe — was no longer viable. But the culture that had grown up around that strategy was still in place, preventing the company from adapting to the new reality.

### The Urgency

Gerstner understood that the first task was not strategy. It was survival. The company was hemorrhaging cash. The board was openly discussing a breakup. Customers were defecting. The industry was moving on.

He used the financial crisis as the foundation for urgency. In his first meeting with the top 100 executives, he delivered a message that the polite, consensus-driven IBM culture had never heard: "The company is losing $8 billion a year. We are going to have to make painful changes. Some of you will not survive those changes. But if we do not change, none of us will survive."

He did not stop at words. He made immediate, symbolic changes that signaled the old culture was dead. He eliminated first-class travel and luxury hotels for executives. He canceled the annual management retreat. He sold the corporate fleet of planes. He moved from the executive dining room to the company cafeteria. Each action was small in financial terms. Each action was enormous in symbolic terms. The message was clear: the era of entitlement is over.

At the same time, Gerstner used data to make the urgency concrete. He brought in customer surveys showing that IBM's service ratings had fallen below its competitors. He shared detailed competitive analyses showing that IBM was losing in every major market. He made sure that every executive saw the numbers that kept him awake at night.

### The Vision

Gerstner's vision was "One IBM." It sounds simple — almost too simple. But in the context of a company that had fragmented into warring fiefdoms, it was radical.

The strategic logic behind "One IBM" was grounded in what was actually happening in the market. The computing industry was moving from a world of proprietary systems, where each vendor owned its own technology stack, to a world of open systems, where different vendors' products had to work together. In this new world, customers faced a problem: they had to integrate dozens of different technologies from dozens of different vendors. IBM's great strategic opportunity was not to be the best at any single piece of technology — it was to be the one company that could make all the pieces work together.

This required IBM to stop thinking of itself as a collection of independent businesses and start thinking of itself as an integrated solutions provider. It meant abandoning the product-centric view that had dominated the company for decades and adopting a customer-centric view. It meant the mainframe division had to work with the PC division, which had to work with the software division, which had to work with the services division — whether they wanted to or not.

The "One IBM" vision also provided a clear criterion for decision-making. When faced with a choice, Gerstner asked: does this bring us together or tear us apart? Does it serve the whole company or just one part? Does it serve the customer or serve our internal politics?

### The Coalition

Gerstner built his guiding coalition in stages. In the first stage, he kept many of the senior executives who had been at IBM when he arrived. He needed their knowledge of the company, their relationships in the industry, and the stability they provided. But he made it clear that keeping their jobs depended on their commitment to the change, and he replaced those who could not adapt.

In the second stage, as the transformation progressed, he brought in outsiders and promoted insiders who embodied the new direction. Sam Palmisano, who would eventually succeed Gerstner as CEO, was a key member of the coalition — a long-time IBM executive who had run the services business and who shared Gerstner's vision of an integrated IBM.

The coalition was not just the senior leadership team. Gerstner created task forces and working groups across the company, bringing together people from different functions, geographies, and levels. These groups were given real authority to make decisions, not just make recommendations. They became a network of change agents distributed throughout the organization.

### Removing Barriers

Gerstner's most significant barrier removal was structural. He broke down the powerful geographic fiefdoms that had made IBM impossible to manage. Historically, IBM's country managers had run their territories as independent kingdoms, with their own P&Ls, their own strategies, and their own cultures. Gerstner flattened this structure, creating global business units that reported directly to corporate headquarters.

He also restructured the compensation system. Previously, IBM's compensation was based on local performance — the country managers got bonuses based on their country's results, which incentivized them to maximize local profit at the expense of the global business. Gerstner shifted compensation to reflect global performance and teamwork. Executives were rewarded based on how well the whole company did, not just their piece of it.

He removed the most resistant executives. Gerstner personally managed the process of replacing senior leaders who could not or would not adapt to the new direction. He was not cruel about it — but he was decisive. He understood that keeping resistant leaders in place sent a signal that the change was optional, and he could not afford that signal.

His most important barrier removal, however, was the creation of IBM Global Services. Before Gerstner, IBM's services business was a fragmented collection of small groups scattered across different product divisions. Gerstner pulled them together into a single organization with its own leadership, its own strategy, and its own profit-and-loss responsibility. Global Services would become the engine of IBM's transformation, growing from $7 billion in revenue in 1993 to more than $50 billion by the end of the decade.

### The Short-Term Wins

The first short-term win was stopping the bleeding. In 1994, IBM posted its first quarterly profit since Gerstner had joined. The stock price, which had fallen below $40, began to recover. The message to investors was clear: the company is not going to die.

More importantly, Gerstner generated wins that mattered to customers. He launched a series of initiatives to improve IBM's responsiveness, including a program to resolve customer complaints within 24 hours. He personally visited dozens of major customers, listening to their frustration and committing to do better. Customer satisfaction scores began to improve.

The most visible win was the decision to keep the company together. In 1995, after months of analysis and debate, Gerstner announced that IBM would not be broken up. The decision was met with skepticism from Wall Street, which had been convinced that divestiture was the only path. But as the integrated company began to demonstrate its value — winning large, complex contracts that required hardware, software, and services working together — the skeptics began to come around.

### Institutionalizing the Change

Gerstner's changes were not just strategic and structural. They were cultural. He understood that the deepest change was in the norms and behaviors that had defined IBM for generations.

He changed the performance management system to hold people accountable for results. He introduced stock options for all employees, aligning everyone's incentives with the company's long-term success. He created a new set of values — "dedication to every client's success," "innovation that matters," "trust and personal responsibility" — and embedded them in every aspect of how the company operated.

Most importantly, Gerstner ensured that the change would survive him. He chose Sam Palmisano as his successor — an insider who had been instrumental in the transformation and who carried the new culture forward. He made sure that the new behaviors were embedded in IBM's leadership development programs, so that every generation of IBM leaders would be formed by the new culture rather than the old one.

When Gerstner left in 2002, IBM was a fundamentally different company. It was not just profitable — it was culturally transformed. The arrogance was gone. The bureaucracy was reduced. The internal focus had shifted to customer focus. And the company continued to evolve, adapting to new challenges and opportunities long after its change leader departed.

### The Lessons

IBM's reinvention teaches us several things about leading change. First, culture is not a soft topic — it is the hardest strategic issue a company can face. Second, change must be led from the top, but it must be implemented through a broad coalition. Third, symbolism matters enormously — small actions that contradict the old culture can be more powerful than grand strategy documents. Fourth, compensation and promotion are the most powerful tools for institutionalizing change. And fifth, the leader's most important job may be to save the company from its own success — from the arrogance, the inertia, and the complacency that success breeds.

## Case Study 2: Nokia's Failure to Change

If IBM's story is about how to lead change successfully, Nokia's story is a cautionary tale — a case study in what happens when a company sees the future coming and still fails to respond.

### The Empire

In 2007, Nokia was the undisputed king of mobile phones. The Finnish company held approximately 40 percent of the global mobile phone market — more than its next three competitors combined. It sold phones in every price category, in every country, in every form factor. Its brand was one of the most valuable in the world. Its supply chain was legendary — Nokia could produce and distribute more phones more efficiently than any company in history. Its R&D budget was enormous, employing thousands of engineers who produced a constant stream of innovation.

Nokia's dominance was not accidental. The company had transformed itself multiple times over its 140-year history, moving from paper to rubber boots to electronics to telecommunications. It had bet heavily on mobile phones in the 1990s and won spectacularly. The company was culturally Finnish — humble, pragmatic, consensus-driven. It was also phenomenally profitable.

Then, on January 9, 2007, Steve Jobs walked onto a stage in San Francisco and introduced the iPhone.

### The Denial

Here is what most people do not know about Nokia and the iPhone. Nokia's engineers analyzed the iPhone in detail, almost immediately after its launch. Their findings were thorough, technically rigorous, and — in many respects — correct.

The iPhone, Nokia concluded, was technically inferior. Its battery life was terrible — Nokia's phones could run for days on a charge; the iPhone barely lasted a day. Its network reliability was poor — calls dropped frequently on the early iPhone networks. Its screen was fragile — glass, in a world where the expectation was for phones to be dropped without breaking. Its radio performance, its antenna design, its heat management — in each of these technical dimensions, Nokia's existing phones outperformed the iPhone.

Nokia's engineers were right about every one of these technical assessments. And they were completely, catastrophically wrong about what mattered.

The iPhone was not competing on the dimensions that Nokia had optimized. It was competing on something entirely different. It was competing on the user experience. On the software platform. On the ecosystem of applications that developers could build. On the transformation of the phone from a communications device into a general-purpose computing platform. The iPhone was not a better phone. It was fundamentally a different category of product — a smartphone that happened to make calls, rather than a phone that happened to have some smart features.

Nokia evaluated the iPhone through the lens of its existing paradigm and found it wanting. The company's engineers and executives genuinely believed that Nokia's superior hardware engineering would protect their position. They could not see that the rules of the game had changed because they were so deeply invested in the old game.

This cognitive trap — evaluating a new entrant by the standards of the old paradigm — is one of the most common failure modes in organizational change. It is exactly what Kodak did with digital photography. It is what Blockbuster did with Netflix. It is what every incumbent does when a disruptive innovation emerges: they look at it and say, "It's not as good as what we do," without recognizing that "good" has been redefined.

### The Organizational Psychology

Nokia's failure to respond to the iPhone was not a failure of information. It was a failure of organizational psychology. Three forces combined to prevent Nokia from acting on what its engineers had discovered.

**Fear of cannibalization.** Nokia's Symbian operating system was the most successful mobile platform in the world, powering hundreds of millions of phones and generating enormous profits. A new platform strategy — one that embraced touchscreens, developer ecosystems, and the iPhone paradigm — would require abandoning or at minimum deprioritizing Symbian. The Symbian team was powerful within Nokia. The executives who had built their careers on Symbian were not going to surrender easily. And there was a genuine strategic question: if we invest in the new platform, how do we keep the Symbian business running in the meantime? The answer that Nokia's leadership arrived at, implicitly, was: we don't — we protect Symbian and incrementally improve it.

**Internal politics.** Nokia's organizational structure was a battlefield. The company had two operating systems in development — Symbian and MeeGo — and the teams responsible for each were locked in a bitter civil war. Instead of unifying behind a single strategic direction, Nokia allowed the conflict to persist, with resources divided, priorities contested, and decisions delayed. The political battle between Symbian and MeeGo consumed energy that should have gone toward competing with the iPhone and the emerging Android ecosystem.

**Cultural arrogance.** Finnish culture values modesty and consensus. This served Nokia well during its rise — the company was disciplined, collaborative, and focused. But it became a liability during the crisis. Nokia's leaders were too polite to confront each other directly about the strategic disagreement. They deferred difficult decisions. They sought consensus where none was possible. The culture that had built the company prevented it from saving itself.

There was also a subtler cultural factor at work: Nokia's success had created a deep sense of confidence that bordered on complacency. The company had beaten every competitor it had ever faced. It had navigated technology transitions before. Its engineers were the smartest in the industry. There was a quiet, deeply held assumption that Nokia would figure this out — that the iPhone was a temporary challenge, not a permanent disruption.

### The Slow Decline

After the iPhone launch, Nokia had approximately three years before the disruption became irreversible. It wasted most of them.

The company did respond. It launched touchscreen phones. It invested in its app store. It developed new user interfaces. But each response was constrained by the same implicit strategic logic: protect Symbian, don't cannibalize the existing business, don't alienate the carrier partners who were Nokia's primary customers. The responses were half-measures — designed to show that Nokia was adapting without actually embracing the new paradigm.

In 2008, Google launched Android and made it available to any manufacturer. This should have been Nokia's opportunity — or its warning. Android was the platform that could have allowed Nokia to compete with the iPhone on software without rebuilding everything from scratch. But Nokia's leadership rejected Android, viewing it as giving up control of the operating system to Google. The decision was rational within Nokia's existing paradigm — the company had always controlled its own stack — but catastrophic in the new paradigm.

By 2010, the situation was clear. The iPhone had redefined the smartphone market. Android was growing explosively. Nokia's Symbian platform was dying. The company's market share was collapsing, and its financial performance was deteriorating. The board brought in Stephen Elop, a former Microsoft executive, as CEO.

Elop's most famous act was the "burning platform" memo, sent to all Nokia employees in February 2011. In it, he described Nokia's situation in terms that were brutally honest:

> "We are standing on a burning platform. There is a raging fire around us. We have more than one explosion — we have multiple points of scorching heat, fueling a fire that is consuming us. We have a burning platform, and we must decide how we are going to change."

The memo was shocking for its candor. It acknowledged that Nokia's strategy was failing, that the company had fallen behind, that dramatic action was necessary. Elop then announced the decision: Nokia would abandon Symbian and MeeGo and adopt Microsoft's Windows Phone as its primary operating system.

The decision was controversial. Many observers argued that Nokia should have embraced Android, which had the largest developer ecosystem and the fastest-growing market share. Windows Phone was a distant third, with a fraction of the apps and developer support. Elop's decision was seen by many as a bet on a losing horse — a decision driven by Elop's Microsoft ties rather than Nokia's strategic interest.

Regardless of the assessment, the decision came too late. By the time Nokia's Windows Phone devices reached the market, the smartphone landscape had been settled. Apple and Samsung dominated the premium and volume segments respectively. Android had become the default platform for the rest of the market. Nokia's Windows Phone devices were well-reviewed but never gained significant traction.

In 2013, Nokia sold its mobile phone business to Microsoft for $7.2 billion. The deal was a fraction of what the business had been worth at its peak in 2007. Within a few years, Microsoft wrote off most of the acquisition, acknowledging that the business was effectively worthless.

### The Lessons

Nokia's failure is one of the most instructive in business history because it is not a story of incompetence or ignorance. Nokia had smart people, deep resources, and clear information about the threat. It failed because of the psychology of incumbency.

The first lesson: seeing the future is not enough. Nokia's engineers analyzed the iPhone thoroughly and correctly identified its technical weaknesses. But they failed to understand that the iPhone was competing on different dimensions — and that those dimensions were more important than the ones Nokia had optimized. Technical excellence in the old paradigm is useless if the paradigm has shifted.

The second lesson: success creates inertia. Nokia's dominance in the feature phone era was so complete that the company could not conceive of a world where its advantages did not matter. The systems, culture, and incentives that had made Nokia successful were the same ones that prevented it from adapting.

The third lesson: internal divisions can be fatal. The civil war between Symbian and MeeGo consumed energy and attention that should have been directed at the external threat. Nokia's leadership could not or would not resolve the conflict, so the conflict resolved itself — in the worst possible way.

The fourth lesson: the timing of change decisions matters enormously. Nokia had a window of opportunity between 2007 and 2010 to make a decisive move. It failed to act decisively until 2011, by which point the window had closed. The cost of delay was not just lost revenue — it was the company's independence.

The most painful lesson, perhaps, is this: the best time to change is when everything is going well. Nokia was at its peak in 2007 — dominant, profitable, respected. That was the moment when it could have made a strategic pivot from a position of strength. But companies almost never change when things are going well. They change when they are in crisis. And by the time the crisis is visible to everyone, it is often too late.

## Case Study 3: Lego's Near-Death and Rebirth

The third case study is different from the first two. IBM succeeded in transforming itself. Nokia failed. Lego's story includes both elements — a near-death experience followed by one of the most remarkable turnarounds in modern business.

### The Empire of the Brick

For most of its history, Lego was not a company that needed to worry about strategy or change. It had invented an almost perfect product: a simple, durable, endlessly creative building toy that children loved and parents trusted. The Lego brick, introduced in 1958, was a masterpiece of design — its clutch power, its precision, its compatibility across generations. Every Lego brick made in the last sixty years still fits every other Lego brick. The product line required minimal innovation; the basic brick was the product, and the sets were just configurations.

For decades, Lego grew steadily and profitably. The company was privately held by the Kirk Kristiansen family, who had founded it in 1932. It was universally admired. And it was, by the late 1990s, in deep, deep trouble.

### The Near-Death

By 2003, Lego was on the verge of bankruptcy. The company was $800 million in debt. It was losing approximately $1 million per day. Sales had been declining for years. The company that had been one of the most beloved brands in the world was running out of time.

The conventional narrative about Lego's near-death is that video games killed the toy business. That is true but incomplete. The deeper problem was that Lego had abandoned its core in a desperate attempt to grow.

In the 1990s, as digital entertainment began to compete for children's attention, Lego's leadership made a fateful strategic choice. Rather than double down on the brick — making Lego more creative, more engaging, more relevant — they decided that Lego needed to become a "lifestyle brand." They would expand beyond the brick into any category where the Lego brand could be applied.

The result was a disastrous diversification. Lego launched clothing lines, watches, jewelry, theme parks, video games, television shows, and books. It created an action figure line called Galidor — a spectacular failure that cost the company hundreds of millions. It built Legoland theme parks, which lost money for years. It launched a clothing brand. Each of these expansions took capital, attention, and management bandwidth away from the core business, which was losing ground to digital competitors.

At the same time, Lego's product line had become wildly complex. The company had more than 12,000 different Lego elements — specialized pieces that were used in only one or two sets. Each new element required new molds, new tooling, new inventory, new logistics. The complexity was strangling the company. Every year, Lego introduced hundreds of new sets while many existing sets sold poorly. The company had lost sight of what made it special.

### The Diagnosis

In 2004, the Kirk Kristiansen family made a painful but necessary decision. They removed the CEO and brought in Jørgen Vig Knudstorp, a former McKinsey consultant who had joined Lego a few years earlier as head of corporate strategy. At 35, Knudstorp was young — the youngest CEO in Lego's history. He was also an outsider to the toy industry. And he was exactly what the company needed.

Knudstorp began by facing the truth. He commissioned a thorough analysis of Lego's financial situation, and the picture was devastating. The company was losing money on most of its products. The theme parks were a cash drain. The diversification into clothing and jewelry had been a strategic error. The complexity of the product line was destroying margins. The core — the brick — was being neglected.

The diagnosis was brutally simple: Lego had tried to be everything to everyone, and in doing so, had stopped being anything to anyone. The company needed to return to its core — the brick — and rebuild from there.

### The Guiding Policy

Knudstorp's guiding policy was articulated in a phrase that became the company's strategic mantra: "Back to the Brick."

This meant several things. First, Lego would stop trying to be a lifestyle brand. It would exit every business that was not directly related to the brick. Second, Lego would radically simplify its product line — fewer sets, fewer elements, fewer suppliers, fewer SKUs. Third, Lego would re-center its innovation strategy around the creative potential of the basic brick, rather than around novelty for its own sake.

The guiding policy was not just about product. It was also about how Lego operated. Knudstorp launched a program called "Lean Lego" — a systematic effort to reduce complexity, improve efficiency, and eliminate waste. Every part of the business was scrutinized for whether it was essential to the core mission.

### The Actions

Knudstorp's actions, over the next several years, were systematic and comprehensive:

**Sold the theme parks.** Lego sold its stake in the Legoland parks to Merlin Entertainments, freeing up capital and management attention. The parks continued to operate under license, generating royalty revenue for Lego without the operational risk.

**Killed 90 percent of products.** Lego reduced its product line from thousands of SKUs to hundreds, focusing on the sets that were profitable, popular, and connected to the core brick experience. Every product had to justify its existence. If it did not meet the strategic criteria, it was eliminated.

**Simplified the element base.** Lego dramatically reduced the number of unique pieces in its inventory, from over 12,000 to fewer than 6,000. Every new element had to go through a rigorous approval process. Designers were encouraged to find creative uses for existing elements rather than designing new ones.

**Reorganized the supply chain.** Lego moved most of its manufacturing back from outsourced suppliers to in-house production, reversing a decision that had caused quality problems and supply disruptions. The company invested in new manufacturing technology that made production more efficient and more flexible.

**Refocused innovation.** Rather than trying to compete with video games on their own terms, Knudstorp recognized that Lego needed to be the best version of itself. The company invested in digital products that enhanced the brick experience — Lego video games (like the wildly successful Lego Star Wars), Lego movies, Lego digital design tools — but always as complements to the physical brick, not replacements.

**Engaged the community.** Lego discovered that its most passionate customers were adults — AFOLs (Adult Fans of Lego) — who had never stopped loving the brick. The company began engaging with this community, listening to their ideas, incorporating their feedback, and eventually creating products co-designed with fans. The Lego Ideas platform, where fans submit designs and vote on which ones Lego should produce, became a powerful source of innovation.

### The Rise

The results were remarkable. Within a few years, Lego had gone from near-bankruptcy to extraordinary profitability. Revenue grew from approximately $1 billion in 2004 to more than $6 billion by 2015. The company's net profit margin became the envy of the toy industry. Lego passed Mattel and Hasbro to become the world's largest toy company.

The transformation was not just financial. Lego had reconnected with its identity. The company was no longer trying to be something it was not. It was the world's best builder of children's creative play experiences, built around a simple plastic brick that had been invented in 1958. Everything else was either an enhancement of that core or a distraction from it.

The most interesting part of Lego's story is what happened next. Having stabilized the core, Lego began to innovate around it in ways that were both creative and strategic. The Lego Movie, released in 2014, was a cultural phenomenon — a film that was genuinely about the creative spirit of Lego, not just a commercial for sets. Lego video games, particularly the Star Wars series, became bestsellers by combining the humor and creativity of Lego with popular franchises. Lego's partnerships with Disney, Marvel, Harry Potter, and other major brands drove enormous growth while staying firmly connected to the brick.

Even more innovative was Lego's approach to digital play. Lego launched Lego Life, a social network for children. It created Lego Boost and Lego Mindstorms, programmable robotics kits that taught children coding through building. It developed Lego Hidden Side, an augmented reality playset that combined physical building with digital gameplay. Each of these innovations started from the brick and extended its possibilities, rather than replacing it.

### The Lessons

Lego's story is, in many ways, the most hopeful of the three case studies. It shows that even a company on the verge of death can come back if it is willing to face the truth, return to its core, and rebuild systematically.

The first lesson: complexity is a silent killer. Lego's near-death was not caused by a sudden crisis but by the gradual accumulation of complexity — more products, more elements, more businesses, more initiatives. Each addition seemed reasonable in isolation. Collectively, they were destroying the company.

The second lesson: the core is more valuable than you think. Lego had abandoned the brick in pursuit of growth. But the brick was not a limitation — it was a foundation. By returning to the core, Lego did not limit its growth; it enabled it. The most innovative products Lego has ever created — the programmable robotics kits, the augmented reality sets, the movies — all came after the company recommitted to the brick.

The third lesson: change does not always mean transforming into something new. Sometimes it means returning to what you were always meant to be. Knudstorp's greatest insight was that Lego did not need to become something different. It needed to become more of itself.

The fourth lesson: engage your community. Lego's most passionate advocates were not inside the company — they were the adult fans who had never stopped believing in the product. By listening to them, Lego discovered possibilities that its own internal teams had missed.

The final lesson: crisis creates opportunity. Lego's near-death was terrifying for everyone involved. But it also created the conditions for transformation. The urgency was real. The need for change was undeniable. The status quo was unsustainable. Without the crisis, Lego might have limped along for years, slowly declining. The crisis forced the company to make the difficult choices that ultimately saved it.

## Leading Change in Your Own Organization

This chapter has given you a framework for understanding change. But frameworks are only useful if they change how you act. Let me close with practical advice — the actions you can take, starting tomorrow, to lead change more effectively in your own organization.

**Start with yourself.** Before you try to change your organization, be honest about your own relationship with change. Are you the one who needs to change first? Most leaders who fail at change fail because they are unwilling to change their own behavior. They want everyone else to adapt. Real change leadership starts with self-awareness.

**Diagnose before you prescribe.** The 8-step model begins with urgency, but urgency without diagnosis is dangerous. Before you launch a change effort, make sure you understand what is really happening. Talk to customers. Talk to frontline employees. Look at the data honestly. The right diagnosis makes everything else easier. The wrong diagnosis guarantees failure.

**Over-invest in urgency.** Whatever amount of urgency you think is necessary, double it. Triple it. Most leaders dramatically underestimate how much urgency is needed to overcome organizational inertia. If people are not genuinely concerned about the status quo, they will not change.

**Build your coalition before you need it.** Do not wait until a crisis to identify your allies. Build relationships with the people who have power, credibility, and influence in your organization. Nurture those relationships. When the time comes to lead change, you will need every ally you can get.

**Communicate until you are tired, and then keep going.** The most common mistake change leaders make is under-communicating the vision by a factor of ten. You cannot repeat the message too many times. You cannot use too many channels. You cannot be too consistent. Repetition is not the enemy of communication — it is the essence of it.

**Remove barriers ruthlessly.** Identify the systems, processes, and people that are blocking the change. Address them directly. Do not compromise with barriers. Do not hope they will go away. Remove them.

**Celebrate progress, not just results.** People need encouragement along the way. Celebrate the small wins. Recognize the people who are making the change happen. Momentum is built through a series of small victories, each one reinforcing the next.

**Be patient about time but impatient about urgency.** Change takes time. The full transformation of a large organization can take years. But urgency cannot wait. If people do not feel the urgency now, they will not sustain the effort over the months and years required for real change.

**Plan for your own irrelevance.** The ultimate test of a change effort is whether it survives your departure. If you are the only thing holding the change together, the change has not happened yet. Your goal should be to make yourself unnecessary — to embed the new behaviors so deeply in the culture, the systems, and the incentives that the organization can sustain them without you.

**Finally, remember what change really is.** It is not a project with a timeline and a budget and a steering committee. Change is the human process of letting go of the familiar and embracing the unfamiliar. It is loss, grief, fear, hope, discovery, and growth — all wrapped together. The leader who treats it as a technical problem will fail. The leader who treats it as a human journey has a chance.

## Summary

Change is the only constant in business. Markets shift. Technology evolves. Competition intensifies. Customer expectations rise. The organizations that survive and thrive are not the ones that avoid change — they are the ones that learn to lead it.

The 8-step model is a framework for leading change effectively. It addresses the fundamental human realities that make change difficult: the brain's threat response, the preference for certainty, organizational inertia, the emotional curve of loss, and the exhaustion of multiple competing priorities.

The three case studies in this chapter illustrate the principles in action. IBM succeeded because Gerstner understood that culture was the real problem and addressed it systematically. Nokia failed because its success created arrogance and internal divisions that prevented decisive action. Lego came back from near-death by returning to its core and rebuilding from there.

The lesson that connects all three stories is the same: change is hard, but it is possible. It requires urgency, vision, coalition, communication, barrier removal, short-term wins, sustained effort, and cultural anchoring. It requires leaders who understand the human dimension of transformation. And it requires the courage to face reality, even when reality is uncomfortable.

The price of not changing is higher than the price of change. That was true for IBM, true for Nokia, true for Lego, and true for every organization that has ever faced a transforming market. The question is not whether you need to change. It is whether you are willing to lead it.

## Further Reading

- Kotter, John P. *Leading Change*. Harvard Business Review Press, 1996 (updated 2012). The original book that introduced the 8-step model. If you read only one book on change, read this one.

- Kotter, John P. *A Sense of Urgency*. Harvard Business Review Press, 2008. A deeper dive into the first and most critical step of the change process.

- Bridges, William. *Managing Transitions*. Da Capo Press, 1991 (updated 2017). The best book on the psychological and emotional side of change. Bridges distinguishes between "change" (the external event) and "transition" (the internal process of letting go and adapting).

- Gerstner, Louis V. *Who Says Elephants Can't Dance?* HarperBusiness, 2002. Gerstner's own account of the IBM turnaround. Required reading for anyone interested in large-scale change leadership.

- Kübler-Ross, Elisabeth. *On Death and Dying*. Scribner, 1969. The original work on the five stages of grief, which has been adapted as a framework for understanding emotional responses to change.

- Rogers, Everett M. *Diffusion of Innovations*. Free Press, 1962 (updated 2003). The classic work on how new ideas and practices spread through populations. Essential for understanding how change moves through organizations.

- Christensen, Clayton M. *The Innovator's Dilemma*. Harvard Business Review Press, 1997. The foundational book on why successful companies fail when they face disruptive innovation. The essential companion to this chapter on the strategic side of change.
