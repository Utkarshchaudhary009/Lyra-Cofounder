# Chapter 28: The Shadow Organization — Power, Politics & Influence

In the autumn of 2005, a private investigator named Ron Roma sat in a nondescript office in suburban Boston and did something that would destroy one of America's most prestigious corporate boards. Acting on behalf of Hewlett-Packard's chairwoman Patricia Dunn, Roma called up a telephone company and, pretending to be a board member named George Keyworth, requested his detailed phone records. The phone company handed them over. No warrant. No court order. Just a lie delivered with enough confidence to fool a customer service representative.

This technique — "pretexting" — is illegal. But Dunn, who had been appointed HP's chairwoman just months earlier, had a problem she believed was urgent enough to justify extraordinary measures. Someone on HP's board of directors was leaking confidential information to the press — detailed accounts of boardroom discussions, strategic plans, executive compensation debates, and personnel decisions. The leaks were embarrassing, professionally damaging, and — in Dunn's view — a betrayal of the board's fiduciary duty. The leaker, she discovered through the pretexting investigation, was George Keyworth, a seventy-six-year-old board member who had served for two decades and was, ironically, one of HP's most respected directors.

The showdown came at a board meeting in May 2006. Dunn presented the evidence. Keyworth admitted the leaks — a series of fifteen separate disclosures to the press. A confrontation erupted. Some board members were furious at Keyworth for the leaks. Others were furious at Dunn for the methods she had used to catch him. The board split into factions. Personal insults were exchanged. One director, Tom Perkins — the legendary venture capitalist who co-founded Kleiner Perkins — stormed out of the meeting and resigned in protest.

Then the press got wind of the investigation itself. The story broke in September 2006. The California Attorney General launched a criminal investigation. The SEC launched an investigation. Congress held hearings. HP's stock dropped. Dunn was forced to resign as chairwoman — and then as a director. She faced criminal charges (the charges were eventually dropped, but her reputation never recovered). Keyworth resigned under pressure. The board was restructured. HP's carefully cultivated image as a company with a uniquely ethical "HP Way" culture lay in tatters.

And what did the leaks actually reveal? Boardroom debates. Strategic disagreements. Personnel issues. Nothing that remotely justified a covert investigation using illegal methods.

Here is what Patricia Dunn understood correctly: power exists in every organization, and the people who ignore it are naive. Here is what she failed to understand: power without wisdom is dangerous. The ability to investigate, to uncover secrets, to expose enemies — these are real forms of power. But power used unwisely destroys the person who wields it as surely as it destroys their targets.

Everyone says they hate office politics. Everyone plays them. The only choice is whether you play well or poorly.

## Section 1: What Is Power?

Let us begin with first principles, because most discussions of organizational politics founder on a basic confusion: people cannot agree on what power actually is.

**Power is the capacity to influence others.** That is the simplest definition, and it is the one I will use throughout this chapter. Power is not inherently good or bad. It is a tool. A knife can heal (in the hands of a surgeon) or kill (in the hands of an attacker). The tool itself is morally neutral. What matters is how it is used, toward what ends, and with what wisdom.

The mistake most people make is to treat power as something dirty — something to be avoided, denied, or apologized for. This is a luxury that only the powerless can afford. If you are trying to get anything done in an organization — launch a product, change a process, hire a great person, kill a failing project — you need power. You need the capacity to influence people who do not report to you, who have different priorities, who are protecting different interests. Without power, you are a passenger, not a driver.

### Formal Power vs. Informal Power

Power in organizations comes in two broad categories: formal and informal.

**Formal power** comes from position. It is the power written into the organizational chart — the authority to hire, fire, approve budgets, make decisions, set direction. When you become a vice president, you get certain formal powers that you did not have as a manager. When you become CEO, you get more. This is the power that organizations officially confer, and it is the power that most people think of when they think about organizational power.

But formal power is less useful than it appears. The CEO of a large company can, in theory, order any employee to do anything. In practice, the CEO cannot monitor everyone, cannot enforce every decision, and cannot overcome resistance from people who simply choose to comply slowly, poorly, or not at all. Formal power gives you the right to command. It does not give you the ability to be obeyed.

**Informal power** comes from relationships, expertise, reputation, and access. It is not written into the org chart. It is not conferred by title. It is earned — or accumulated — through behavior over time. The senior engineer who has been at the company for fifteen years, who knows where every body is buried and who has the respect of the entire engineering organization, has enormous informal power despite having no direct reports. The administrative assistant who has worked for four CEOs and knows how things actually get done has more informal power than most vice presidents.

The single most important truth about organizational power is this: **formal power tells you who is supposed to be in charge. Informal power tells you who actually is.**

This gap between the formal organization and the informal organization is what I call **the shadow organization** — and understanding it is the key to understanding how work really gets done.

### The Shadow Organization

Every organization has two structures. The first is the one printed on the organizational chart: the boxes and lines, the reporting relationships, the formal hierarchy. This structure tells you who reports to whom, who approves what, and where formal accountability lies.

The second structure is invisible. It is the network of relationships, trust, influence, and information flow that exists alongside the formal hierarchy. This is the shadow organization. It has no official recognition, no budget line, no place in the annual report. But it is where actual decisions are made, real influence is exercised, and genuine power resides.

Consider the following scenario. A large company announces a reorganization. The formal org chart changes. New people are put in charge. Reporting lines are redrawn. But six months later, the same people are making the same decisions in the same way, and the reorganization is quietly declared a failure. What happened? The formal organization was changed, but the shadow organization — the relationships, the trusted advisors, the informal networks of influence — remained intact. The shadow organization absorbed the formal change and continued operating as it always had.

Organizational network analysis (ONA) is the technique that makes the shadow organization visible. Developed by scholars like Rob Cross and Karen Stephenson, ONA maps the informal relationships within an organization by asking questions like: "Who do you go to for advice?" "Who helps you get work done?" "Who do you trust with sensitive information?" and "Who would you miss if they left?" The resulting network map reveals who the real influencers are — often people far from the top of the formal hierarchy.

The key insight of ONA is that influence follows predictable patterns. A small number of people — typically 5 to 10 percent of the organization — sit at the center of the informal network. These are the people who connect different groups, who know who needs to be involved in a decision, who can get things done across organizational boundaries. They may not have the biggest titles, but they have the greatest influence.

### French & Raven's Five Bases of Power

The most influential framework for understanding power in organizations was developed in 1959 by social psychologists John French and Bertram Raven. Their five bases of power remain the standard taxonomy because they are comprehensive and practical.

**Legitimate Power** is power based on position or title. The manager has the authority to assign work. The judge has the authority to sentence. The CEO has the authority to make strategic decisions. Legitimate power is the most visible form of power, but it is also the most limited — it only works within the scope of the position, and it only works if people accept the legitimacy of the hierarchy.

**Reward Power** is the ability to provide benefits — raises, promotions, bonuses, interesting assignments, public recognition, access to important people. Reward power is most effective when the recipient values the reward. A manager who controls access to coveted projects has reward power. A senior leader who can fast-track someone's career has reward power. The limitation: once you have given the reward, your power is temporarily exhausted until you acquire more rewards to distribute.

**Coercive Power** is the ability to impose costs — demotions, firings, bad assignments, public criticism, withholding of resources. Coercive power is the stick to reward power's carrot. It is extremely effective in the short term and almost always destructive in the long term. People who are coerced comply minimally, resentfully, and only as long as they are watched. Coercive power breeds resistance, turnover, and — in extreme cases — sabotage.

**Expert Power** is power based on knowledge, skill, or competence. The engineer who knows how to debug the most critical system has expert power. The surgeon who can perform the most complicated procedure has expert power. The analyst who understands the data better than anyone else has expert power. Expert power is unique among the five bases because it is entirely earned — it cannot be conferred by title or taken by force. It is also the most durable form of power because it is rooted in genuine capability that others depend on.

**Referent Power** is power based on relationships, likability, and personal identification. When people admire you, want to be like you, or value their relationship with you, you have referent power over them. Referent power is the most subtle and the most dangerous to fake. It comes from genuine trust, respect, and connection. It is the power that charismatic leaders have over their followers, that mentors have over their protégés, that trusted colleagues have over their peers.

### Beyond French & Raven: Three Additional Sources

The five bases cover the fundamentals, but three additional sources of power deserve attention.

**Information Power** is access to data that others need. In any organization, information is currency. The person who knows what the CEO is thinking, who has early access to the budget numbers, who understands the competitive intelligence, or who has visibility into a critical project has power — because they can decide what to share, when, and with whom. Information power is closely related to expert power but distinct: you can have access to information without having deep expertise, and you can have deep expertise without access to privileged information.

**Network Power** is power derived from connections. If you know the right people, you have network power. This is not simply "who you know" in the superficial sense — it is about being connected in ways that allow you to bridge gaps, facilitate introductions, and access resources that others cannot. Network power is the foundation of the shadow organization. The people with the most network power are those who occupy structural holes — positions that connect otherwise disconnected groups. If you are the only person in your company who has strong relationships with both engineering and marketing, you have enormous network power because you can facilitate communication and coordination that would otherwise be impossible.

**Resource Power** is control over things that people need — budget, headcount, equipment, office space, access to customers, manufacturing capacity, data center space. In most organizations, resources are not distributed equally. The people who control them have power, because they can allocate them in ways that help or hinder others. Resource power is closely tied to formal power (managers control budgets), but it can also be held informally (the senior engineer who controls access to the test environment has resource power regardless of title).

## Section 2: The Shadow Organization — Mapping Real Influence

Formal organizations are designed on PowerPoint. They have clean lines, clear boxes, and hierarchical symmetry. Every person fits into a neat slot. Every reporting relationship is unambiguous. Every decision has a clear owner.

Real organizations look nothing like this.

In a real organization, decisions are made in hallways, in Slack messages, over coffee, during lunch, in conversations that never appear on any calendar. Influence flows through relationships, not through org chart lines. And the people who really "run" the organization are often not the ones with the most impressive titles.

### How to Read the Real Organization

If you want to understand the shadow organization in your own workplace, here are the questions to ask:

**Who do people go to for advice?** This is the single best indicator of real influence. When someone faces a difficult decision — a strategic choice, a personnel issue, a technical problem — who do they seek out? The person they go to may not be their manager. It may be a former colleague, a mentor from another department, or a wise senior individual contributor who has no direct reports but has seen everything. In ONA terms, these people are advice seekers. They are the nodes in the network that receive the most incoming requests for guidance.

**Who knows everyone?** Every organization has people who seem to know everyone — who can connect you to the right person in any department, who understand the history and context of every decision, who have relationships that span levels and functions. These people are connectors. They are invaluable because they make the organization work as a network rather than a collection of silos.

**Who gets things done across boundaries?** When a project requires coordination between three departments that do not normally work together, who can make it happen? These people are boundary spanners. They have relationships and credibility across organizational lines, and they can navigate the political complexities that paralyze people who only operate within their own silo.

**Who controls information flow?** In every organization, information flows through specific channels. Some people are gates — they sit at the point where information enters or leaves a group. The executive assistant who triages the CEO's inbox controls information flow. The head of corporate communications who decides what gets announced controls information flow. The engineer who maintains the documentation repository controls information flow. These people have power that far exceeds their formal authority.

**Who do people trust with sensitive information?** This is a different question from "who has formal security clearance." Trust is earned, not granted. The person that multiple people confide in — about their frustrations, their ambitions, their concerns about a project or a leader — has a special kind of influence. They know what is really going on. And because they are trusted, they can shape perceptions and influence decisions in ways that are invisible to the formal hierarchy.

### The Trap of the Formal Org Chart

The most dangerous assumption you can make about organizational power is that the formal org chart tells you how decisions get made. It does not. The formal org chart tells you how decisions are *supposed* to be made. The shadow organization tells you how they actually *are* made.

New leaders often fall into this trap. They join an organization, study the org chart, identify the key players by title, and proceed as if the formal structure is the real structure. They are surprised when their decisions are not implemented, when resistance comes from unexpected places, or when they discover that the person they thought was in charge has no real influence.

The experienced leader does something different. They spend the first several months in a new role doing organizational reconnaissance — building relationships, having conversations, observing who talks to whom, and gradually mapping the shadow organization. They learn that the person with the best title may be a figurehead, and the person with no direct reports may be the one who truly runs things.

### Why the Shadow Organization Matters

The shadow organization is not a bug in organizational design. It is a feature. Organizations need informal networks because formal processes are too slow, too rigid, and too limited to handle the complexity of real work.

When you need an answer quickly, you do not file a formal request through official channels — you call someone you know. When you need to build consensus for a difficult decision, you do not schedule a formal meeting — you have informal conversations first to gauge reactions and address concerns. When you need to get something done across departmental lines, you do not escalate to your boss's boss — you find the person who has relationships in the other department and ask for an introduction.

The shadow organization is how work actually gets done. The people who understand it are effective. The people who ignore it are perpetually frustrated.

## Section 3: Coalition Building — The Art of Alliance

Power is rarely exercised alone. It is exercised through alliances. No one — not the CEO, not the most brilliant engineer, not the most charismatic leader — can accomplish anything significant without the support of others.

This is not a cynical observation. It is a practical one. Organizations are systems of interdependent people. No decision is made in isolation. Every action has ripple effects. To get something done, you need to bring others along — to align their interests with yours, to earn their support, to build the coalition that will make your initiative succeed.

### The Logic of Coalition Building

Coalition building is not manipulation. It is the process of finding common ground and building mutual commitment. It begins with a simple recognition: people support what they help create. If you design a plan in isolation and then present it to others as a fait accompli, you will face resistance — not because the plan is bad, but because people were not part of creating it.

Effective coalition builders follow a predictable pattern.

**First, they identify stakeholders.** Before proposing anything, they map the landscape of people whose support or opposition will matter. They ask: who will be affected by this decision? Who has power to help or hinder? Whose expertise is needed? Whose approval is required? They cast a wide net, including both obvious stakeholders (people with formal authority) and less obvious ones (people whose informal influence could make or break the effort).

**Second, they understand each stakeholder's interests.** Why does each person care about this issue? What are their goals, fears, constraints, and incentives? What would make them support the initiative? What would make them oppose it? This requires genuine curiosity and empathy — the ability to see the situation from perspectives other than your own.

**Third, they build relationships before they need them.** The mistake that inexperienced leaders make is to approach potential allies only when they need something. This creates a transactional dynamic that feels manipulative. The effective coalition builder invests in relationships continuously — showing interest in others' work, offering help without expectation of return, building trust over time. When the time comes to ask for support, the foundation is already in place.

**Fourth, they find the win-win.** Every coalition must be built on genuine mutual benefit. You cannot simply ask people to support your agenda because it is good for you. You must show them how it is also good for them — how supporting your initiative advances their goals, solves their problems, or addresses their concerns. This is not about tricking people into thinking your interest is theirs. It is about finding the actual intersection of interests.

**Fifth, they start with the easy wins.** When building a coalition, it is wise to approach the most likely supporters first. Secure their commitment early. Build momentum. Then approach the more skeptical stakeholders with the credibility that comes from having initial backing. A coalition that already has support from respected people is more attractive to fence-sitters.

**Sixth, they manage opposition carefully.** Not everyone will support you. Some people will oppose your initiative openly. Others will resist passively — promising support but failing to deliver. Effective coalition builders do not ignore opposition or try to crush it. They engage with it respectfully, seeking to understand the underlying concerns. Sometimes opposition can be converted into support by addressing genuine concerns. Sometimes it cannot — but even then, understanding the opposition helps you anticipate and neutralize objections.

### The Mistake of Going It Alone

The most common failure of ambitious people in organizations is the belief that they can accomplish great things through individual effort alone. This belief is attractive because it feels heroic. It is also wrong.

No significant organizational change has ever been accomplished by one person. Not one. The most celebrated leaders — the turnaround CEOs, the visionary founders, the legendary change agents — all built coalitions. They found allies. They earned support. They aligned interests. The lone hero narrative is a myth that obscures the messy, relational reality of how things actually get done.

Consider Jack Welch at General Electric. The popular narrative is of a singular leader who transformed a conglomerate through sheer force of will. The reality is that Welch spent years building his coalition — cultivating the board, courting key executives, developing a cadre of loyal leaders who would carry his vision throughout the organization. His famous initiatives were not commands from on high. They were coalition-building exercises that involved thousands of people in shaping the direction of the company.

The lesson: if you are trying to accomplish something significant and you find yourself doing it alone, stop. The problem is not that others do not see the wisdom of your plan. The problem is that you have not built the coalition to support it.

## Section 4: Influence Without Authority — The Practical Art

Most of the work in organizations happens sideways — across departments, between peers, among people who do not have formal authority over one another. You may need engineering to build your feature, marketing to promote your product, finance to approve your budget, legal to sign off on your contract, and operations to deliver your service. None of these people report to you. You cannot order them to help you. You can only influence them.

Influence without authority is the most important practical skill in organizational life. It is also the most neglected.

### The Strategies of Influence

There are seven strategies for exercising influence without authority, and they are worth understanding because they apply to almost any organizational situation.

**1. Build relationships first.** The most reliable way to influence people is to have a relationship with them before you need something. People help people they like, people they trust, and people they feel connected to. If your first interaction with a colleague is a request, you start at a disadvantage. If you have a pre-existing relationship — built through previous collaboration, shared experiences, or simply friendly interaction — your request starts from a position of goodwill.

This seems obvious, yet it is remarkable how many people skip the relationship-building step. They email a stranger in another department with an urgent request and are surprised when they get a slow or grudging response. They need something from a senior executive and have no existing connection to draw on. They are trying to influence people who have no reason to care about their success.

The practical implication: invest in relationships before you need them. Attend the cross-functional meetings. Join the social events. Take the coffee chat. Respond to others' requests promptly and generously. Build the relational capital that you can draw on when you need it.

**2. Create value first.** The most powerful negotiating position is to have already given something before you ask for something. This is the principle of reciprocity — one of the most reliable dynamics in human psychology. When someone does something for us, we feel a natural obligation to do something for them.

Effective influencers are generous with their time, their knowledge, and their resources. They help colleagues solve problems, share credit freely, and look for opportunities to create value for others without expecting immediate return. When they eventually ask for something in return, the reciprocity dynamic works in their favor.

**3. Find the win-win.** Influence is easiest when the other person genuinely benefits from helping you. This is not about tricking people into thinking your interest is theirs. It is about finding the actual intersection where helping you also helps them.

Suppose you need the legal department to expedite a contract review. Legal has no particular incentive to prioritize your request over any other. But if you can show how the deal helps legal's own goals — perhaps it sets a precedent that makes future reviews easier, or it involves a client that legal has been trying to build a relationship with — you create a genuine win-win. The request is no longer a favor. It is a collaboration.

**4. Appeal to shared purpose.** People are more likely to be influenced when they feel connected to a larger mission. If you can frame your request in terms of shared values, common goals, or organizational purpose, you tap into a deeper motivation than self-interest.

The head of engineering may not care about your marketing campaign. But if you can show how your campaign drives revenue that funds engineering's headcount growth, you appeal to a shared interest. If you can connect your project to the company's stated mission, you appeal to a shared purpose.

**5. Leverage your expertise.** Expert power is one of the few forms of power that does not depend on position. If you are the acknowledged expert in your domain, people will listen to you — not because they have to, but because it is in their interest to do so.

The key to leveraging expertise is to be genuinely useful. The expert who hoards knowledge or uses it to make others feel inferior creates resentment, not influence. The expert who shares generously, explains clearly, and helps others solve their problems builds the kind of reputation that makes people seek out their input.

**6. Ask for help.** There is a counterintuitive dynamic in human relationships: asking for help can actually strengthen a relationship. When you ask someone for advice or assistance, you signal that you value their judgment, their expertise, or their perspective. This is flattering. It also creates a sense of investment — people who help you become more committed to your success.

Benjamin Franklin famously used this technique. When he wanted to win over a political rival, he would ask to borrow a rare book from the man's library. The rival, flattered by the request, would lend the book. Franklin would return it with a thank-you note. The act of doing a favor for Franklin made the rival more favorably disposed toward him — the psychology of consistency at work.

**7. Use the power of binding.** Before making a request, get the other person to agree to a general principle that your specific request falls under. For example: "I know we both agree that customer satisfaction is the top priority, right?" Once they agree, your specific request — "So I'm going to need your team to prioritize this bug fix" — is harder to resist because rejecting it would be inconsistent with the principle they just endorsed.

This is not manipulation if the principle is genuinely shared. It is a way of making the connection between values and actions explicit.

### When Influence Doesn't Work

Influence is not magic. There are situations where it fails.

Some people are not open to influence. They are driven by ego, fear, or ideology in ways that make rational appeals ineffective. Some organizational cultures are so dysfunctional that influence has no traction — where trust is absent, where people hoard information, where every interaction is adversarial.

In these situations, you have three options. You can escalate to someone with formal authority (which may work in the short term but creates resentment). You can work around the person — find alternative paths to accomplish your goal that do not require their cooperation. Or you can recognize that the situation is not fixable from your position and invest your energy elsewhere.

The most dangerous mistake is to keep trying the same influence strategies in a situation where influence has no chance of working. Recognizing when to stop trying and change your approach is itself a form of wisdom.

## Section 5: Organizational Politics — The Good, the Bad, and the Ugly

"Office politics" is almost always used as a term of criticism. "He's so political." "She's playing politics." "I don't have time for the politics around here." These phrases describe politics as something toxic — something that honest, hardworking people should avoid.

This framing is wrong. Politics — the process by which different interests are negotiated, coalitions are built, and decisions are made — is an inevitable feature of organizational life. The only question is whether it is done well or poorly.

### The Good: Politics as Alignment

Good organizational politics is the art of building alignment among stakeholders with different interests. Consider a strategic decision that affects multiple departments: the marketing team wants to invest in a new campaign, the engineering team wants to invest in technical debt reduction, and the finance team wants to cut costs. Each position is legitimate. Each team has genuine interests.

Someone must navigate these competing interests to reach a decision. That someone — whether a leader, a project manager, or a cross-functional team — is engaged in politics. They are negotiating. They are building coalitions. They are helping each group see how their interests can be partially met while the organization moves forward.

Good politics is:
- Making your interests known honestly
- Listening to others' interests genuinely
- Searching for creative solutions that address multiple concerns
- Building coalitions around shared purposes
- Communicating transparently about trade-offs and constraints
- Giving credit to others and taking responsibility for failures

Good politics is what makes organizations function. Without it, every decision would require formal escalation, every conflict would become a crisis, and the organization would grind to a halt under the weight of its own procedural machinery.

### The Bad: Politics as Dysfunction

Bad politics is what happens when the process of negotiation and coalition-building becomes detached from organizational goals. Instead of building alignment around what is best for the organization, people build alignment around what is best for themselves — or for their department, their project, or their career.

The hallmarks of bad politics include:

**Empire-building.** The leader who consistently expands their scope, headcount, and budget not because the organization needs it but because a larger organization means more power and prestige. Empire builders treat their department as a personal fiefdom rather than a contribution to a larger enterprise.

**Information hoarding.** The person who deliberately withholds information to maintain their own importance, or who uses information selectively to advance their position. Information hoarding makes the organization less effective overall but makes the hoarder more indispensable in the short term.

**Credit-taking.** The manager who presents their team's work as their own, who takes credit for successes while deflecting blame for failures. Credit-taking destroys trust and demoralizes the people who actually did the work.

**CYA (Cover Your Ass) culture.** The pervasive behavior of documenting everything, avoiding any decision that could be criticized, and making sure that any failure can be plausibly attributed to someone else. CYA culture makes organizations slow, risk-averse, and incapable of learning.

**Turf wars.** The destructive conflict between departments or individuals over who "owns" a particular domain — customers, processes, data, decisions. Turf wars consume enormous amounts of organizational energy and produce nothing of value.

Bad politics is bad for everyone. It undermines trust, slows decision-making, wastes resources, and drives good people out of the organization. But it is important to understand that bad politics is not caused by "political people." It is caused by systems that reward political behavior over performance.

### The Ugly: Politics as Destruction

Ugly politics is bad politics taken to its extreme — where the methods used to gain or maintain power are genuinely destructive and often unethical.

**Sabotage.** Deliberately undermining a colleague's work, project, or reputation. This could be as overt as deleting files or as subtle as providing incomplete information, scheduling meetings that conflict with important deadlines, or spreading negative rumors.

**Deception.** Lying about facts, fabricating results, misrepresenting others' positions, or making promises with no intention of keeping them. Deception is a violation of trust that, once discovered, is nearly impossible to recover from.

**Backstabbing.** Pretending to be an ally while working against someone behind their back. Backstabbing is particularly destructive because it undermines the very possibility of trust — if colleagues cannot rely on each other's stated commitments, collaboration becomes impossible.

**Gaslighting.** Making someone question their own perception of reality — denying that events happened, claiming that the other person misunderstood, or systematically undermining their confidence and judgment.

**Public humiliation.** Deliberately embarrassing someone in a meeting, email, or public forum to assert dominance or diminish their standing. Public humiliation is a form of coercive power that destroys relationships and creates lasting resentment.

Ugly politics is the dark side of organizational life. It exists in every organization, though its prevalence varies enormously. The people who practice it often succeed in the short term — which is why it persists. But in the long term, ugly politics destroys the organizations that tolerate it. People who are victimized leave or disengage. The culture becomes toxic. The best performers — who have the most options — depart first, leaving the organization to be run by the people who are best at politics rather than the people who are best at work.

### The Reason It All Matters

Here is the uncomfortable truth that every leader must face: politics is not optional. Every decision in an organization involves trade-offs among competing interests. Every allocation of resources creates winners and losers. Every strategic choice advantages some departments, some projects, and some people over others.

You cannot opt out of politics by pretending to be above it. The person who refuses to build coalitions, who refuses to advocate for their interests, who refuses to negotiate — that person is not apolitical. They are simply ceding their influence to others. They are a passenger, not a driver.

The goal is not to eliminate politics. The goal is to practice good politics — and to recognize, avoid, and call out bad and ugly politics when you encounter it.

## Section 6: Networking — The Power of Connection

The word "networking" makes many people uncomfortable. It feels transactional, calculating, inauthentic. The image that comes to mind is the slick operator working a room at a conference, handing out business cards, making "connections" that have no substance.

That image is a caricature, and it obscures something important. Relationships matter. The people you know, the people who know you, and the structure of your network are among the most powerful determinants of your professional effectiveness.

### The Strength of Weak Ties

In 1973, sociologist Mark Granovetter published a paper that changed how we understand networks. His question was simple: when people find jobs, where do they hear about them?

The answer was surprising. Most jobs came not through close friends and family (strong ties) but through acquaintances — people the job seeker knew, but not well (weak ties). Granovetter called this "the strength of weak ties."

The logic is elegant. Your close friends and family tend to move in the same circles you do. They know the same people, hear about the same opportunities, and have access to the same information. Your acquaintances, by contrast, move in different circles. They have access to information and opportunities that you would never encounter through your strong ties.

Granovetter's finding has been replicated across dozens of studies in different contexts. Weak ties are disproportionately valuable for finding jobs, discovering new ideas, accessing diverse perspectives, and identifying opportunities outside your immediate environment.

The practical implication is clear: the most valuable people in your network are often not the ones you know best. They are the ones who connect you to worlds you do not inhabit.

### The Power of Centrality

Not all positions in a network are equal. Some people are central — they have many connections, they connect otherwise disconnected groups, and information flows through them. Other people are peripheral — they have few connections and limited access to network resources.

Social network analysis, the field that grew out of Granovetter's work, has identified several network positions that confer disproportionate influence.

**Hubs** are people with many direct connections. They know a lot of people. They have broad access to information and resources. Hubs are influential because they can reach many people quickly, and because their visibility makes them credible.

**Brokers** are people who connect otherwise disconnected groups. They sit at what network theorists call structural holes — gaps in the network where no one else is providing a bridge. Brokers have extraordinary influence because they control the flow of information between groups. If you are the only person who connects engineering to marketing, you can shape what each group knows about the other.

**Pulse-takers** are people who sit at the center of the informal communication network. They may not have the most connections, but they are the people that others consistently turn to for information, advice, and interpretation. Pulse-takers have influence because they shape the narrative — they are the ones who explain what is happening and what it means.

### The Trap of Inauthentic Networking

The most common mistake people make when they try to build their network is to approach it transactionally. They collect contacts. They send LinkedIn requests to strangers. They show up at events and hand out business cards. The result is a network that is wide but shallow — and, more importantly, a reputation for being someone who treats relationships as instruments.

Authentic networking is different. It is not about collecting contacts. It is about building genuine relationships with people you find interesting, respect, or enjoy. It is about being helpful without expecting immediate return. It is about staying in touch because you care, not because you might need something someday.

The people who are best at networking are not the slick operators. They are the people who genuinely like other people, who are curious about what others do, and who look for ways to be useful. They do not "network." They build relationships.

## Section 7: The Ethics of Power

No discussion of organizational politics can avoid the question that has haunted political philosophy for five centuries: does the end justify the means?

I am Niccolò Machiavelli. I am the figure most associated with the answer "yes" — with the idea that a prince (or a CEO, or any leader) must sometimes do terrible things for the greater good. I am also the most misrepresented figure in political philosophy. Let me clarify what I actually believed.

### What Machiavelli Actually Said

In *The Prince*, I wrote that a ruler who wants to maintain power must learn how not to be good — that there are times when conventional morality is a luxury that leaders cannot afford. I wrote that it is better to be feared than loved if you cannot be both. I wrote that a prince must have the cunning of a fox and the strength of a lion.

But I also wrote that a prince should avoid being hated at all costs — and that taking property from subjects or interfering with their families is the fastest way to earn hatred. I wrote that cruelty should be used quickly and decisively, and that benefits should be dispensed gradually so that people can savor them. I wrote that a prince should keep his word unless keeping it would harm him.

My central argument was not that morality is irrelevant. It was that the morality of a leader is different from the morality of a private citizen — because a leader's decisions affect the lives and welfare of many people. A private citizen who tells a small lie causes limited harm. A leader who tells a lie that starts a war causes enormous harm. The calculus is different.

### Influence vs. Manipulation

The critical distinction in organizational ethics is between influence and manipulation.

**Influence** is the art of persuading people to align with your interests by showing them how doing so also serves their interests. It is transparent, respectful, and grounded in mutual benefit. When you influence someone, you are open about what you want and why. You engage their reason and their values. You respect their autonomy to make their own choice.

**Manipulation** is the art of tricking people into acting against their interests by concealing your true intentions, distorting information, or exploiting their weaknesses. Manipulation is deceptive, disrespectful, and grounded in exploitation. When you manipulate someone, you are not open about what you want. You are using their emotions, their fears, or their trust to get them to do something they would not choose if they had full information.

The line between influence and manipulation is not always clear, but there are useful tests.

**The publicity test.** If your tactics were published on the front page of a newspaper, would you be comfortable? If you would be embarrassed or exposed, you are probably in manipulation territory.

**The reversal test.** If someone used these tactics on you, would you feel respected or exploited? If you would feel exploited, you are probably manipulating.

**The mutual benefit test.** Does the other person genuinely benefit from agreeing with you, or are you extracting their cooperation for your benefit alone? If the benefit is one-sided, you may be manipulating.

**The respect test.** Do you respect the other person's autonomy to make their own choice, or are you trying to override their judgment? If you are overriding their judgment through deception or pressure, you are manipulating.

### Red Lines

Never lie. Deception destroys trust, and trust destroyed is nearly impossible to rebuild. A reputation for honesty is the most valuable asset you can have, and it is astonishingly fragile.

Never sabotage others. Competition is healthy. Deliberately undermining someone else's work is not competition — it is destruction.

Never take credit for others' work. Not only is it unethical; it is stupid. The people whose work you steal will eventually expose you, and your reputation will be destroyed.

Never violate confidentiality. Trust is the currency of organizational life. If you betray a confidence, you are bankrupt.

Never humiliate others publicly. There is no organizational goal that requires you to destroy someone's dignity. And the witnesses to public humiliation will never trust you.

### The Machiavellian Trap

The greatest danger of studying power and politics — of reading this very chapter — is that you conclude that the game is rigged, that ethics are for suckers, and that the only rational approach is to be ruthless.

This conclusion is wrong for two reasons.

First, it is wrong practically. The people who succeed in the long term are overwhelmingly those who build trust, maintain integrity, and treat others with respect. The ruthless manipulators may win in the short term, but they eventually overreach, get caught, and fall. The literature on leadership, governance, and organizational behavior is remarkably consistent on this point: sustainable success requires ethical conduct.

Second, it is wrong morally. You cannot justify bad behavior by saying "that is how the world works." The world works the way people make it work. If you choose to act unethically because you assume others are doing the same, you are not a realist — you are contributing to the very culture you claim to be navigating.

The real Machiavellian insight is not "the ends justify the means." It is: understand power because power is real. But use that understanding wisely, because power without wisdom destroys the person who wields it.

## Case Study 1: The Borgia Playbook in Corporate America

The corporate boardroom of the early twenty-first century bears a striking resemblance to the courts of Renaissance Italy. The stakes are different — stock prices rather than kingdoms — but the dynamics of power are remarkably similar: alliances form and dissolve, factions compete for control, information is weaponized, and the line between legitimate influence and destructive politics is constantly tested.

### The HP Board Wars

Let us return to the story that opened this chapter, because the full saga of HP's boardroom battles reveals patterns that recur across organizations of every size and type.

The Hewlett-Packard that Patricia Dunn tried to govern in 2005 was not the company of its founders. Bill Hewlett and David Packard had built a company famous for its distinctive culture — "The HP Way" — which emphasized respect for individuals, contribution to the community, and management by wandering around. The HP Way was, in its time, one of the most admired corporate cultures in America. It was also, by 2005, largely a memory.

The company had gone through a tumultuous decade. It had acquired Compaq in a controversial 2002 deal that split the board and the founding families. Carly Fiorina, the CEO who drove the Compaq acquisition, had been fired in 2005 after a boardroom revolt. Mark Hurd had been brought in as the new CEO. The company was trying to compete in a rapidly consolidating technology industry while managing the legacy of two very different founders' visions.

The board itself was a collection of powerful, accomplished individuals — former CEOs, venture capitalists, academics, and a former US Senator. They had strong opinions and even stronger egos. Board meetings were reported to be contentious, with factions forming around different strategic visions.

The leaks that triggered Dunn's investigation were, in retrospect, a symptom of a dysfunctional board. Directors who felt their views were being suppressed or ignored took their case to the press. The leaks were not about espionage or betrayal — they were about influence. Directors who could not win internal battles were waging external ones.

Dunn's response — hiring private investigators who used pretexting — was a catastrophic overreaction. But it is important to understand why she did it. She believed that the leaks were undermining the board's ability to function, that they were violating the confidentiality that board governance requires, and that she had a fiduciary duty to stop them. She was not wrong about the problem. She was wrong about the solution.

The aftermath was brutal. Dunn was indicted by the California Attorney General on four felony counts (later reduced to two misdemeanors, then dropped entirely). Tom Perkins, the venture capitalist who resigned in protest, wrote a public letter calling the investigation "fascist." The HP board became a national embarrassment. The company's reputation for ethical leadership was shattered.

The lesson of HP is not that boardroom politics are dirty. It is that power without institutional guardrails is dangerous. Dunn had the formal power to launch an investigation. No one stopped her — not the board, not the legal department, not the ethics office. The systems designed to check power failed because the person with power was the one who controlled the systems.

### The Viacom/Redstone Family Drama

If the HP story is about institutional failure, the Redstone saga is about something more primal: the struggle for control of a media empire as its founder declined.

Sumner Redstone built Viacom — which owned Paramount Pictures, MTV, Nickelodeon, Comedy Central, and other major media properties — through a series of acquisitions and mergers that made him one of the most powerful figures in entertainment. By his nineties, Redstone controlled a voting trust that held roughly 80 percent of the voting shares of both Viacom and CBS, the two companies that emerged from his corporate empire. He was, in practical terms, a modern-day Medici — a single individual whose personal decisions determined the fate of a vast business empire.

The problem was that Redstone was in declining health. He had difficulty speaking. He was under 24-hour medical care. And two factions were battling for control of his legacy: his daughter, Shari Redstone, and his companion, a woman named Manuela Herzer.

The battle played out like a Renaissance drama. Herzer, who had been Redstone's companion and health caretaker, was removed from his health care directive in 2015. She responded by filing a lawsuit challenging Redstone's mental competence, alleging that he was being manipulated by Shari and that he was not capable of making his own decisions. The suit triggered a legal battle that exposed the family's dysfunction in excruciating detail — emails, medical records, financial documents, and testimony about Redstone's condition.

Shari Redstone, for her part, was fighting to protect her inheritance and her influence over the media empire. She had been pushed aside by her father years earlier, but she saw the decline of his health as an opportunity to reassert her position. She built alliances with key board members, cultivated relationships with the management teams of Viacom and CBS, and positioned herself as the guardian of her father's legacy.

The boardroom drama escalated when the CEOs of Viacom and CBS — Philippe Dauman and Les Moonves — were drawn into the conflict. Dauman, who was CEO of Viacom and a long-time Redstone ally, was removed from the trust that would control Redstone's shares after his death. He sued. Moonves, who was CEO of CBS, saw an opportunity to merge CBS and Viacom (a deal that Shari Redstone wanted) while also trying to protect his own position.

The intrigue eventually produced a series of outcomes that no one could have predicted. Moonves was forced out of CBS in 2018 following multiple allegations of sexual misconduct — an entirely separate scandal that intersected with the political dynamics of the board. The CBS-Viacom merger that Shari Redstone had wanted finally happened in 2019, but on very different terms than anyone had anticipated. Sumner Redstone died in 2020 at the age of 97, his empire reshaped by years of conflict that he was largely unable to influence.

### What Corporate Power Struggles Teach Us

The HP and Viacom/Redstone stories are extreme cases. Most organizational politics are not this dramatic. But the patterns are universal.

**Power attracts people who want it.** The people who rise to positions of formal power in large organizations are, almost by definition, ambitious and competitive. They did not get where they are by being passive. When their interests conflict — and they will — the result is politics.

**Power without accountability is dangerous.** Patricia Dunn had the formal authority to launch an investigation. No one told her she could not. In the Viacom case, Sumner Redstone had absolute control through his voting shares, and no one could overrule him. In both cases, the result was destructive.

**Organizational governance matters.** The reason we have boards, checks and balances, and governance processes is not to make organizations bureaucratic. It is to prevent the worst outcomes of unchecked power. When governance fails — when boards become rubber stamps, when checks are bypassed, when processes are ignored — the result is almost always trouble.

**Personal relationships are the real battleground.** In both HP and Viacom, the formal structures of governance were less important than the informal relationships among key players. Who trusts whom. Who is aligned with whom. Who has private conversations before the public meeting. The shadow organization was where the real power struggle happened.

## Case Study 2: Sheryl Sandberg at Facebook/Meta

In 2008, Sheryl Sandberg joined Facebook as its chief operating officer. At the time, Facebook was a three-year-old startup with roughly 100 million users, almost no revenue, and a founder-CEO, Mark Zuckerberg, who was twenty-three years old and had no interest in building a business. He wanted to build a product.

Sandberg came from Google, where she had built the AdWords and AdSense businesses — the advertising platforms that generated virtually all of Google's revenue. She was a seasoned operator with a deep understanding of how to build revenue from consumer internet products. She was also arriving at a company where she had no formal authority over product — Zuckerberg controlled that — and where the founder's vision was law.

The story of how Sandberg built Facebook's business is not just a story of corporate growth. It is a master class in influence without authority.

### The Situation

When Sandberg arrived, Facebook had no advertising strategy to speak of. The company was venture-funded and burning cash. Its user base was growing explosively, but there was no clear path to revenue that would support a large, independent company. Zuckerberg was philosophically opposed to traditional advertising — he had built Facebook as a social utility, not a commercial platform.

Sandberg's formal role was clear: she was the COO, responsible for business operations. But her informal challenge was daunting. She had to build a revenue-generating business inside a company where the founder was skeptical of revenue generation. She had to create an advertising platform that would respect users' privacy and Facebook's social mission. And she had to do all of this without alienating Zuckerberg or threatening his control over the product.

### How She Built Influence

**She built the relationship with Zuckerberg first.** Before she tackled any business challenge, Sandberg invested in her relationship with the CEO. She understood that her influence depended entirely on his trust. She spent time with him. She learned how he thought, what he valued, and how he made decisions. She earned his confidence by being reliable, honest, and aligned with his larger vision.

**She created value before asking for anything.** Sandberg did not arrive at Facebook and immediately demand changes. She spent her first months learning the business, understanding the culture, and identifying where she could be most useful. She focused on solving problems that Zuckerberg and the team cared about — operational challenges, organizational gaps, and growth bottlenecks. By the time she made her first major proposals, she had already demonstrated her value.

**She found the win-win.** Sandberg's core insight was that Facebook could build an advertising business that was consistent with its social mission. Instead of traditional display ads that interrupted the user experience, Facebook could offer social ads — advertisements that were integrated into the user's news feed, that reflected users' connections and interests, and that actually added value to the user experience by showing relevant products and services. This was not a compromise between business and mission. It was a synthesis.

**She respected Zuckerberg's primacy over product.** Sandberg never tried to take control of product decisions. She understood that Facebook was Zuckerberg's company in a way that was deeper than equity or title — it was his vision, his creation, his identity. She built the business around the product, not the product around the business. She made sure that Zuckerberg always felt he was in control of the company's direction.

**She built a team and let them shine.** Sandberg hired exceptional people and gave them real responsibility. She built a business organization that was world-class — with expertise in sales, partnerships, advertising technology, and business development. She then pointed to her team's accomplishments as evidence of what was possible, rather than taking personal credit.

**She expanded her influence gradually.** Rather than trying to change everything at once, Sandberg took on challenges one at a time. First, she stabilized the company's finances. Then, she built the advertising platform. Then, she expanded into international markets. Then, she built the partnerships and developer ecosystem. Each success increased her credibility and expanded her scope of influence.

### The Results

The results speak for themselves. When Sandberg joined Facebook in 2008, the company had roughly $300 million in revenue. By 2021, when she announced she was stepping down as COO, Meta's annual revenue was approximately $118 billion. Facebook went from a startup that was struggling to justify its valuation to one of the most valuable companies in the world.

Sandberg accomplished this without ever challenging Zuckerberg's control over product. She did not build a power base that threatened the founder. She did not create factions or engage in destructive internal politics. She built a business, built a team, and built a relationship of trust with the most important stakeholder.

### The Critique

The story of Sheryl Sandberg's influence at Facebook is not without its shadows. The Cambridge Analytica scandal, which erupted in 2018, raised serious questions about what Sandberg knew and when about the misuse of Facebook user data by political consulting firms. Critics argued that Sandberg, as the business leader, should have been more vigilant about how third-party developers were using Facebook data. The company's response to the scandal was widely criticized as slow and inadequate.

There is also a broader critique: that Sandberg's influence was used to maximize revenue at the expense of other values. The advertising business she built was based on extracting user data and targeting users with unprecedented precision. This model, critics argue, created the conditions for the very problems that later plagued Facebook — misinformation, polarization, election interference, and privacy violations.

Sandberg's defenders argue that she was building the business side of the company while Zuckerberg controlled product and policy decisions. The implication is that the ethical failures were not hers. But this argument is itself a testament to the nature of influence without authority: if Sandberg was influential enough to build a $118 billion business, was she also influential enough to insist on stronger privacy protections? If she was powerful enough to succeed, was she also powerful enough to challenge the company's direction?

### What Sandberg Teaches Us

Sandberg's story is the most important case study in this chapter because it demonstrates both the power and the limits of influence without authority.

The power: Sandberg built one of the most successful business operations in corporate history without direct control over the product. She did it through relationship-building, value creation, strategic patience, and genuine alignment with the founder's vision. Her approach is a template for anyone who needs to influence decisions they do not control.

The limits: influence without authority is still influence without authority. Sandberg could shape the business, but she could not — or did not — shape the deeper values and priorities of the company. When those values led to crisis, her influence was insufficient to redirect them. The question of whether she should have tried harder or should have succeeded anyway is one that students of organizational power must answer for themselves.

## Case Study 3: Robert Moses — The Master of Political Power

No one in American history understood organizational power better than Robert Moses. He never held elected office. He never became mayor, governor, senator, or president. Yet for nearly half a century, he shaped New York City more than any mayor or governor ever did.

Moses built: 658 playgrounds, 416 miles of parkways, 13 bridges, 2 dams, the 1964 World's Fair, Lincoln Center, Shea Stadium, the United Nations headquarters site, the New York State Power Authority, Jones Beach State Park, and dozens of other public works. He reshaped the physical landscape of New York and its suburbs in ways that are still visible today.

He did it through a masterful understanding of power — its sources, its uses, and its limits. He also did it through methods that were often destructive, sometimes racist, and ultimately corrupting. His story is the most powerful illustration in this book of what it means to understand power — and what happens when power becomes unchecked.

### The Foundation: Formal and Informal Power

Moses's formal power came from appointed positions. He was New York State Parks Commissioner, head of the Triborough Bridge Authority, head of the State Power Authority, chairman of the State Council of Parks, and New York City Parks Commissioner — sometimes holding multiple positions simultaneously. These were not the highest positions in government, but they were positions with specific powers: the ability to build things, to issue bonds, to control land, to hire and fire.

The genius of Moses's power structure was the Triborough Bridge Authority (TBA). The TBA operated bridges and tunnels in and around New York City. It charged tolls. And critically, its bonds were structured in a way that gave Moses almost complete control over the revenue. He did not need legislative approval to spend toll money. He did not need a majority vote on the board (he appointed the board members). The TBA was, in effect, a private treasury that Moses controlled.

This is the most important lesson about resource power in this chapter: **control the cash flow, control the organization.** Moses understood that formal authority was less important than control over resources. Mayors and governors came and went. Moses stayed, because the TBA kept generating toll revenue year after year, and he controlled how it was spent.

### How Moses Built His Empire

Moses used a set of power techniques that are still studied by students of organizational politics. They are worth cataloging because they work in any context.

**He accumulated multiple positions.** Moses did not rely on a single source of formal power. He held multiple positions simultaneously, often in different levels of government. When one position's authority was challenged, he would use another. When a mayor tried to assert control over parks, Moses would use his state position to override him. When the governor tried to control the bridge authority, Moses would use his city position to resist.

This technique — accumulating formal roles until you become impossible to remove from any one of them — is widely used in organizations. The person who is the head of engineering, the lead on the most important project, and the de facto product manager for the legacy system cannot be reassigned without disrupting three functions at once.

**He controlled information.** Moses understood that power flows through information. He was notoriously secretive about his plans, releasing details only when they were too far along to be stopped. He controlled the flow of information to the press, to politicians, and to the public. He released good news selectively and buried bad news in obscure reports.

In organizational terms, this is the strategy of information asymmetry: you have more information than the people you need to influence, and you use that advantage to guide their decisions.

**He built coalitions strategically.** Moses supported politicians who supported him. When a governor or mayor was friendly to his projects, Moses would deliver: jobs, contracts, ribbon-cutting ceremonies, favorable press coverage. When a politician opposed him, he would quietly support their opponents in the next election. He built a network of political allies who owed him favors, and he called in those favors relentlessly.

**He created irreversibility.** Moses's most powerful technique was to start building before anyone could stop him. He would break ground on a project before the legal challenges were resolved, before the environmental reviews were complete, before the community had been consulted. His logic was simple: once the concrete was poured, no one was going to tear it down. Build first, ask for permission later.

This technique — strategic irreversibility — is widely used in organizations. The executive who launches a project before going through formal approval, the engineer who deploys code before the security review is complete, the manager who hires someone before the headcount is approved — they are all using the same principle. It is risky. It is often unethical. And it is remarkably effective.

**He understood the system better than anyone.** Moses had an encyclopedic knowledge of the laws, regulations, contracts, and procedures that governed public works in New York. He knew exactly how much discretion each position had, where the legal loopholes were, which approvals were real and which were ceremonial. He could outmaneuver opponents simply because he understood the rules better than they did.

### The Dark Side

The same power that allowed Moses to build hundreds of parks and playgrounds also allowed him to do enormous harm.

His projects destroyed neighborhoods. The Cross-Bronx Expressway, one of his most famous projects, cut through the heart of the Bronx, displacing thousands of families and dividing communities that have never recovered. The expressway was routed through predominantly minority neighborhoods — not by accident, but by design. Moses explicitly chose routes that minimized political opposition from wealthy white neighborhoods and maximized his ability to build quickly through poor and minority communities.

His park projects were segregated. Moses built magnificent state parks on Long Island, but he designed them in ways that discouraged public transportation access — making it difficult for poor and minority residents to reach them. He infamously ordered the construction of low bridges on the parkways leading to Jones Beach, bridges too low for buses to pass under. The effect was to exclude the poor and minority populations who relied on public transit.

His power became unchecked. By the 1960s, Moses had accumulated so much control over so many different authorities and agencies that no one could effectively challenge him. He answered to no one. He was, in the words of his biographer Robert Caro, "the power broker" — a man whose power had grown beyond the systems designed to constrain it.

### The Lesson: Power Corrupts

The story of Robert Moses is the most important cautionary tale in this chapter. It demonstrates that understanding power is essential — but that power without accountability is dangerous. Moses knew how to build power, how to use it, and how to protect it. He accomplished extraordinary things. But his accomplishments came at a terrible human cost.

The same is true in any organization. The leader who understands power can accomplish more than the leader who ignores it. But the leader who understands power and has no checks on that power will eventually do harm — to people, to the organization, and to themselves.

The lesson is not that power is evil. It is that power must be accompanied by wisdom, accountability, and ethical constraints. Moses had power in abundance. He lacked the wisdom to use it well.

## The One Thing to Remember

> Organizational politics are not optional. You can play well, or you can play poorly — but you cannot opt out. The key is to understand how power really works, build genuine relationships, create value for others, and use your influence ethically. Power without wisdom destroys. Wisdom without power is impotent.

## How to Use This Tomorrow

1. **Map your shadow organization.** Draw two org charts. The first is the formal one — the box-and-line structure your company publishes. The second is the real one: who do people actually go to for advice? Who connects different groups? Who controls information? Whose opinion carries more weight than their title would suggest? The gap between the two charts is where you need to focus your attention.

2. **Audit your power sources.** Using French and Raven's five bases plus the three additional sources (information, network, resources), assess your own power. Which sources are strong? Which are weak? What is your plan to strengthen the weak ones without compromising your integrity?

3. **Build your coalition before you need it.** Identify three to five people whose support would be critical for your next major initiative. Invest in those relationships now — before you need anything from them. Find ways to create value for them. Build the relational capital you will draw on later.

4. **Practice influence without authority.** For your next cross-functional project, consciously apply the seven strategies of influence. Build relationships first. Create value before asking. Find the win-win. Ask for help. Track what works and what does not. Influence is a skill, and like any skill, it improves with deliberate practice.

5. **Run the ethics test.** Before using any influence tactic that feels even slightly aggressive or manipulative, run the four tests: the publicity test (would I be comfortable if this were public?), the reversal test (would I feel respected if this were done to me?), the mutual benefit test (does the other person genuinely benefit?), and the respect test (am I respecting their autonomy?). If any test gives you pause, choose a different approach.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1: Map Your Organization's Shadow Structure**

Choose a significant decision that your organization made recently — a project prioritization, a budget allocation, a reorganization, or a strategic choice. Map the decision process. Who was formally responsible? Who actually influenced the outcome? Who was consulted? Who was left out? What does this tell you about the shadow organization in your workplace?

**Exercise 2: The Power Inventory**

List the ten most influential people in your organization (broadly defined — not just by title). For each person, identify their primary sources of power using the French and Raven framework plus the three additional sources. Which sources are most common among influential people in your organization? Are there patterns — for example, does the organization reward expertise or relationships more? What does this tell you about how to build your own influence?

**Exercise 3: The Influence Challenge**

Identify a situation at work where you need something from someone who does not report to you. Using the strategies from Section 4, design an influence plan. Who do you need to influence? What do they care about? What value can you create for them first? What is the win-win? Execute the plan. Then reflect: what worked, what did not, and what would you do differently next time?

**Exercise 4: The Ethics Line**

Think of a situation where you observed or experienced influence that crossed the line into manipulation. What made it cross the line? Using the four tests (publicity, reversal, mutual benefit, respect), identify where exactly the line was crossed. Now think of a situation where you were on the other side — where you used influence that could be seen as borderline. Would you do it again? What would you do differently?

## Further Reading

- **The 48 Laws of Power** by Robert Greene — A controversial and widely-read book that distills historical examples of power dynamics into practical laws. Greene's approach is more cynical than this chapter's, but the historical examples are valuable. Read it as a catalog of patterns to recognize, not necessarily as a playbook to follow.

- **The Power Broker: Robert Moses and the Fall of New York** by Robert Caro — The single best case study of organizational power ever written. Caro's biography of Moses is over a thousand pages and every one of them is worth reading. It is not just a biography — it is a master class in how power is accumulated, used, and abused. If you read only one book on power, this should be it.

- **Influence Without Authority** by Allan Cohen and David Bradford — A practical guide to the specific challenge of getting things done when you do not have formal power. The authors provide a structured framework for identifying stakeholders, understanding their interests, and building the relationships that make influence possible.

- **The No Asshole Rule** by Robert Sutton — A research-based argument for why toxic political behavior destroys organizations, and what leaders can do about it. Sutton's work is an important counterweight to the cynical view that organizational politics requires ruthlessness.

- **Give and Take** by Adam Grant — Grant's research on reciprocity in organizations shows that the most successful people are not takers (who exploit others) or matchers (who trade favors), but givers — people who contribute generously without expecting immediate return. The book is a powerful empirical argument for ethical influence.
