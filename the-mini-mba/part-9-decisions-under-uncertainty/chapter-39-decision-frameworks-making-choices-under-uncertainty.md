# Chapter 39: Decision Frameworks — Making Choices Under Uncertainty

On January 28, 1986, the space shuttle Challenger lifted off from Kennedy Space Center at 11:38 a.m. Eastern time. Seventy-three seconds later, the shuttle broke apart in a fireball, killing all seven crew members. The disaster was broadcast live on television. Millions of schoolchildren — Christa McAuliffe, a schoolteacher, was one of the crew members — watched in real time as the shuttle disintegrated against a blue Florida sky.

The cause was a failure in the O-rings — rubber seals in the solid rocket boosters that were not designed to operate in freezing temperatures. The temperature at launch time was 36 degrees Fahrenheit, about 15 degrees colder than any previous shuttle launch. The cold made the O-rings brittle and prevented them from sealing properly, allowing hot gas to escape and ignite the external fuel tank.

Here is what makes the Challenger disaster a case study in decision-making rather than just a tragedy of engineering: the engineers at Morton Thiokol, the company that built the solid rocket boosters, knew about the O-ring problem in cold weather. They had data. They had warned NASA. The night before the launch, during a tense teleconference, they explicitly recommended against launching in temperatures below 53 degrees.

NASA managers overruled them.

This was not a decision made by a single reckless individual. It was the product of a decision-making process — a system of communication, authority, and group dynamics — that was deeply flawed. Confirmation bias, groupthink, normalization of deviance, and a culture that punished dissent all combined to produce a decision that, viewed with the clarity of hindsight, was catastrophically wrong.

But here is the harder truth, the one that takes longer to sink in: the same flawed decision-making processes that killed the Challenger crew play out every day in boardrooms, in investment meetings, in product launches, in hiring decisions, in strategy sessions. The stakes are usually lower. But the cognitive machinery is the same.

The Challenger disaster is not a story about bad people. It is a story about a bad decision-making process. And the gap between good people and good decisions is the subject of this chapter.

## Decision Quality vs. Outcome Quality

Let me start with the most important idea in this chapter — the idea that, if you absorb nothing else, will change how you think about every decision you make for the rest of your career.

Here it is: **the quality of a decision is not determined by the quality of its outcome.**

You can make a brilliant decision and lose. You can make a terrible decision and win. The outcome is influenced by luck — by factors outside your control. The decision is the only thing you control.

This is obvious to poker players. I spent twenty years playing professional poker, and every hand taught me this lesson. You can have the best hand, play it perfectly, and lose because the one card you needed — a card with a 4 percent chance of appearing — shows up on the river. You can have a mediocre hand, play it poorly, and win because your opponent makes an even worse mistake, or because an unlikely card saves you.

Poker players have a word for judging decisions by outcomes. We call it **"resulting."** It is the cognitive bias of evaluating a decision based on what happened rather than how you arrived at it. And resulting is, without question, the single most dangerous cognitive bias in business.

Think about what resulting does to your ability to learn. If you make a good decision and it works out, you learn nothing — because you assume you were right. If you make a good decision and it fails, you learn the wrong lesson — you assume you were wrong. If you make a bad decision and it works out, you reinforce bad habits. If you make a bad decision and it fails, you may or may not learn the right lesson, depending on whether you attribute the failure to the decision or to bad luck.

Here is the pattern most executives follow:

- Good outcome → Good decision (actually, it could have been a terrible decision that got lucky)
- Bad outcome → Bad decision (actually, it could have been a brilliant decision that got unlucky)

The result is that organizations systematically learn the wrong things from their experience. They celebrate the lucky and punish the unlucky. They reward the reckless whose risks happened to pay off, and they penalize the prudent whose caution happened to be unnecessary.

I once worked with a hedge fund manager who made a trade based on meticulous analysis. He had researched the company, modeled the scenarios, assessed the probabilities. He was 70 percent confident the trade would work. It failed. The next week, his boss called him into a meeting and essentially said: "You made a bad trade. We lost money. Don't do it again."

This is resulting in its purest form. The trade was a good trade — it had positive expected value, it was based on sound reasoning, and the manager had properly calibrated his uncertainty. The outcome was bad because the 30 percent probability happened. That is not a bad decision. That is probability.

The CEO who approves a product launch that has a 60 percent chance of success and a 40 percent chance of failure, and then gets fired when the 40 percent happens, was not a bad CEO. But try telling that to the board. Resulting is so deeply embedded in our culture — in our compensation systems, in our hiring practices, in our performance reviews — that even people who understand it intellectually struggle to overcome it.

I want to pause here and let this land, because it is genuinely counterintuitive. We are raised to believe that results matter. They do — outcomes matter enormously. But outcomes are not feedback about decision quality. They are a combination of decision quality and luck. And if you cannot separate the two, you cannot learn, you cannot improve, and you will systematically make your organization worse by punishing good decisions that happen to fail and rewarding bad decisions that happen to succeed.

## The Decision Stack

Every decision, whether it is a poker hand, a product launch, or a hiring choice, follows a structure. I call this the **Decision Stack** — the anatomy of any decision. Understanding it helps you diagnose where your decision-making process is breaking down.

The Decision Stack has six layers:

### 1. Frame

What decision are you actually making? This sounds trivial, but it is not. Most bad decisions start with a bad frame — asking the wrong question.

"Is this a good investment?" is a bad frame. It is too vague. A better frame: "Given my portfolio's current allocation, risk tolerance, and return requirements, should I allocate $X to this opportunity, knowing what I know about its probability distribution?"

The frame determines everything that follows. If you frame a decision as "should we launch this product?" rather than "given our current product portfolio, market position, and resource constraints, should we launch this product now or wait six months?" — you will get a different answer. The frame is the lens through which you see the decision, and a distorting lens produces a distorted decision.

### 2. Gather

What information do you have? What information do you need? What information is available but you have not yet accessed? What information is simply unknowable?

The gather phase is where most business decisions go wrong, but not for the reasons you might think. The problem is usually not that organizations fail to gather enough information. The problem is that they gather information that confirms what they already believe — confirmation bias — and they stop gathering when they have enough information to support their preferred conclusion.

A disciplined gather phase requires you to actively seek disconfirming evidence. Ask: "What would make me change my mind?" and then go find that information. If you cannot find anything that would change your mind, you are not thinking — you are defending a conclusion.

### 3. Evaluate

Given the frame and the information, what are your options? What are the possible outcomes for each option? What is the probability of each outcome? What is the value of each outcome?

The evaluate phase is where you do the explicit probabilistic thinking. Most business decisions are made with implicit probabilities — "I think this will work" — rather than explicit ones — "I think there is a 65 percent chance this works, a 25 percent chance it works but takes longer than expected, and a 10 percent chance it fails completely."

The discipline of making probabilities explicit is not about getting the numbers exactly right. It is about forcing yourself to think in shades of gray. It is about creating a record you can revisit. And it is about making yourself vulnerable to being wrong in a way that you can learn from.

The evaluate phase is also where **expected value** comes into play. Expected value is a simple calculation: for each option, multiply the probability of each outcome by its value, then sum across all outcomes. The option with the highest expected value is not guaranteed to produce the best outcome — but over many decisions, consistently choosing the highest expected value option yields the best results. The key insight is that expected value does not depend on any single outcome. A decision with positive expected value can produce a negative outcome. That does not mean it was the wrong decision. It means the unlikely happened. The discipline of expected value thinking prevents you from overreacting to individual outcomes.

A common mistake in the evaluate phase is what I call **narrow bracketing** — evaluating each decision in isolation rather than as part of a portfolio. A product launch with a 60 percent chance of success might look risky if you evaluate it alone. But if you are launching ten products, each with a 60 percent chance of success, the portfolio is quite safe — you will almost certainly have several winners. The evaluate phase should consider not just the individual decision but the context of other decisions being made simultaneously.

### 4. Decide

This is the moment of choice. You have framed, gathered, and evaluated. Now you choose.

The decide phase is where most analysis paralysis lives. Organizations get stuck because they keep gathering more information, running more analyses, scheduling more meetings. They are trying to eliminate uncertainty before making a decision. You cannot eliminate uncertainty. The goal is to make the best decision given the uncertainty that remains.

The decide phase requires courage — the willingness to act without certainty. If you are waiting for certainty, you will never decide.

### 5. Execute

The best decision in the world is worthless if it is not implemented well. Execution is part of the decision process, not separate from it.

This is obvious but often ignored. Organizations make a decision — "we will enter the Asian market in Q3" — and then treat the execution as someone else's problem. But the execution reveals information that should feed back into the decision. Maybe the Asian market entry is taking longer than expected. Maybe the costs are higher. The decision is not made once and then executed. It is made, executed, iterated, and remade.

### 6. Learn

The final layer — and the one most organizations skip entirely — is learning. After the outcome is known, you go back and compare your expectations to reality.

How did my probability estimate compare to what actually happened? What did I miss? What assumptions turned out to be wrong? What would I do differently next time?

The learn phase is the only way to improve your decision-making over time. But it requires something that most organizations are terrible at: honest post-mortems that separate decision quality from outcome quality.

I will return to this in the case study on Thinking in Bets, because the learn phase is where the decision journal and the decision pod become essential tools. For now, just recognize that learning is not automatic. It requires a deliberate process.

## Thinking in Bets

I have spent more than two decades making decisions under uncertainty for a living. Professional poker is, at its core, a decision-making sport — you are dealt incomplete information, you must act under time pressure, and the outcome of any single hand is heavily influenced by luck. The only way to win over the long run is to make better decisions than your opponents, hand after hand, day after day, year after year.

The approach I developed — which I call **Thinking in Bets** — is built on four practices that apply directly to business decisions.

### Practice 1: Express Uncertainty as Probabilities

Most businesspeople express confidence with language like "I'm pretty sure" or "I think this will work" or "our analysis suggests." These phrases sound precise, but they are not. They are verbal placeholders that mask uncertainty rather than express it.

The alternative is to use explicit probabilities. Instead of "I think this deal will close," say "I'm 70 percent confident this deal will close." Instead of "our analysis suggests this product will succeed," say "our analysis puts the probability of success at 60 percent, with a 30 percent chance of moderate success and a 10 percent chance of failure."

This is uncomfortable at first. It feels presumptuous — how can you assign a number to something uncertain? But that discomfort is the point. You are already assigning an implicit probability to every uncertain outcome. Making it explicit forces you to think about what you actually believe. And it makes you accountable — when the outcome happens, you cannot say "I was wrong" if your 70 percent confidence turned out to be the 30 percent that did not happen. You were not wrong. You were probabilistically correct.

### Practice 2: Create a Decision Journal

A decision journal is exactly what it sounds like: a record of your decisions, the reasoning behind them, and what you expected to happen. You write it before you know the outcome.

The decision journal is the most powerful tool I know for combating resulting. When you have a written record of your thinking at the time you made the decision, you cannot rewrite history. You cannot tell yourself, "I knew it all along" (hindsight bias). You cannot convince yourself that the outcome was obvious (creeping determinism). You have a document that captures your actual uncertainty, your actual reasoning, and your actual probability estimates.

Here is the format I recommend:

- Date and decision
- The frame: what decision am I making?
- The options I considered
- The information I had (and crucially, the information I was missing)
- My probability estimate for each option
- The reasoning behind my estimate
- My prediction: what do I expect to happen?
- When will I know if I was right?

Reviewing your decision journal after outcomes are known is where the real learning happens. You look back and ask: was I too confident? Not confident enough? What information did I miss? What assumptions were wrong? The pattern you are looking for is not "was I right?" but "was my calibration good?" — meaning, did my 70 percent estimates happen about 70 percent of the time?

### Practice 3: Separate Luck from Skill

This is the hardest practice, because it requires you to fight your own brain. Your brain wants to take credit for good outcomes and blame bad luck for bad outcomes. That is the self-serving bias, and it is one of the most well-documented biases in psychology.

The alternative is equally uncomfortable: take credit for good decisions (not good outcomes) and take responsibility for bad decisions (not bad outcomes), while recognizing that luck played a role in both.

Ask yourself, after any outcome: "How much of this outcome was due to my decision, and how much was due to luck?" There is no formula for this. But the act of asking the question changes your thinking. It makes you less likely to get overconfident after wins and less likely to get discouraged after losses.

### Practice 4: Form a Decision Pod

A decision pod is a small group of trusted peers who agree to give each other honest feedback on decisions. The rules are:

1. You present a decision you are facing, without the outcome (if the outcome is already known, you present it before you reveal what happened)
2. The group challenges your thinking, asks questions, and offers alternative perspectives
3. No one judges you for being wrong — the pod exists to improve decision quality, not to evaluate results
4. You agree to be vulnerable — to show your uncertainty, your doubts, your reasoning process

The decision pod is the single most effective tool I know for improving decision quality. It forces you to articulate your thinking to people who will push back. It exposes blind spots. It reduces overconfidence. And it creates a culture of intellectual honesty that is rare in most organizations.

I will share a story from my own experience. During my poker career, I was part of a small group of professional players who met regularly to discuss hands. We would present a hand we had played, describe our thinking, and ask for feedback. The first time I presented, I expected the group to validate my play. Instead, they pointed out three things I had missed — assumptions I had made without checking, probabilities I had miscalculated, biases I had not recognized. It was uncomfortable. It was also invaluable. I became a better player because of that feedback.

The same principle applies in business. The executives who have a trusted advisor who tells them the truth — not what they want to hear — make better decisions than those who do not. The decision pod institutionalizes that relationship.

## Cognitive Biases in Decision-Making

Let me walk through the cognitive biases that most consistently corrupt business decisions. I will not try to cover every bias — there are more than a hundred documented — but I will focus on the ones that do the most damage in organizational decision-making.

### Confirmation Bias

Confirmation bias is the tendency to seek out, interpret, and remember information that confirms what you already believe. It is the most pervasive bias in decision-making, and it is the hardest to recognize in yourself.

In a business context, confirmation bias shows up when a team is evaluating a potential acquisition. Someone on the team — usually the CEO or a senior executive — decides that the acquisition is a good idea. Suddenly, everyone is finding reasons why it makes sense. The financial model is adjusted to support the conclusion. The due diligence focuses on confirming synergies rather than identifying risks. The dissenting voices are marginalized.

I have watched organizations spend millions on deals that should never have happened, because confirmation bias turned the decision process into a justification exercise.

The antidote to confirmation bias is active disconfirmation — deliberately seeking evidence that your current view is wrong. Before you make a major decision, assign someone to build the case against it. Not as a devil's advocate exercise (more on that later), but as a genuine attempt to find reasons why the decision is a mistake.

### Sunk Cost Fallacy

The sunk cost fallacy is the tendency to continue investing in a failing course of action because you have already invested time, money, or effort that you cannot recover.

Economists will tell you that sunk costs are irrelevant to future decisions. What matters is the expected value of future investments, not what you have already spent. But humans are not economists. We feel the pain of losing what we have invested, and we irrationally throw good money after bad to avoid admitting that the earlier investment was a mistake.

I see sunk cost fallacy most often in product development. A company has spent two years and $10 million building a product. The market has shifted. The product does not meet customer needs. The rational decision is to kill it. But the team says: "We have already invested so much. Let us pivot. Let us try one more thing. Let us not waste all that work."

The $10 million is gone. It is a sunk cost. The only question that matters is: if you were starting from scratch today, with no prior investment, would you build this product? If the answer is no, kill it. The $10 million is not a reason to spend another $5 million. It is a reason to stop spending.

### Anchoring

Anchoring is the tendency to rely too heavily on the first piece of information you receive — the "anchor" — when making decisions. Once the anchor is set, all subsequent information is interpreted relative to it.

In negotiations, anchoring is well understood. The first offer sets the anchor. If you are selling your company and the buyer offers $50 million, that number becomes the reference point, even if your company is worth $100 million. All subsequent negotiation happens relative to $50 million.

But anchoring operates in more subtle ways. The first analyst report you read about a company shapes your view of all subsequent information. The first product design you see becomes the baseline against which all alternatives are judged. The first budget number proposed anchors the entire planning process.

The antidote to anchoring is to generate your own anchor before you hear anyone else's. Do your own analysis first. Set your own reference point. Then when someone else's anchor arrives, you have a benchmark of your own to compare it against.

### Overconfidence

Overconfidence is the tendency to overestimate your own knowledge, abilities, and judgment. It is perhaps the most dangerous bias for senior executives, because success tends to amplify it. The more successful you have been, the more confident you become — and the less able you are to recognize your own limitations.

The research on overconfidence is sobering. When people say they are "90 percent confident" about something, they are right about 70 to 80 percent of the time. The more confident people are, the less accurate they tend to be. Experts are often less accurate than they think — and sometimes less accurate than novices, because experts have more elaborate (and more confidently held) mental models that can be wrong in sophisticated ways.

The best antidote to overconfidence is calibration training — making explicit probability estimates and tracking your accuracy over time. When you can see, in black and white, that your 90 percent estimates are actually only accurate 70 percent of the time, you start to adjust your confidence levels.

### Hindsight Bias

Hindsight bias is the tendency to see past events as having been more predictable than they actually were. After the outcome is known, you believe you "knew it all along."

Hindsight bias is particularly dangerous because it destroys learning. If you believe you knew the outcome in advance, you do not examine what you actually knew, what you missed, or what you could have done differently. You simply tell yourself: "I was right" or "It was obvious."

The 2008 financial crisis is a perfect example. After it happened, everyone claimed to have seen it coming. But before it happened, almost no one predicted it. The housing market had been rising for years. The financial system was complex. The risks were distributed in ways that were hard to see.

Hindsight bias is the enemy of honest post-mortems. It makes you believe you were smarter than you actually were, which means you do not learn from your mistakes.

The antidote is the decision journal. When you have a written record of what you actually thought before the outcome was known, you cannot rewrite history. You can see your actual predictions — and your actual confidence levels — and compare them to what happened.

### Normalization of Deviance

Normalization of deviance, a term coined by sociologist Diane Vaughan, describes the process by which deviations from safe or expected standards become accepted over time. It happens incrementally. A small deviation occurs and nothing bad happens. The deviation is deemed acceptable. Another small deviation occurs, slightly larger, and still nothing bad happens. The acceptable range expands. Inch by inch, the boundary shifts.

In business, normalization of deviance shows up in budget overruns that are always "the last one," in product launches that ship with known bugs because the previous ones shipped with bugs and it was fine, in safety procedures that are skipped because no one has been hurt yet, in ethical boundaries that edge slightly because no one has gotten caught.

The danger is that the organization does not realize the boundary has shifted. Each individual deviation seems minor. It is only in retrospect — after the catastrophic failure — that the pattern becomes visible. The O-rings had been eroding for years before Challenger. Each erosion was documented, analyzed, and accepted. The normalization of that deviance made the disaster not just possible but predictable.

### Groupthink

Groupthink is the tendency for groups to prioritize harmony and consensus over critical evaluation. When groupthink takes hold, dissenting opinions are suppressed, alternatives are not explored, and the group makes decisions that its individual members might privately disagree with.

Groupthink is what killed the Challenger crew. NASA managers wanted to launch. They had a schedule to keep. They had political pressure. The engineers who warned against launching were marginalized. No one wanted to be the person who delayed the launch. No one wanted to be the person who raised a concern that turned out to be unnecessary.

The classic symptoms of groupthink include: an illusion of invulnerability (we cannot fail), collective rationalization (we have reasons for ignoring the warning signs), belief in inherent group morality (we are good people making good decisions), stereotyping outsiders (the engineers are being alarmist), self-censorship (I will not raise my concern), illusion of unanimity (everyone seems to agree, so I must be wrong), and direct pressure on dissenters (you are being negative).

The antidotes to groupthink are structural. Assign someone to play devil's advocate. Create anonymous feedback channels. Encourage debate before decisions, and enforce commitment after decisions (the "disagree and commit" model we will discuss in the Bezos case study). Most importantly, create psychological safety — an environment where people can raise concerns without fear of retaliation.

I have seen groupthink destroy more decisions than any other bias. It is insidious because it feels good. Consensus feels harmonious. Disagreement feels uncomfortable. But the discomfort of disagreement is the price of good decisions. If your meetings feel too comfortable, you are probably suffering from groupthink.

## Pre-Mortem Analysis

One of the most powerful decision-making tools I know is the **pre-mortem**. It was developed by psychologist Gary Klein, and it is deceptively simple.

Here is how it works. Before you make a major decision, gather the relevant stakeholders. Tell them: "It is six months in the future. Our decision has been implemented. And it has failed spectacularly. The outcome is a disaster. Now, everyone write down why it failed."

The pre-mortem does two things that ordinary risk assessment does not.

First, it bypasses the optimism bias — the tendency to underestimate the likelihood of negative outcomes. When you ask people to identify risks in a normal planning meeting, they are still working from the assumption that the project will succeed. They identify risks, but they discount them. The pre-mortem assumes failure, which forces people to think seriously about what could go wrong.

Second, it surfaces risks that people might be reluctant to mention. In a normal meeting, raising a risk can feel like being negative — like you are not a team player. In a pre-mortem, everyone is required to generate reasons for failure. There is no social cost to identifying problems. In fact, the person who identifies the most credible failure scenario is contributing the most value.

I have facilitated pre-mortems for dozens of organizations, and they consistently surface risks that the team had not considered. A product launch pre-mortem might reveal that the sales team is not trained on the new features, or that the manufacturing partner has a history of delays, or that the pricing model assumes a customer willingness-to-pay that has not been validated. These risks would have emerged eventually — but by then, it would be too late to address them.

The post-mortem — analyzing a failure after it happens — is too late. The pre-mortem gives you the chance to prevent the failure before it occurs.

## Decision Trees

A decision tree is a visual tool for mapping decisions, uncertainties, and outcomes. It forces you to think explicitly about what you know and what you do not know.

The structure is simple. A decision tree has:

- **Decision nodes** (usually drawn as squares): points where you choose between options
- **Chance nodes** (usually drawn as circles): points where uncertainty determines the outcome
- **Branches**: the possible paths from each node
- **End nodes** (usually drawn as triangles): the final outcomes, with their values

The power of decision trees is not in the visual itself. It is in the discipline required to build one. To create a decision tree, you must:

1. Identify all possible options (decision nodes)
2. Identify all possible outcomes for each option (chance nodes)
3. Assign probabilities to each outcome
4. Assign values to each end state
5. Calculate the expected value of each option by multiplying probabilities by values and summing across branches

The exercise of building a decision tree forces you to confront your assumptions. If you think a product launch has a 70 percent chance of success, what does "success" mean exactly? How do you define it? What is the value of success? What are the other 30 percent of outcomes? Are they all the same, or are there different types of failure with different costs?

I have seen executives resist building decision trees because they do not want to commit to specific probabilities. "We cannot put a number on that," they say. But you are already using a number — you just are not articulating it. When you say "I think this will work," you are expressing a probability. Making it explicit does not add uncertainty. It reveals it.

Decision trees are also useful for identifying the value of additional information. If you are uncertain about a key variable — customer adoption rate, competitor response, regulatory approval — you can calculate the expected value of perfect information: how much would it be worth to know with certainty what will happen? If the answer is "a lot," then it is worth investing in more research before making the decision.

Here is a concrete example. Imagine you are deciding whether to launch a new product. You estimate a 60 percent chance of success, with success worth $10 million and failure costing $2 million. The expected value is (0.6 * $10M) + (0.4 * -$2M) = $6M - $0.8M = $5.2M. That seems like a good bet. But you are uncertain about one key assumption: customer adoption. If you could know with certainty whether customers will adopt the product, you could avoid the $2 million loss if adoption will be low. The expected value of that information is the $2 million loss times the 40 percent probability of failure — $800,000. If you can learn whether customers will adopt for less than $800,000 — through a market study, a prototype test, or a pre-sale campaign — it is worth doing the research before making the full launch decision.

This kind of analysis prevents two common mistakes: making a decision without enough information (when the information would be cheap to obtain) and delaying a decision to gather more information (when the information would not change the expected value calculation).

## Mental Models — A Decision-Maker's Toolkit

Mental models are frameworks for understanding the world. They are simplified representations of how things work — not the truth, but useful approximations that help you make better decisions. The best decision-makers have a large toolkit of mental models that they apply flexibly across different situations.

Here are the mental models I have found most useful for business decisions under uncertainty.

### Second-Order Thinking

Second-order thinking is asking: "What happens after what happens?" Most people think only about the first-order effects of a decision. They ask: "If I lower prices, will sales increase?" That is first-order thinking. Second-order thinking asks: "If I lower prices, sales increase, which means competitors will also lower prices, which means margins compress for everyone, which means we all end up worse off — so should I lower prices?"

The most dangerous decisions are the ones where the first-order effect is positive and the second-order effect is negative. Lowering prices feels good in the short term (more customers!). But the second-order effect — a price war — can destroy the industry's profitability.

Second-order thinking is what separates good strategists from average ones. Everyone can see the immediate consequences. Few people can trace the chain of consequences two or three steps out.

### Inversion

Inversion is a mental model popularized by the German mathematician Carl Jacobi, who advised: "Invert, always invert." Instead of asking "how do I achieve success?" ask "what would guarantee failure?" Then avoid those things.

Inversion is powerful because it bypasses the optimism bias. When you ask "how do I achieve success?" your brain generates positive scenarios. When you ask "what would guarantee failure?" your brain generates risks and obstacles. Both perspectives are useful. But most people only use the first one.

Apply inversion to any major decision. Before you launch a product, ask: "What would guarantee that this product launch fails?" The answers — poor timing, wrong pricing, inadequate marketing support, unaddressed competitor response — give you a checklist of things to avoid.

### Circle of Competence

The circle of competence is the boundary of what you know and understand deeply. Within your circle, you can make decisions with confidence. Outside your circle, you are guessing.

The hard part is knowing where the boundary is. Most people overestimate the size of their circle. They think they understand things they do not actually understand. The most dangerous person in any organization is the one who does not know what they do not know.

Warren Buffett lives by the circle of competence. He has said: "I don't try to jump over 7-foot bars. I look for 1-foot bars that I can step over." He invests in businesses he understands deeply and avoids businesses he does not understand, even if they are obviously successful. He missed the tech boom because technology was outside his circle. He did not care. He waited for opportunities inside his circle.

The lesson for decision-making: know what you know, and know what you do not know. When you are operating outside your circle of competence, get advice from someone who is inside it. Or do not make the decision.

### Occam's Razor

Occam's razor states that when you have competing explanations for a phenomenon, the simpler explanation is more likely to be correct. The principle is not a guarantee — sometimes the truth is complicated — but it is a useful heuristic.

In decision-making, Occam's razor helps you avoid overcomplicating problems. When you see a decline in sales, the simplest explanation might be: the price is too high, or the product quality has declined, or a competitor has introduced a better alternative. Before you build an elaborate theory involving changing customer preferences, macroeconomic trends, and shifting cultural norms, rule out the simple explanations first.

### Hanlon's Razor

Hanlon's razor states: "Never attribute to malice what can be adequately explained by incompetence."

This is an essential mental model for organizational decision-making. When someone makes a decision that harms you or your department, your instinct is to assume they acted maliciously — that they were trying to undermine you, or that they were pursuing their own agenda at your expense. Hanlon's razor reminds you that the far more likely explanation is that they made a mistake, or they did not have the same information you have, or they were operating under different constraints.

This mental model does two things. First, it protects you from unnecessary anger and conflict. Second, it improves your decision-making by forcing you to consider alternative explanations for others' behavior — which leads to better responses than assuming malice.

## Group Decision-Making

The quality of a group's decisions is not simply the sum of its members' individual decision-making abilities. Groups can make better decisions than individuals — or much worse ones, as the Challenger disaster demonstrates.

Here are the structural practices that improve group decision-making.

### Disagree and Commit

"Disagree and commit" is a decision-making principle popularized by Jeff Bezos at Amazon. The idea is simple: when a group cannot reach consensus on a decision, team members are allowed (and expected) to debate vigorously. They surface their disagreements, present their evidence, and argue their positions. But once a decision is made — even if not everyone agrees — everyone commits to implementing it as if they fully supported it.

The key insight is that disagreement is about making a better decision, not about proving you are right. Once the decision is made, continued disagreement is no longer productive. It undermines execution.

Disagree and commit requires psychological safety. People must feel safe expressing disagreement during the debate phase. If dissent is punished — even subtly — people will stay silent, and the group will suffer from false consensus.

### Red Team / Blue Team

Red team / blue team is a practice borrowed from military and intelligence operations. The "blue team" proposes a plan or decision. The "red team" is assigned to attack it — to find weaknesses, identify risks, and argue against it.

The red team is not the devil's advocate (one person playing a role). It is a dedicated team of people who are genuinely trying to find flaws in the plan. The difference matters. A devil's advocate can be dismissed — "that is just his job." A red team's criticisms carry more weight because they are not playing a role. They are genuinely trying to prevent a bad decision.

The key to making red teams effective is to give them resources, time, and protection. If the red team is understaffed, rushed, or punished for finding problems, the exercise is useless.

### The "Devil's Advocate" Role

If you cannot field a full red team, at least assign a devil's advocate. One person in the meeting is explicitly tasked with finding reasons not to proceed.

The devil's advocate role must be rotated. If the same person is always the devil's advocate, they become marginalized — "that is just Sarah, always negative." Rotating the role ensures that everyone develops the muscle of critical thinking.

The devil's advocate must also be protected. If people who raise concerns are penalized, no one will volunteer for the role, and the exercise becomes performative — the designated devil's advocate raises mild concerns that are quickly dismissed.

### The Rule of Pre-Decision Dissent

Here is a rule I recommend every organization adopt: before any major decision, someone must be assigned to argue against it. Not optionally. Not if they happen to disagree. Assigned.

The rule ensures that every decision is stress-tested. It prevents groupthink. And it creates a culture where dissent is not just tolerated but expected.

The rule does not mean the decision will not be made. It means the decision will be better because the arguments against it have been surfaced and addressed.

## Case Study 1: Jeff Bezos's Decision-Making Framework

Jeff Bezos built Amazon from a garage-based online bookstore into one of the most valuable companies in human history. The scale of that achievement is hard to overstate — it is like building a small town into a global city in a single generation. And while many factors contributed to Amazon's success, one of the most important was Bezos's decision-making framework.

Bezos thought explicitly about how decisions should be made. He wrote about it in his annual shareholder letters. He discussed it in interviews. He built it into Amazon's culture, its operating processes, and its leadership principles. The framework is not a secret — it is documented, public, and replicable.

### Type 1 vs. Type 2 Decisions

Bezos's most important contribution to decision-making theory is his distinction between Type 1 and Type 2 decisions.

**Type 1 decisions are irreversible.** They are like walking through a door that closes behind you. Once you make a Type 1 decision, you cannot go back, or the cost of reversal is extremely high. Examples: acquiring a company, entering a new geographic market, building a data center, signing a long-term lease, choosing a core technology platform.

**Type 2 decisions are reversible.** They are like walking through a door that is still open behind you. If you make a Type 2 decision and it turns out to be wrong, you can walk back through the door. The cost of reversal is low. Examples: choosing a software tool, hiring a mid-level employee, launching a test feature, setting a price for a short-term promotion.

The key insight is that most organizations treat all decisions like Type 1 decisions. They apply the same level of scrutiny, the same number of approvals, the same amount of analysis to every decision. The result is paralysis. Decisions take too long. Opportunities are missed. The organization becomes slow and bureaucratic.

Bezos's framework is: **most decisions are Type 2.** They are reversible. You can try something, see if it works, and undo it if it does not. The cost of being slow — of missing the opportunity — is often higher than the cost of being wrong.

I have seen this dynamic in every organization I have worked with. A company spends three months analyzing whether to run an A/B test on a landing page. Three months! The test itself would take two weeks. The decision is completely reversible — you can always go back to the original page. But the organization has built a culture where every decision requires the same level of approval, analysis, and committee review.

Bezos's framework liberates organizations to move faster. It says: if the decision is Type 2, make it quickly, with 70 percent of the information you would ideally have. If you wait for 90 percent, you are too slow.

The practical implication is significant. Most organizations have a bottleneck at the top — senior leaders are approving decisions that could be made much lower in the organization. Bezos's framework suggests a simple rule: if a decision is Type 2, delegate it. Do not escalate it. Do not require four levels of approval. Let the people closest to the work make the call. The cost of an occasional wrong decision is lower than the cost of slowing down every decision.

For Type 1 decisions, the opposite applies. Take your time. Gather as much information as you can. Consult widely. Use structured frameworks. Run pre-mortems. Involve senior leadership. The cost of being wrong on a Type 1 decision is high enough that the additional process is justified.

The framework also has a meta-implication: you can make a Type 2 decision about how to categorize other decisions. If you are uncertain whether a decision is Type 1 or Type 2, treat it as Type 2. If it turns out to be Type 1, you will learn that quickly. If it turns out to be Type 2, you saved time and moved faster.

### Disagree and Commit

"Disagree and commit" is a principle Bezos articulated in his 2016 shareholder letter. I mentioned it earlier, but it deserves a deeper treatment here.

The scenario Bezos describes is: you have a team that cannot reach consensus on a decision. The senior leader believes strongly in a particular direction. The team has real concerns — and they have expressed them. Under a traditional consensus model, the leader would either push for consensus (which may never come) or impose the decision from above (which creates resentment and passive resistance).

Disagree and commit offers a third path. The leader says: "I hear your disagreement. I respect your reasoning. But I believe this is the right path, and I am going to commit to it. I ask you to commit as well. Not to change your mind, but to execute with full energy, even though you personally disagree."

The commitment is real. The people who disagreed during the debate are expected to execute as if they fully agreed. They do not sabotage. They do not say "I told you so" if it fails. They give the decision their best effort.

Bezos himself has described how this works in practice: "If you have conviction on a particular direction even though there is no consensus, it is helpful to say, 'Look, I know we disagree on this, but will you gamble with me on it?' At Amazon, we have a lot of experience with this model. At the senior level, we disagree a lot, but we commit. And we get a lot done."

### The 6-Page Memo

Amazon meetings start with silence. Participants read a six-page memo — written in full narrative prose, not bullet points — before any discussion begins. The memo is the meeting. Discussion follows, but only after everyone has absorbed the same information.

The 6-page memo is a decision-making tool disguised as a communication format. Here is why it matters for decision quality.

**Writing forces clarity.** When you write in full sentences and paragraphs, you cannot hide behind vague language. You cannot use bullet points to skip the logical connections between ideas. You must state your assumptions explicitly. You must explain your reasoning. You must acknowledge counterarguments.

**Reading ensures shared context.** In most meetings, people arrive with different levels of preparation. Some have read the materials. Some have skimmed. Some are hearing the proposal for the first time. The 6-page memo forces everyone to start from the same baseline. The first 30 minutes of the meeting are silent reading — which means the next hour of discussion is grounded in shared understanding.

**The structure forces rigor.** Bezos specified the format: a memo of up to six pages (hence the name, though the actual length can vary), written in narrative form, with a specific structure. The memo starts with the context or question, describes the approach, explains the data and assumptions, discusses alternatives considered, and makes the recommendation.

I have watched teams spend two weeks writing a single 6-page memo. That is the level of investment. Because the process of writing — thinking through the logic, finding the weak spots, trying different framings, getting feedback, rewriting — is where the value is created.

### Data-Informed, Not Data-Driven

One of the most misunderstood aspects of Amazon's approach is its relationship with data. Amazon is famous for being data-obsessed. But Bezos was equally clear that data has limitations.

Data cannot tell you the future. Data cannot tell you what customers will want next year. Data cannot tell you whether to enter a new market. Data tells you what has already happened. Decisions about the future require judgment, intuition, and vision.

Bezos called this being "data-informed" rather than "data-driven." Data informs the decision. It does not make the decision. The difference is subtle but crucial. A data-driven organization waits for the data to tell it what to do — which leads to paralysis when the data is ambiguous, and to following competitors rather than leading. A data-informed organization uses data as input to judgment — which allows it to make bold bets based on imperfect information.

This is especially relevant for Type 1 decisions. For irreversible decisions, you will never have perfect data. You will never know the outcome in advance. The temptation is to keep gathering more data until you are certain. Bezos's framework says: at some point, you must use your judgment. The data will never give you certainty.

### Wandering

Bezos's final contribution to decision-making is the concept of "wandering" — exploration without a clear destination.

Most corporate decision-making is weighted toward efficiency: find the shortest path to the known goal. Wandering is the opposite. It is exploration without knowing what you will find. It is investing in ideas that may not pay off. It is allowing teams to pursue paths that are not obviously profitable.

Wandering is how Amazon Web Services was born. It is how the Kindle was born. It is how Alexa was born. None of these products emerged from a traditional strategic planning process. They emerged from wandering — from giving smart people the freedom to explore ideas without knowing where they would lead.

The tension between efficiency and exploration is one of the hardest challenges in organizational decision-making. Efficiency is about executing known plans. Exploration is about discovering unknown opportunities. Both are necessary. But the structures that support efficiency — detailed budgets, quarterly targets, approval processes — can kill exploration.

Bezos's solution was to create space for wandering within a culture that was otherwise ruthlessly efficient. The 6-page memo allowed ideas to be evaluated on their merits rather than their origin. The "two-pizza team" structure allowed small groups to explore ideas without needing organization-wide approval. The willingness to fund experiments that might fail — and to celebrate the learning from those failures — created a culture where wandering was safe.

## Case Study 2: The Challenger Disaster — How NOT to Make Decisions Under Uncertainty

I have referenced the Challenger disaster throughout this chapter as the archetypal case of flawed group decision-making. It is time to examine it in detail.

The engineering details are straightforward. The space shuttle used two solid rocket boosters, each made up of four segments. The segments were joined by O-rings — rubber seals designed to prevent hot gas from escaping between the segments. The O-rings were designed to operate within a specific temperature range. At low temperatures, the rubber became brittle and lost its ability to seal.

The night before the launch, the temperature at Kennedy Space Center was forecast to be well below freezing. Engineers at Morton Thiokol — the company that built the boosters — had data from previous launches showing O-ring damage when temperatures were below 60 degrees. They recommended against launching below 53 degrees, which was the coldest previous launch temperature. The actual temperature at launch time was 36 degrees.

NASA managers wanted to launch. The shuttle had already been delayed several times. The launch window was closing. Political pressure was high — President Reagan was scheduled to give the State of the Union address that evening, and he was expected to mention the shuttle and the teacher on board.

The teleconference on the night before the launch is one of the most thoroughly documented decision-making failures in history. Here is what happened.

### The Confirmation Bias

NASA managers had already decided that the launch should proceed. The teleconference was not a genuine decision-making meeting — it was a confirmation-seeking exercise. The managers were looking for reasons to launch, not reasons to delay.

When the Thiokol engineers presented their data showing O-ring damage in cold weather, the NASA managers did not engage with the data. Instead, they pushed back. They asked for more evidence. They suggested that the data was inconclusive. They argued that the engineers could not prove that a disaster would occur.

This is confirmation bias in action. The managers sought evidence that supported their preferred conclusion (launch) and dismissed evidence that contradicted it (don't launch). They demanded a higher standard of proof from the anti-launch position than from the pro-launch position.

### The Normalization of Deviance

"Normalization of deviance" is a term coined by sociologist Diane Vaughan, who studied the Challenger disaster extensively. It describes the process by which deviations from safe operating procedures become normalized over time.

The O-rings had shown signs of damage in previous launches. In 1981, an inspection revealed that O-rings had been eroded by hot gas. NASA investigated and decided the erosion was acceptable — the O-rings had enough margin to tolerate some erosion. In 1984, another flight showed more erosion. Again, it was deemed acceptable. By 1985, O-ring erosion was a known issue, but it had become normal. It had happened before and nothing catastrophic had occurred. The absence of a disaster was taken as evidence that the risk was acceptable.

This is a classic decision-making trap: past success becomes a rationale for future risk. "It did not break last time, so it will not break this time." But the absence of failure is not evidence of safety. It is just the absence of failure. The risk remains, even if it has not materialized yet.

### The Authority Gradient

The authority gradient — the difference in power between the engineers and the managers — was a critical factor in the decision.

The Thiokol engineers who recommended against launching were junior. They were making a technical argument against the will of their customer, NASA. The NASA managers were senior, powerful, and had the authority to override the engineers' recommendation.

When the engineers presented their data, the NASA managers pushed back. The engineers' own management — senior executives at Thiokol — eventually came under pressure to reverse their recommendation. The Thiokol senior managers, who were in the room with the engineers, initially supported the no-launch position. But after the NASA managers pushed back, the Thiokol managers asked for a private caucus. During that caucus, the senior managers overruled their own engineers and changed the recommendation to launch.

The engineers were not fired. They were not punished. But they were overruled. And their voices were not heard in the final decision.

The authority gradient is a structural problem in organizational decision-making. When junior people have information that senior people need to hear, the information often does not reach the decision-makers — or it reaches them in diluted form. Senior people, by virtue of their position, are less likely to hear bad news. The solution is to create channels for dissenting information to reach decision-makers without being filtered by the hierarchy.

### The "Go" Culture

NASA had a culture of "go" decisions. The default position was that a launch would proceed. "No-go" was an exception that required explicit justification. Over time, this created a bias toward action — toward proceeding, toward launching, toward saying yes.

In a "go" culture, the burden of proof falls on the person saying "no." They must provide overwhelming evidence that the action should not be taken. The person saying "yes" faces no comparable burden — they can proceed by default.

This is the opposite of how high-stakes decisions should be made. When the cost of failure is catastrophic — as it was with the Challenger — the default position should be "no." The burden of proof should fall on the person saying "yes." They should have to demonstrate that the risks are acceptable.

### What a Better Process Would Have Looked Like

Imagine the same meeting, with the same information, but with a better decision-making process.

First, the frame would have been different. The question would not have been "should we launch?" but "do we have sufficient evidence that launching is safe?" The burden of proof would be on the pro-launch position.

Second, someone would have been assigned to argue against launching. The devil's advocate would have had the resources and the protection to make a strong case, without worrying about career consequences.

Third, the authority gradient would have been flattened. The engineers would have presented their case directly to the decision-makers, without their senior management as an intermediary. Or the decision-makers would have come to the engineers' facility to hear the evidence on the engineers' territory.

Fourth, the pre-mortem would have been conducted. Someone would have said: "It is tomorrow afternoon. The shuttle has exploded. Everyone on board is dead. Why did it happen?" The answers would have pointed directly to the O-ring failure in cold weather.

Fifth, the groupthink would have been surfaced. Someone would have asked: "Is anyone in this room privately uncomfortable with this decision but not speaking up?" Psychological safety would have allowed people to voice their concerns.

Sixth, the decision would have been explicitly framed as Type 1. The launch decision was clearly irreversible — once the shuttle lifted off, there was no going back. Under Bezos's framework, this classification would have triggered a different level of scrutiny, a different standard of evidence, and a different decision-making process. The burden of proof would have shifted from "prove it is unsafe" to "prove it is safe."

Seventh, the probabilities would have been made explicit. Someone would have said: "Based on our data, I estimate the probability of O-ring failure at these temperatures to be X percent. What is the organization's tolerance for that risk?" The discussion would have shifted from "will it fail?" to "what is the acceptable probability of failure?" — a fundamentally different and more honest conversation.

Any one of these process changes might have prevented the disaster. The tragedy is that none of them were in place.

## Case Study 3: Annie Duke's "Thinking in Bets"

I have spent this chapter describing a set of decision-making principles and practices. Now let me show you how they work together in practice — starting with a story from my own life.

In 2004, I won the World Series of Poker Tournament of Champions, the largest women's-only poker tournament in history at the time. The first-place prize was $2 million. It was the biggest win of my career.

The day after I won, I received an email from someone I had never met. He congratulated me on the win and then said something I have never forgotten: "You made a great decision to enter that tournament, and you got lucky."

He was right.

I had made a good decision to enter the tournament. The structure suited my playing style. The field was manageable. I had prepared well. But I also got lucky. I could have played the same way and lost. The cards could have gone against me. An opponent could have gotten lucky on the river. Any number of things could have happened that would have resulted in a different outcome.

The person who emailed me was not being rude. He was reminding me not to fall victim to resulting. He was saying: "You won. Congratulations. But do not let the outcome tell you that you were a genius. You made good decisions and you got lucky. Keep making good decisions, and the luck will average out over time."

I have tried to live by that principle ever since.

### How Poker Players Make Decisions

Poker is a game of incomplete information. You know your own cards. You know the community cards. You know the betting patterns. But you do not know your opponents' cards. You do not know what cards will come next. Every decision is made under uncertainty.

This is the exact same environment as business. In business, you have incomplete information about your competitors, your customers, your market, your technology, your regulatory environment. You do not know what will happen next. Every decision is a bet — an allocation of resources under uncertainty.

Poker players develop habits that business executives would benefit from adopting.

**Poker players think in distributions.** They do not ask "will I win this hand?" They ask "what is my probability of winning this hand, given what I know?" The question is always probabilistic.

**Poker players focus on decisions, not outcomes.** A professional poker player evaluates a hand based on whether the decision was correct given the information available at the time, not on whether the hand was won or lost. The outcome is noise. The decision is signal.

**Poker players do not tilt.** "Tilting" is poker slang for making emotional decisions — chasing losses, playing recklessly, abandoning your strategy because you are angry or frustrated. The best poker players are masters of emotional regulation. They know that emotional decisions are bad decisions.

**Poker players review their hands.** After a session, professional players review hands they played, especially the ones they lost. They ask: "Was that a bad decision that got punished by the outcome, or a good decision that got unlucky?" They know that the only way to improve is to separate decision quality from outcome quality.

### The Decision Journal in Practice

I have kept a decision journal for more than fifteen years. It is the single most important tool in my decision-making toolkit.

Here is an example of a real entry from my journal. I have changed the details to protect confidentiality, but the structure is accurate.

**Date:** March 15
**Decision:** Whether to invest $500,000 in Company X, a software startup
**Frame:** Given my current portfolio allocation and risk tolerance, should I invest in this early-stage company?
**Options:**
- Invest $500,000
- Invest $250,000
- Do not invest
**Probability estimates:**
- 40 percent: Company succeeds (returns 3x or more)
- 35 percent: Company survives but does not achieve breakout success (returns 0.5x to 1.5x)
- 25 percent: Company fails (returns 0)
**Expected value:** 40% * 3x + 35% * 1x + 25% * 0x = 1.2x + 0.35x + 0x = 1.55x
**Reasoning:** Strong team, large addressable market, but high competition. The CEO has a track record of execution. The product has early traction with enterprise customers. Key risk: competitors with deeper pockets.
**Decision:** Invest $250,000 (reduce exposure due to uncertainty about competitive dynamics)
**Prediction:** I expect to know within 18 months whether the company is on a trajectory to succeed or fail.

Two years later, the company was acquired for a modest return — about 1.3x. Not a home run, but not a failure. The outcome was in the middle of my probability distribution.

When I reviewed the journal entry, I could see that my reasoning was sound. I had identified the key risk (competition). I had calibrated my investment size to my uncertainty. The outcome was consistent with my probability estimates. I had made a good decision.

If I had not kept the journal, I might have looked at the 1.3x return and thought: "Was that worth it? Should I have invested more? Less?" The journal gave me a clear answer: my decision was good. The outcome was within expectations.

### The Decision Pod in Practice

My decision pod has been meeting for eight years. There are five of us. We meet monthly, for two hours. The format is simple.

One person presents a decision they are facing. They describe the frame, the options, the probabilities, and their current thinking. The rest of us ask questions, challenge assumptions, and offer alternative perspectives.

The ground rules are critical.

**No judgment.** If the decision goes badly, we do not say "I told you so." We analyze what happened and separate decision quality from outcome quality.

**Honesty.** The pod is only useful if people tell the truth. If you think someone is making a mistake, you say so — directly and respectfully.

**Vulnerability.** You must be willing to show your uncertainty, your doubts, your fears. If you present only your confident conclusions, the pod cannot help you.

**Confidentiality.** What is said in the pod stays in the pod.

The value of the pod is not that the group always knows the right answer. It is that the group asks better questions than you would ask yourself. They see blind spots you miss. They challenge assumptions you have accepted without examination.

I have watched members of my pod make dramatically better decisions because of the feedback they received. I have seen people avoid costly mistakes because someone in the pod asked: "Have you considered what happens if your assumption about X is wrong?" The pod does not guarantee good outcomes. But it dramatically improves decision quality.

One of my pod members — a startup founder — once presented a decision about whether to raise a new round of funding. He was leaning toward raising, based on his belief that the market was about to heat up. The pod asked: "What is your evidence that the market is heating up, and not just three anecdotal data points?" He could not answer. He had been anchoring on a few conversations with investors who were enthusiastic, while ignoring the broader signal — deal volume was actually declining. The pod helped him see that his "market is heating up" belief was not supported by the data. He decided to delay the raise and focus on extending runway. Six months later, the market had indeed tightened. A competitor who had raised at the same time was struggling under the terms they had accepted. The pod had saved him from a costly mistake.

A different pod member — a private equity partner — presented a decision about whether to acquire a company. He was excited about the deal. Everyone on his team was excited. The pod asked one question: "What would have to be true for this to be a bad investment?" That question, the inversion mental model in action, surfaced a set of assumptions that no one on his team had challenged. The company's growth was dependent on a single customer relationship that was not contractually locked in. If that customer left, the entire investment thesis collapsed. The pod did not tell him not to do the deal. It told him to do more due diligence on that customer relationship before committing. He did. The relationship turned out to be weaker than the seller had represented. He renegotiated the price down by 30 percent, reflecting the risk.

### The "Thinking in Bets" Framework for Organizations

The practices I have described can be applied at the organizational level. Here is how.

**Decision journals for teams.** Before a major decision, the team creates a decision journal together. They document the frame, the options, the probabilities, the assumptions. After the outcome is known, they review the journal together. The questions are: Did our assumptions hold? Did our probability estimates match reality? What would we do differently next time?

**Decision pods for executives.** A small group of senior leaders from different organizations (non-competing, to allow candor) meets regularly to review decisions. The format is the same as my personal pod: present a decision, get feedback, learn from the collective wisdom.

**Probability training for teams.** Organizations that make a lot of decisions — especially under uncertainty — benefit from training their people in probabilistic thinking. The goal is not to turn everyone into statisticians. It is to create a culture where people express uncertainty explicitly, where "I am 70 percent confident" is a normal and expected phrase, and where people are not punished for being uncertain.

**Resulting awareness.** The most important cultural change is awareness of resulting. When a decision leads to a bad outcome, the question should not be "who made this bad decision?" It should be "was this a bad decision, or a good decision that got unlucky?" The distinction is not academic. It determines whether the organization learns the right lesson.

I have seen organizations transform their decision-making by adopting these practices. I have also seen organizations resist them — because they require vulnerability, honesty, and a willingness to be wrong. The organizations that adopt them make better decisions over time. The ones that do not keep making the same mistakes, learning the wrong lessons from their outcomes, and wondering why their results do not improve.

### The Expected Value Mindset

At the core of Thinking in Bets is what I call the **expected value mindset**. It is the recognition that every decision is a gamble — not in the casino sense, but in the sense that you are betting resources (time, money, reputation) against an uncertain future. The goal is not to be right on every decision. The goal is to have positive expected value across all your decisions.

This shift in mindset has profound implications. It means you stop evaluating individual decisions by their outcomes. You start evaluating them by their process. You stop asking "did this work?" and start asking "was this a good bet given what I knew at the time?" You stop punishing bad outcomes and start punishing bad processes. You stop rewarding good outcomes and start rewarding good processes.

The expected value mindset also changes how you think about failure. A failure that resulted from a good decision is not a failure — it is the 30 percent that happens when you are 70 percent confident. It is a statistical inevitability, not a personal failing. The only real failure is making bad decisions — failing to gather information, failing to consider alternatives, failing to challenge assumptions, failing to calibrate your confidence.

I have seen this mindset transform leadership teams. When the CEO starts talking about "expected value" instead of "results," the entire organization shifts. People stop hiding bad outcomes. They start sharing them — because they know they will be evaluated on their decision process, not on whether luck went their way. The quality of debate improves. The willingness to take calculated risks increases. The learning from both successes and failures becomes more honest and more useful.

## The One Thing to Remember

> The quality of a decision is not determined by the quality of its outcome. A good decision can lead to a bad outcome. A bad decision can lead to a good outcome. The skill is evaluating decisions based on the process, not the result. Judge yourself by what you can control — your decision-making process — not by what you cannot control — the outcome.

## How to Use This Tomorrow

1. **Start a decision journal.** Today. Take one decision you are currently facing. Write down: the frame, the options, your probability estimates for each option, your reasoning, and your prediction. Put it in a place where you can find it in six months. When the outcome is known, go back and compare your prediction to reality. Do this for every significant decision going forward.

2. **Run a pre-mortem on your current project.** Gather the team. Say: "It is six months from now. Our project has failed. It is a complete disaster. Everyone write down three reasons why it failed." Collect the responses. You will almost certainly surface risks you had not considered. Address them before they become real problems.

3. **Assign a devil's advocate for your next major decision.** Before the meeting, identify someone who will be responsible for arguing against the proposed course of action. Give them permission to be direct. Take their arguments seriously. If their concerns cannot be addressed, reconsider the decision.

4. **Practice expressing uncertainty probabilistically.** For the next week, replace phrases like "I think" and "I'm pretty sure" with explicit probabilities. "I am 70 percent confident this deal will close." "I think there is a 60 percent chance this product launches on time." Pay attention to how it feels to be explicit. Pay attention to how others respond.

5. **Distinguish Type 1 from Type 2 decisions.** Before you start analyzing a decision, ask: "Is this decision reversible?" If yes, it is Type 2. Make it quickly, with less information, and without excessive process. If no, it is Type 1. Take your time. Gather more data. Use a structured framework. Save your analytical energy for the decisions that actually need it.

6. **Form a decision pod.** Identify three to five trusted peers who are also committed to improving their decision-making. Agree to meet regularly (monthly is good). Agree on the ground rules: no judgment, honesty, vulnerability, confidentiality. Start with a decision you are currently facing. The first meeting will be uncomfortable. Push through it. The discomfort is the sign that you are doing something worthwhile.

7. **Redesign your meeting culture around decision quality.** Look at your next team meeting. How much time is spent presenting information versus challenging assumptions? How much time is spent on pre-mortems versus post-mortems? How many people feel safe raising dissenting views? If the answers are not what you want, change the structure. Start every decision discussion with five minutes of silent reading (borrowed from Amazon's 6-page memo). End every decision discussion with a round where each person states their confidence level. Create a culture where "I disagree, and here is why" is the most valued contribution in the room.

8. **Track your calibration.** Keep a simple spreadsheet. Every time you make a probability estimate, record it. When the outcome is known, record that too. At the end of each quarter, check your calibration: did your 70 percent estimates happen about 70 percent of the time? If not, adjust. This is the single most effective practice for reducing overconfidence, and it takes five minutes a week.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1: The Decision Journal Audit**

Think of a significant decision you made in the past year — a job change, an investment, a product launch, a hire. Without looking at any records (you probably did not keep a decision journal, did you?), write down: your frame for the decision, the options you considered, the probabilities you assigned (even implicitly), and your reasoning. Now compare this to what actually happened. How accurate was your memory? Did you remember being more certain than you actually were? Did your reasoning seem clearer in hindsight than it was at the time? The exercise is designed to show you why decision journals are essential — because your memory of your own decisions is distorted by outcome knowledge.

**Exercise 2: The Pre-Mortem**

Take a project or decision you are currently working on. Write a one-page narrative describing, in detail, how it failed. Make it specific. What went wrong? When did it start going wrong? Who made the critical mistake? What external factors contributed? Now, next to each failure point, write down what you could do now to prevent it. The goal is not to predict the exact failure — it is to surface risks that you would otherwise overlook because optimism bias makes you focus on success scenarios.

**Exercise 3: The Calibration Test**

Make ten predictions about outcomes you will know within the next month. They can be anything: "My team will complete the project by the deadline" or "Our competitor will announce a price cut" or "I will get at least seven hours of sleep tonight." For each prediction, assign a probability (from 0 to 100 percent). At the end of the month, check your accuracy. If you said 70 percent, did the event happen about 70 percent of the time? Most people are overconfident: their 70 percent predictions happen much less than 70 percent of the time. The goal of the exercise is to calibrate your confidence — to get your expressed probabilities to match your actual accuracy.

**Exercise 4: The Type 1 / Type 2 Audit**

Review the last ten decisions your organization made. For each one, ask: was it Type 1 (irreversible) or Type 2 (reversible)? How much time and process was invested in each? If your organization spent significant time on Type 2 decisions, you have a process problem. Identify three Type 2 decisions that could have been made faster. Recommend a new process: for Type 2 decisions, maximum one meeting, maximum three people, maximum one week.

**Exercise 5: The Groupthink Diagnostic**

Think of a recent decision made by a team you were part of. Was there genuine debate, or was there an appearance of consensus? Did anyone express significant disagreement? If not, was it because everyone genuinely agreed, or because people were reluctant to speak up? If you suspect groupthink, write down what you could have done — or could do in the future — to create psychological safety for dissent. The diagnostic is not about blaming anyone. It is about recognizing the structural conditions that suppress disagreement.

## Further Reading

- **Thinking in Bets: Making Smarter Decisions When You Don't Have All the Facts** by Annie Duke — My own book, and the most complete treatment of the framework I have described in this chapter. It goes deeper into the psychology of decision-making under uncertainty, the practices of probabilistic thinking, and the practical tools (decision journal, decision pod) that improve decision quality. If you read one book from this list, start with this one.

- **The Power of Hindsight** — Actually, skip that one. I am joking. The point is that hindsight bias is so powerful that we have to actively work to overcome it. The book you should actually read is **Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions** by Gary Klein — the origin of the pre-mortem concept. Klein's research on how expert decision-makers actually think (as opposed to how textbooks say they should think) is the foundation for much of the applied work in naturalistic decision-making.

- **Good Strategy Bad Strategy** by Richard Rumelt — I recommended this in Chapter 1, and I recommend it again here. Rumelt's framework (diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions) is a decision-making framework as much as a strategy framework. His treatment of bad strategy — fluff, failure to face the problem, mistaking goals for strategy — is a masterclass in identifying poor decision-making.

- **The Challenger Launch Decision** by Diane Vaughan — The definitive sociological study of the Challenger disaster. Vaughan's concept of the "normalization of deviance" is one of the most important contributions to organizational decision-making theory. The book is academic but accessible, and it will change how you think about how organizations drift toward disaster.

- **Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion** by Robert Cialdini — Not directly about decision-making, but about the cognitive shortcuts and biases that influence decisions. Cialdini's six principles of influence (reciprocity, scarcity, authority, consistency, liking, social proof) are essential knowledge for anyone who wants to understand why people make the decisions they do — including why they say "yes" when they should say "no."
